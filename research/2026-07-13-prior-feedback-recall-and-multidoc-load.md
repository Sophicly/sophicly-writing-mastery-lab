# Prior-Feedback Recall (Ask-Then-Reveal) & Multi-Document Cognitive Load — Research Findings

**Date:** 2026-07-13
**Method:** 8 search angles (WebSearch) + 6 primary sources fetched and indexed; claims
verified against source text (abstracts, results, conclusions) not summaries. Effect sizes
quoted only where the source states them; single-study directional findings flagged as such.
**Purpose:** settle two redraft-planning design questions —
(Q1) should Sophia ASK the student to recall what cost them marks last time BEFORE revealing the
stored grade + priority target, and how to frame/persist that recall;
(Q2) how many prior-assessment reference pads a student may open side-by-side.
**Builds on:** `2026-07-12-prediction-before-reading-pedagogy.md` (F1 generation effect, F4
revisit-is-the-feedback, F6 never-score-accuracy). Q1 is the *feedback* twin of that file's
*prediction* case: recall-before-reveal is pretesting applied to the student's own prior result.

## VERIFIED FINDINGS

### F1 — Generating a response BEFORE seeing the answer beats being told directly; the wrong attempt is bound to the correction (HIGH)
The pretesting / errorful-generation literature is unambiguous: producing an answer (even a wrong
one) and THEN meeting corrective feedback yields better retention of the correct answer than
studying the correct answer from the outset. Mechanism: the corrective feedback is *co-encoded*
with the prior erroneous response, which deepens encoding and heightens attention to the feedback
when it arrives. This is the same generation/pretesting effect established in the prediction file
(F1: generation d=.40; pretesting d up to 1.1), now applied to a student's own prior result.
**Design implication:** ask-then-reveal is the correct shape. Asking the student to recall what
cost them marks, THEN revealing the stored target, primes attention to that target more than
showing it cold. Tell-directly leaves the priming value on the table.
- Pretesting effect + corrective feedback: link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-025-01813-x
  (Memory & Cognition 2025); journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.455 (2025)
- Errorful generation binds feedback: link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-011-0167-z
  (Metcalfe & Kornell / Huelser, M&C 2011); pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8440464/

### F2 — ⭐ THE LOAD-BEARING CAVEAT: feedback effectiveness FALLS as attention moves from the TASK up to the SELF (HIGH — Kluger & DeNisi FIT)
Feedback Intervention Theory, from the canonical meta-analysis (607 effect sizes, 23,663
observations): feedback improved performance on average d=.41, **but over one-third of feedback
interventions DECREASED performance.** The organising finding: *"feedback intervention
effectiveness decreases as attention moves up the hierarchy closer to the self and away from the
task."* Task-focused feedback helps; feedback that directs attention to the self (self-worth,
"am I good at this") reliably undercuts performance.
**Design implication:** this is the guard-rail on the whole feature. Asking a 13–16-year-old to
recall "what am I weak at" is a self-level prompt — exactly the third of cases where feedback
backfires. The recall ask MUST be pinned to the TASK ("what did the mark scheme want more of on
Q3?"), never to the self ("what are your weaknesses?"). Ask-then-reveal is supported ONLY under
task-level framing. (Confidence HIGH; the effect-size heterogeneity — the losing third — is the
robust part.)
- Kluger, A.N. & DeNisi, A. (1996), *Psychological Bulletin* 119(2), 254–284 —
  mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf

### F3 — The affective risk lives in the REVEAL of a DISCREPANCY, not in the act of recalling (HIGH — directly on-domain: essay self-assessment)
112 students wrote an essay, self-assessed, then received the tutor grade — creating a gap between
predicted and actual. Students who had **over-estimated** their work suffered a decline in positive
emotion, a rise in negative emotion, and a self-efficacy drop of ~2/5 of a standard deviation
(β = −.42 self-efficacy, β = −.53 positive, β = +.39 negative) on discovering the gap.
Under-estimators felt *better*. Notably, self-efficacy shifted strongly off a discrepancy that was
small on average. Domain is essay writing (close to Sophicly), though sample is higher-ed, Spain.
**Design implication:** the danger point is not "recall your target" — it is the moment the reveal
CONTRADICTS the student's self-image (they thought they did fine; the grade says otherwise). Two
mitigations fall straight out: (a) frame the recall so the student is retrieving a *known action
target*, not betting on a grade they'll be shown to have mis-judged — do NOT turn the recall into a
"guess your grade" game (compounds the overestimation dent, and the prediction file's F6 already
bans scoring prediction accuracy); (b) lead the reveal with progress/what-improved framing
(ipsative, F4) so a downward surprise lands inside a growth frame, not as a verdict.
- Panadero et al. (2026), *European Journal of Psychology of Education* —
  link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10212-026-01087-0

### F4 — Self-referential (ipsative) framing motivates engagement with developmental feedback (MEDIUM–HIGH)
Comparing a learner's current work to their OWN previous performance (not to a cohort or a fixed
bar) fosters autonomy, competence and relatedness (self-determination theory), increases
willingness to act on developmental feedback, and supports an incremental ("growth") view of
ability. Evidence base is qualitative/evaluative (distance-learner case studies), so MEDIUM–HIGH,
not meta-analytic. Documented downside: ipsative framing "can raise grade expectations."
**Design implication:** the redraft frame is inherently ipsative (this attempt vs last attempt) —
lean into it. Present the prior target as "here's the one thing to move forward from last time,"
not "here's where you fell short." This is the delivery wrapper that keeps F2's task-focus and
softens F3's discrepancy risk.
- Hughes, Okoro et al. (2014), *Open Learning* 29(1) —
  discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1541050/1/Use%20of%20self%20referential%20feedback%20final%20draft.pdf

### F5 — Writing the recalled reflection beats keeping it conversational (MEDIUM–HIGH — self-explanation / writing-to-learn)
Prompted self-explanation — having the learner generate, in their own words, why/how something
matters — improves comprehension and transfer with a mean effect g ≈ .55 across 64 studies
(~6,000 participants); generating one's own explanation beats being given the explanation.
Committing the recall to a written document section is a generative, self-explanatory act (and
inherits the generation effect from the prediction file, F1). It also creates a durable artifact
the student can later set beside the revealed target — turning the reveal into F1's co-encoded
correction rather than a passing line of chat.
**Design implication:** WRITE the recalled reflection into a short document section, not chat-only.
Keep it to one or two lines (a target, not an essay) so it stays a task-level prompt (F2) and does
not become a rumination surface. Conversational-only recall forfeits the writing-to-learn gain and
leaves nothing to co-encode the reveal against.
- Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018), *Educational Psychology Review* 30, 703–725 —
  link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x
  (digest: bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique...)

### F6 — Working-memory capacity is ~3–5 chunks in adults and LOWER before adulthood (HIGH — Cowan)
Cowan's central-capacity estimate is ~4 chunks (range 3–5) in young adults when grouping/rehearsal
are controlled. Critically for a 13–16 audience: the same paradigm gives ~1.5 stored items at age 7
rising toward the adult ~3 through childhood — capacity is still maturing across the teen years and
should be assumed at or below the adult lower bound, not at it.
**Design implication:** treat each open document (the active planning doc + each reference pad) as a
concurrent source competing for the same 3–5-chunk store. For this age band, budget conservatively
toward the low end. This is the hard ceiling behind Q2's cap.
- Cowan (2010), *The Magical Mystery Four*, PMC2864034 (restating Cowan 2001, *BBS* 24) —
  pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/

### F7 — Splitting attention across sources that must be INTEGRATED imposes extraneous load — but easy ACCESS to a source reduces it (HIGH, with a design-critical nuance)
Cognitive Load Theory's split-attention effect: when a task forces the learner to hold and mentally
integrate multiple separate sources, extraneous (non-learning) load rises and learning suffers
(Chandler & Sweller 1992). BUT a direct on-point study cuts the other way on *availability*: 125
**secondary-school** students wrote a stance essay from four texts; those ALLOWED to return to the
texts during writing showed *lower* extraneous load than those who had to work from memory — the
split-attention penalty fell on the group forced to hold sources in the head. (Task performance
didn't differ significantly — availability lowered load without changing the score.)
**Design implication:** the reference-pad viewer is on the right side of this — being able to glance
back at prior marked work is LESS loading than being asked to recall it from memory. The load risk
is not "documents are open," it is "too many sources demanding simultaneous integration." So the
design answer is a *cap*, not a *ban*: keep a small number open, each serving the current step, and
never require the student to integrate across all of them at once.
- Chandler & Sweller (1992), *Br. J. Educ. Psychol.* 62 —
  bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.2044-8279.1992.tb01017.x
- Andresen, Anmarkrud & Bråten (2018), *Frontiers in Education* 3:59 —
  frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2018.00059/full

## REFUTED / UNSUPPORTED (design assumptions that the evidence does NOT back)

- **"Recalling your own weaknesses first is inherently demotivating for anxious teens"** —
  NOT supported as stated. The evidence (F2, F3) locates the harm in SELF-LEVEL framing and in the
  REVEAL of an unexpected discrepancy, not in the recall act itself. Task-level recall of an action
  target is a benign, even beneficial, retrieval-practice move. So the design is safe IF framed per
  F2/F3 — the blanket "don't ask them to recall weaknesses" over-reads the risk.
- **A hard "≈2, maybe 3" cap justified by pure working-memory limits alone** — partially
  over-stated. F7 shows document *availability* reduces load rather than adding it, so the cap is
  about simultaneous INTEGRATION demand, not raw document count. The cap is right; the rationale is
  "limit sources integrated at once," not "each open pad consumes a memory slot outright."
- **"Guess your grade" as the recall prompt** — actively contra-indicated (F3 + prediction-file
  F6): inviting a grade prediction manufactures the overestimation-discrepancy that dents
  self-efficacy, and scoring prediction accuracy is already banned for this age group.

## CAVEATS (honest limits)
- **Age gap.** Kluger & DeNisi (F2), the miscalibration study (F3), ipsative (F4) and
  self-explanation (F5) are predominantly adult/higher-ed samples; Cowan (F6) and the multi-doc
  study (F7) DO reach children/secondary. Directional for the 13–16 band, mechanism-consistent.
- **Construct gap on F1↔Q1.** The pretesting/errorful-generation base uses test items with single
  correct answers, not "recall your own prior feedback." Extrapolation is mechanism-sound but not
  directly measured (same caveat as the prediction file).
- **No source gives an exact optimal pad count.** The cap below is a reasoned synthesis of Cowan's
  lower bound (F6) minus the active doc, not a directly measured optimum.

## DESIGN ANSWERS

**Q1 — ask-then-reveal vs tell-directly: SUPPORTED, conditionally.** Ask the student to recall
first, then reveal (F1: the recall attempt primes and co-encodes the revealed target; beats
showing it cold). The single non-negotiable condition is F2 (Kluger & DeNisi): keep the ask at the
TASK level. Ship ask-then-reveal.

**Q1a — how to sequence the reveal:** recall (task-level) → reveal wrapped in ipsative/progress
framing (F4) → treat the revealed target as the "correction" the recall is co-encoded against (F1).
Do NOT let the recall become a grade guess (F3, and prediction-file F6 ban on scoring accuracy).

**Q1b — the framing rule (load-bearing):** ask about the WORK, never the SELF. Good:
*"Last time, what did the mark scheme want more of in your Q3 answer?"* / *"What's the one thing you'd
do differently on this question?"* Bad: *"What are you weakest at?"* / *"Where did you fall short?"* /
*"What grade do you think you got?"* Rule of thumb: the prompt must be answerable by naming an
action on the task, not by judging the self. (Kluger & DeNisi 1996 is the citation to cite.)

**Q1c — write it into a document section, not chat-only: SUPPORTED (F5).** Persist the recalled
reflection as a short (1–2 line) written target in a doc section — this captures the writing-to-learn
/ self-explanation gain (g≈.55) and gives the reveal something concrete to co-encode against. Keep it
brief and action-shaped so it stays task-level (F2) and doesn't become a rumination box.

**Q2 — pad cap: cap at 2 reference pads open simultaneously (soft ceiling 3), and Neil's instinct is
confirmed.** Rationale: working-memory capacity is ~3–5 chunks in adults and lower/still-maturing at
13–16 (F6); the active planning doc already occupies one concurrent-source slot, leaving ~2 for
reference pads before you reach the low end of the range. The viewer itself is *helpful* — glancing
back beats recalling from memory (F7) — so the correct control is a CAP with soft guidance, not a
ban: allow 2 by default, permit a 3rd, and gently discourage more (e.g. auto-suggest closing the
oldest) rather than hard-blocking. Never design a step that requires integrating across all open
pads at once — that is the actual split-attention trap (F7), independent of the count.
