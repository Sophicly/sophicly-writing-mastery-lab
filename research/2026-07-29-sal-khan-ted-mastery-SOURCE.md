**Status: ARGUMENT / FRAMING SOURCE, not evidence.** Supplied by Neil, 29 Jul 2026.

Use it for rhetoric and structure. Do NOT cite it as research — where it makes empirical claims,

## Why this is worth keeping
Khan's **home-building analogy is the single best explanatory device we have** for the whole
argument, and it is better than anything in the academic literature for a parent audience:

> "We were told we have two weeks to build a foundation. Do what you can." … the inspector comes,
> "the concrete is still wet right over there … I'll give it an 80 percent." "Great! That's a C.
> Let's build the first floor." … all of a sudden, while you're building the third floor, the whole
> structure collapses.
> … "if your reaction is the reaction you typically have in education … you might say, maybe we had
> a bad contractor, or maybe we needed better inspection. **But what was really broken was the
> process.** We were artificially constraining how long we had to something, pretty much ensuring a
> variable outcome, and we took the trouble of inspecting and identifying those gaps, **but then we
> built right on top of it**."

That last clause — *we identified the gaps and then built on top of them* — is the thesis of our
mastery article in one line, and it is Neil's argument exactly.

## The formulation of the inversion (quotable, and it matches Carroll/Bloom precisely)
> "Instead of artificially constraining, fixing when and how long you work on something, pretty much
> ensuring that variable outcome, the A, B, C, D, F — do it the other way around. **What's variable
> is when and how long a student actually has to work on something, and what's fixed is that they
> actually master the material.**"

## The gap-accumulation mechanism (why a student concludes they "can't do it")
> "I didn't know 25 percent of the material. Even the A student, what was the five percent they
> didn't know? … the whole class will then move on to the next subject, probably a more advanced
> subject that's going to build on those gaps." … "It's not because algebra is fundamentally
> difficult or because the student isn't bright… that 30 percent that I didn't know is showing up.
> And then I start to disengage."

**Direct English-department translation for our article:** the student who "can't analyse language"
is usually a student who never mastered inference, and is now being asked to evaluate. Same
mechanism, our subject.

## The Winnetka reference — CHECKED, it is real
> "there were experiments in Winnetka, Illinois, 100 years ago, where they did mastery-based
> learning and saw great results, but they said it wouldn't scale because it was logistically
> difficult."

Verified as a real historical programme: Carleton W. Washburne ran individualised instruction in the
Winnetka public schools, 1919–1943 (OpenAlex record: "Carleton W. Washburne: His Administrative and
Curricular Contributions in the Winnetka Public Schools, 1919 Through 1943", 1988). **Khan's
"logistically difficult, therefore abandoned" framing is the historically standard account** — and
it is the key to why the 1980s critiques may not bind today (see the research doc §"What changed").

## The literacy analogy (strong, but it is rhetoric)
> "400 years into the past … about 15 percent of the population knew how to read… if you fast
> forward to today … pretty close to 100 percent of the population is capable of reading."
> … "What percentage of the population do you think is capable of truly mastering calculus…? A lot
> of you might say… maybe 20, 30 percent. But what if that estimate is just based on your own
> experience in a **non-mastery framework**?"

⚠ Powerful and usable, but it is an *argument by analogy*, not a finding. Frame it as Khan's
argument, attributed — never as "research shows".

## The mindset half — HANDLE WITH CARE
Khan gives mastery and mindset equal billing ("the two ideas… the key leverage points"). The
evidence for the two halves is **not** equally strong: see the research doc's REFUTED/CAVEATS on
growth mindset (Sisk et al. 2018 — overall effects weak; Yeager et al. 2019 — real but small, and
concentrated in lower-achieving students in supportive school contexts). Our article should lead
with mastery and treat mindset as a *consequence* of mastery experiences, not as a co-equal claim.
