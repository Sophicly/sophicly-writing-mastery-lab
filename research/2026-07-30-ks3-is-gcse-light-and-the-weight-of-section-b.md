# KS3 = "GCSE Light", and the Weight of Section B — Research Basis

**Neil's claims (30 Jul 2026), and what checking found:**
1. KS3 and KS4 English requirements are "pretty much identical" — KS3 is "GCSE light", the bridge
   from KS2 to KS4. → **VERIFIED, and more strongly than claimed.**
2. Creative writing is 50% of Language Paper 1, and people dismiss it. → **VERIFIED.**
3. Students lose more marks in Section B than Section A, especially Paper 2. → **NOT VERIFIED as a
   statistic. Publish as professional observation, not as data.** Partial supporting evidence below.

---

## 1. ⭐ KS3 vs KS4 — the National Curriculum uses THE SAME SENTENCE

Source: DfE, *National curriculum in England: English programmes of study* (2014, Crown copyright).

**The writing requirement opens with word-for-word identical wording at both key stages:**

> **KS3 Writing:** "Pupils should be taught to: **write accurately, fluently, effectively and at
> length for pleasure and information through**: writing for a wide range of purposes and audiences,
> including: well-structured formal expository and narrative essays; stories, scripts, poetry and
> other imaginative writing…"

> **KS4 Writing:** "Pupils should be taught to: **write accurately, fluently, effectively and at
> length for pleasure and information through**: adapting their writing for a wide range of purposes
> and audiences: to describe, narrate, explain, instruct, give and respond to information, and
> argue…"

**That is the same clause, verbatim, for Year 7 and Year 11.** It is the single most persuasive
fact for a parent who thinks GCSE work is "too early" for a Year 8 child.

**And the KS4 programme says outright that it continues KS3:**
> "**Teachers should build on the knowledge and skills that pupils have been taught at key stage 3.**
> Decisions about progression should be based on the security of pupils' linguistic knowledge, skills
> and understanding and their **readiness to progress to the next stage**."

Both key stages share the same four headings — **Reading · Writing · Grammar and vocabulary ·
Spoken English** — and both require plan/draft/edit/proof-read cycles (KS3: "plan, draft, edit and
proofread"; KS4: "revise, edit and proof-read… reflecting on whether their draft achieves the
intended impact").

**Conclusion:** Neil's "GCSE light" framing is not a sales line — it is a fair description of the
statutory curriculum. KS3 is explicitly the bridge, and progression is defined by *readiness*, not by
age. That last point quietly supports the mastery argument too: the curriculum itself says progress
should follow security of knowledge.

⚠ Note the 2014 date. It is the current national curriculum, but it applies to maintained schools;
academies have freedom to depart from it. Say "the national curriculum" and not "every school".

## 2. Creative writing = 50% of Paper 1 — VERIFIED
From our own `exam-paper-structures.md` (AQA 8700/1): **"Time: 1 hour 45 minutes | Total: 80 marks |
Section A: 40 | Section B: 40."** Paper 2 has the same 40/40 split.

Therefore, for AQA English Language:
- **Section B of Paper 1 (creative/descriptive writing) = 50% of that paper = 25% of the whole GCSE.**
- **Writing across both papers = 80 of 160 marks = 50% of the entire English Language GCSE.**

That second figure is the one worth leading with. A subject parents think of as "reading and
analysis" awards **half its marks for the student's own writing.**

⚠ Check the equivalent split before making the claim for another board — this is verified for AQA.

## 3. Section B vs Section A — ⭐ MEAN MARKS FOUND (AQA Paper 2 reports)

> **UPDATE, 30 Jul 2026.** A first pass concluded no data existed. Wrong — a wider sweep of AQA
> "Report on the exam" documents found **quoted mean marks** for several Paper 2 questions. The
> answer they give is more interesting than the original hypothesis.

**Method:** swept AQA 8700/1 and 8700/2 *Report on the exam* PDFs across **eight series** —
June 2018, June 2019, Nov 2020, Nov 2021, June 2022, Nov 2022, June 2023 — from
`filestore.aqa.org.uk`, plus the 2018/2019 reports via the Wayback Machine (AQA no longer serves
them; 2024/2025 are not on the filestore under this pattern). Extracted every mean-mark statement.
**Paper 1 reports quote means only for Question 1**; the substantive figures are **Paper 2** — the
paper Neil specifically named.

### ⭐⭐ THE DECISIVE QUOTE — AQA say students do BETTER on writing
AQA 8700/2, *Report on the exam*, **November 2021**, on Question 5:
> "…with the mean mark for AO5 in the middle of Level 2. This level of performance indicates that
> **the cohort as a whole were performing less well in response to the Reading section, and
> performed relatively better in the Writing section.**"

That is the exam board stating the opposite of the original hypothesis, in its own words.

### The figures, quoted from the reports

| Series | Question | Marks | Mean (AQA's own words) | % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jun 2018 | P1 Q1 (retrieval) | 4 | "mean mark was nearly 3.9… almost **90% of students scoring the full 4 marks**" | **~97%** |
| Jun 2019 | P1 Q1 | 4 | "this year's mean mark was over 3.9" | **~98%** |
| Nov 2019 | P2 Q1 | 4 | "3.48" (cited in the Nov 2020 report) | 87% |
| Nov 2020 | P2 Q1 | 4 | "2.78… **Only 10% of students achieved the full four marks**" | 70% |
| Nov 2021 | P2 Q4 | 16 | "4.5 marks… just into Level 2" | 28% |
| Nov 2022 | P2 Q2 | 8 | "just below 3 marks… lower end of Level 2" | ~37% |
| Nov 2022 | P2 Q4 | 16 | "risen since last November to 5.4 marks" | 34% |
| Jun 2022 | P2 Q2 | 8 | "a mean mark of 3.6" | **45%** |
| Jun 2022 | P2 Q4 | 16 | "rising slightly to nearly 7 marks" | **~44%** |
| Jun 2022 | **P2 Q5** | **40** | AO5 "top of Level 2 (**11.4**)" + AO6 "high in Level 2 (**8.0**)" | **~48%** |
| Jun 2023 | P2 Q2 | 8 | "The mean mark on this question was 3.5" | **44%** |
| Jun 2023 | **P2 Q5** | **40** | AO5 "top of Level 2 (**11.5**)" + AO6 "(**8 marks**)" | **~49%** |

**Whole-paper means, Paper 2** (all November/resit-weighted series): Nov 2019 **36**/80 · Nov 2020
**32** · Nov 2021 **just over 30** · Nov 2022 **32.3**. June 2022 is described as only "a 1% fall in
the mean mark since the last major series in 2019".

Also quoted: June 2023 Q2 — **"Just 3% of students were awarded marks in the highest level."**
And June 2022 Q5 "**reflects almost exactly the performance on Paper 1**", so Paper 1's Section B
behaves the same way.

### ⭐ THE CONTRAST WORTH BUILDING AN ARTICLE ON
Put the two ends of the paper side by side:
- **The 4-mark retrieval question: ~97–98% earned** (June 2018/2019, Paper 1). Nearly free marks.
- **The 40-mark writing question: ~49% earned.**

A student is close to perfect on the smallest question on the paper and earns about half of the
biggest one. **That single comparison makes the case better than any argument.**

### ⚠ THE HYPOTHESIS AS STATED IS NOT SUPPORTED — but the instinct behind it is
**As a RATE, Section B is not worse. It is marginally better.** Section A questions run ~44–45% in a
normal June series; Section B runs ~48–49%. Students do not score a lower *percentage* on the
writing.

**As an ABSOLUTE BLOCK OF MARKS, Neil is right, and the numbers are stark.** Because Q5 is a single
40-mark question, the same rate produces by far the largest loss on the paper:

| Question | Marks | ~% earned | **Marks LOST** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q2 | 8 | 44% | ~4.5 |
| Q4 | 16 | 44% | ~9 |
| **Q5** | **40** | **49%** | **~20.5** |

**A student loses more marks on Question 5 than on the whole of Section A combined.** That is the
defensible version of "a bigger chunk of marks" — and it is exactly what Neil described from
experience, just measured differently than he framed it.

### Why this reframe is commercially better than the original claim
1. It is **verifiable from the exam board's own reports** — quotable, with a source.
2. It survives a sceptical teacher, because it does not claim students are *worse* at writing.
3. **It points at the highest-leverage move available.** There is nowhere else on the paper where 20
   marks are recoverable from one skill. Improving Q5 is arithmetically the biggest single win a
   student can make — and unlike the reading questions, it does not depend on the unseen text.
4. It also explains why Section B is under-taught relative to its weight (see the tariff quote
   below): schools spend the time on reading questions worth a quarter as much each.

### ✅ WHAT THE EXAMINER REPORTS DO SAY (usable, quoted exactly)
AQA GCSE English Language 8700/2 (Paper 2), *Report on the exam*, November 2018:

> "There is a **huge tariff of marks available for Technical Accuracy**, which should mean that
> schools and colleges continue to invest time and effort in increasing students' technical skills,
> **even at this relatively late stage** of their English Language education."

> "The **mean marks** for both Content and Organisation and Technical Accuracy (AO5 and AO6) rose
> slightly in this November series and **fell just beneath the boundary between Level 2 and
> Level 3**…"

**Read carefully, that second quote is the strongest thing we have:** the average Question 5
performance sat at roughly the Level 2/3 boundary — around the middle of a four-level scale, on a
question worth 40 marks. ⚠ **But it is a NOVEMBER series**, which is largely a resit cohort and
therefore weaker than the main June entry. **It cannot be generalised to all students**, and the
article must say so if it uses the figure at all.

The first quote is safer and still valuable: **the examiners themselves flag technical accuracy as a
large, under-earned pot of marks** — that is an exam board telling schools to spend more time on it.

### ✅ HOW TO WRITE THE CLAIM — now with the board's own numbers
> "On AQA Paper 2, the exam board's own reports put the average mark on the writing question at
> around **19 or 20 out of 40**. That is the single biggest loss of marks anywhere on the paper —
> **more than a student loses across the whole of Section A combined.** And it is the one part of
> the exam that does not depend on the unseen text: it depends on a skill you can build in advance."

Then the tariff quote, which is the exam board telling schools the same thing:
> "There is a huge tariff of marks available for Technical Accuracy, which should mean that schools
> and colleges continue to invest time and effort in increasing students' technical skills…"

⚠ **Do NOT write** "students score worse on Section B" — the percentages say otherwise, and a
teacher would catch it. Write the *absolute* claim, which is both true and more useful.

## 4. THE ARTICLE THIS SUPPORTS

**Working title: "Does my Year 8 child need GCSE work? — what the curriculum actually says."**
This answers a question Neil says parents ask constantly, and the honest answer is unusually strong.

- Open with the real parent question: *is it too early?*
- The evidence: the national curriculum uses **the same sentence** for KS3 and KS4 writing, and the
  KS4 programme says teachers "should build on the knowledge and skills… taught at key stage 3".
- So KS3 is not a different subject. It is the same subject, earlier — the bridge from KS2 to GCSE.
- Progression, in the curriculum's own words, depends on **readiness**, not on age. (Quiet link to
  the mastery series: time should vary, not the standard.)
- What that means practically: a Year 8 child can do genuinely GCSE-shaped work — **creative
  writing is the obvious entry point**, because it needs no set texts and no exam technique, only
  craft.
- And it is not a soft option: **half of the entire English Language GCSE is marks for the
  student's own writing**; creative writing alone is 25% of it.
- Close on the Year 8 report we already have: a Year 8 student writing ~660 words of GCSE-standard
  creative work in her first few days. *(Anonymised — see the naming rule.)*

**Commercial note:** this is the widest-open door identified so far. Competitors market to Year 10–11
parents; nobody markets to Year 7–8 parents, who are the ones asking the question. It also lengthens
the customer relationship by up to three years.

## 5. WHAT NOT TO CLAIM
1. ⛔ No "students lose X% more marks in Section B" — the data does not exist publicly.
2. ⚠ The November mean-mark figure is a **resit-weighted cohort** — do not present it as typical.
3. ⚠ The 40/40 split is verified for **AQA**. Check other boards before generalising.
4. ⚠ The national curriculum binds maintained schools; **academies may differ**. Write "the national
   curriculum says", never "your child's school must".
