# The Habits of Mastery — Why Students "Get Through" Instead of Getting Good

**Neil's observation (29 Jul 2026), and the question:** students form a habit of *completing* rather
than *mastering*. His example: a student was given a series of scripted questions designed to build
a structured response. He skipped the questions and wrote the answer straight out. The work **looked
finished**. But the scripted questions were empty, and the answer had the wrong structure. Neil's
hypothesis: school trains "finish it and don't get into trouble", because time is short — and very
few students ever form a habit of aiming at expert performance.

**Verdict: this is a well-documented phenomenon with a research literature behind it, and Neil's
causal explanation — that the habit is learned from what gets asked of students — is the single
best-evidenced part of it.** It is strong enough to carry its own article.

## VERIFIED FINDINGS

### 1. ⭐ THE CORE FINDING — students adapt their learning to what they think is being asked
- Marton, F. & Säljö, R. (1976), *On Qualitative Differences in Learning — II: Outcome as a Function
  of the Learner's Conception of the Task*, **British Journal of Educational Psychology**.
  doi:10.1111/j.2044-8279.1976.tb02304.x — 832 citations.
- Publisher abstract, verbatim: two groups read the same textbook sections; one group got questions
  "which demanded a thorough understanding of the meaning", the other "detailed factual questions".
  "Besides providing further evidence of qualitative differences in learning, the experiment showed
  that **students did adapt their way of learning to their conception of what was required of
  them.**"
- **This is Neil's argument, demonstrated experimentally in 1976.** The student is not lazy. He has
  correctly worked out what the system rewards — a finished-looking product — and optimised for it.

### 2. Surface processing vs deep processing — the original distinction
- Marton, F. & Säljö, R. (1976), *On Qualitative Differences in Learning: I — Outcome and Process*,
  **British Journal of Educational Psychology**. doi:10.1111/j.2044-8279.1976.tb02980.x —
  **2,755 citations.**
- Verbatim: differences in outcome correspond to "whether the learner is engaged in **surface-level
  or deep-level processing**". The paper's framing is that learning must be described by *what* is
  learned, not how much.
- Useful for the article because it names the thing precisely: skipping the scaffold is not a
  shortcut through the same road, it is a **different kind of processing** that produces a different
  kind of result.

### 3. ⭐ "Gaming the system" — the exact behaviour, studied directly
- Baker, R. S., Corbett, A. T., Koedinger, K. R. & Wagner, A. Z. (2004), *Off-Task Behavior in the
  Cognitive Tutor Classroom*, **Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing
  Systems (CHI '04)**. doi:10.1145/985692.985741 — 251 citations. Follow-up: *Detecting When Students
  Game the System, Across Tutor Subjects and Classroom Cohorts* (2005), doi:10.1007/11527886_28.
- This is the literature on students who exploit a learning system to produce correct-looking
  progress without learning — clicking through hints, guessing systematically. Neil's student did the
  analogue version by hand: skip the scaffold, submit the artefact.
- ⚠ **Read the papers before quoting effect claims.** Crossref holds no abstract for the 2004 CHI
  paper, so the specific findings here are named but NOT quoted. If the article wants a number
  (e.g. how much less gamers learn), pull it from the paper first.

### 4. Help-seeking research — the same pattern in a different guise
- Aleven, V. et al., *Help Helps, But Only So Much: Research on Help Seeking with Intelligent
  Tutoring Systems* (2016), doi:10.1007/s40593-015-0089-1 — 243 citations.
- Relevant because the failure mode is the same: help exists, the student uses it as a way to get to
  the answer rather than a way to understand. Ties directly to WML's own help-ladder design
  (see WML CLAUDE.md §4c.9).

## ⭐ THE DOUG LEMOV QUESTION — RESOLVED FROM PRIMARY SOURCES (29 Jul 2026)
Neil recalled Lemov setting "a 100% standard as the only acceptable level of effort", and thought it
was in *Practice Perfect*. **Checked against Neil's own copies of both books.** Three separate
things were being blended, and one of them contradicts the claim outright.

### ✅ USE THIS — "Right is Right", *Teach Like a Champion* Technique 2
Verbatim from the text: **"Set and defend a high standard of correctness in your classroom… Do not
accept partially or almost right answers; hold out for all the way… Believes they are capable of
getting answers as right as students anywhere else."**
This IS the mastery standard Sophicly teaches: an answer that is nearly right is not right. It is
the correct citation for the point Neil wants to make.

### ⚠ NOT THIS — "100%", *Teach Like a Champion* Technique 36
"There is one acceptable percentage of students **following a direction** given in your classroom:
100%." (Confirmed by Neil's own funnel image `100% Doug Lemov.png`.) This is about **behavioural
compliance**, not work quality. Citing it as a standard of effort or attainment misreads it.

### ⛔ AND *PRACTICE PERFECT* SAYS THE OPPOSITE — this is the important one
Verbatim from Neil's copy of *Practice Perfect*: **"While we want participants to experience
primarily success during practice, the ideal success rate still isn't 100 percent — if that's the
case, then the activity isn't hard enough."**
**Do not attribute a "100% standard" to Practice Perfect.** The book explicitly rejects it as a
target for practice design. Printing that claim would be refutable from the book itself.

### The reconciliation — and it is genuinely useful pedagogy
The two ideas are not in conflict; they apply at different moments:
- **When JUDGING an answer:** hold the full standard. Nearly right is not right. (*Right is Right*)
- **When DESIGNING practice:** it should be hard enough that the student does NOT succeed every
  time. A 100% success rate means the practice is too easy. (*Practice Perfect*, and this converges
  exactly with Bjork's **desirable difficulties** — see the mastery research doc.)

**Article-ready line for a parent:** *if your child gets everything right in practice, the practice
is too easy — but when the work is finally marked, "nearly right" should never be called right.*

## WHAT IS NOT ESTABLISHED (be careful)
1. **How common this is.** Neil says himself it may be a minority. No prevalence figure was found,
   and none should be invented. Write it as a pattern we see, not as "most students".
2. **That schools cause it.** The Marton & Säljö finding shows students adapt to the *task demands
   they perceive*. That supports "the habit is learned from what gets rewarded". It does NOT license
   blaming teachers, and the article must not read that way — same rule as the mastery piece:
   the system has a job to do.
3. **Achievement-goal theory** (mastery goals vs performance goals) is the obvious neighbouring
   framework, but its measurement literature is genuinely contested — see Hulleman, Schrager,
   Bodmann & Harackiewicz (2010), *A meta-analytic review of achievement goal measures: Different
   labels for the same constructs or different constructs with similar labels?*, **Psychological
   Bulletin**, doi:10.1037/a0018947 (877 citations). **Use the framework as vocabulary if helpful,
   but do not quote effect sizes from it.**

## THE ARTICLE THIS SUPPORTS
**Working title: "The habit that stops good students getting great grades."**
- Open with Neil's anecdote — it is concrete, recognisable to any parent, and it is the whole thesis
  in one story: *the work looked finished; the questions underneath were empty.*
- Name the habit: finishing vs mastering. Give the parent a way to spot it at home — ask to see the
  planning, not the final piece.
- The research: students adapt to what they believe is required (Marton & Säljö). So a system that
  rewards completion produces students who complete.
- The standard we hold instead: Lemov's "Right is Right" — no rounding up an almost-correct answer.
- What mastery habits look like in practice, and why the scaffold IS the lesson, not an obstacle
  before the lesson.
- ⚠ Tone: never contemptuous about the student. He behaved rationally. The system taught him.
