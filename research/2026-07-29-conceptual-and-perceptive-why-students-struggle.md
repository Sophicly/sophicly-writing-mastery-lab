# "Conceptual" and "Perceptive" — Why Students Struggle With the Top Band

**Neil's point (29 Jul 2026):** the top band does not just want a well-structured argument. It wants
a response that is **conceptualised** (writing about *ideas*, convincingly) and **perceptive**
(seeing through the text to deeper, hidden meaning). These are two further skills, and they are
where students really struggle.

**Verdict: correct, and the mark scheme proves the first half by itself.** The research on the
second half is solid but it lives under different names — inference-making and situation-model
building — so it must be applied carefully rather than quoted as if it were about GCSE English.

---

## 1. THE MARK SCHEME SAYS IT OUTRIGHT (our own protocols, source of truth)
From `protocols/aqa/literature/modules/knowledge-mark-scheme.md` — AQA English Literature, top level:
- **AO1 top band:** "**Critical, exploratory, conceptualised** response to task and whole text.
  **Judicious** use of precise references to support interpretation(s)."
- **Typical features at the top of the level:** "a critical, exploratory, well-structured argument.
  It takes a **conceptualised approach** to the full task supported by a range of judicious
  references. There will be a **fine-grained and insightful analysis** of methods… **Convincing
  exploration** of one or more ideas/perspectives/contextual factors/interpretations."

Compare the band below it: "**Thoughtful, developed** response… **apt** references." And below that:
"**Clear, explained** response… **effective** use of references."

**The ladder is explicit: clear → thoughtful → conceptualised.** A student can write a perfectly
clear, well-organised, accurate essay and still sit two bands below the top, because clarity is not
the same as conceptual thinking. That is exactly Neil's point, and it comes from the mark scheme
itself — no external evidence needed.

⚠ **Terminology gate (WML CLAUDE.md §5c):** the controlling concept is a **conclusion element built
toward**, never named upfront. Any article describing "conceptualised" must match what the protocols
actually teach, not a general-knowledge gloss.

## 2. WHY "PERCEPTIVE" IS HARD — the research, under its real names

### 2a. Comprehension is layered; deeper meaning requires a different layer
- Graesser, A. & McNamara, D. (2010), *Computational Analyses of Multilevel Discourse
  Comprehension*, **Topics in Cognitive Science**, doi:10.1111/j.1756-8765.2010.01081.x — 329
  citations.
- Verbatim: the framework "includes the **surface code, the textbase, the situation model**, the
  genre and rhetorical structure, and the pragmatic communication level."
- **Why this matters for us:** a student can hold the textbase (what the words say) perfectly and
  still not have built a situation model (what is actually going on, and what it means). "Perceptive"
  reading is a *different level of representation*, not simply more effort at the same level. This
  gives us a principled explanation for a very familiar experience: the student who has clearly read
  the play and still cannot say anything insightful about it.

### 2b. Inference-making is a distinct, separable skill — and it is where weak comprehenders fail
- Cain, K. & Oakhill, J. (2001), *Comprehension skill, inference-making ability, and their relation
  to knowledge*, **Memory & Cognition**, doi:10.3758/bf03196414 — **607 citations.**
- ⚠ No abstract available via Crossref/OpenAlex, so **no quotation is recorded here**. The finding is
  named, not quoted. If the article wants a specific claim from it, read the paper first.
- The relevance: this literature separates *decoding and literal comprehension* from *inference*, and
  finds inference to be a specific weakness for some readers, not merely a by-product of general
  ability. It supports "inference is a trainable skill", which is the pedagogical claim we make.

### 2c. ⭐ Literary interpretation is a TEACHABLE PRACTICE — the best citation of the three
- Educational Testing Service (2020), *Building and Justifying Interpretations of Texts: A Key
  Practice in the English Language Arts*, doi:10.1002/ets2.12304 — 38 citations.
- Verbatim from the abstract, and it reads like a description of our own protocols: literary analysis
  is "a bundle of disciplinary skills and strategies", including "strategies for **deepening
  comprehension, inferring the author's purpose, considering multiple perspectives, connecting text
  to context, generalizing and applying themes**, and participating in cultural conversations about
  texts."
- It also lists what habitual practice looks like: "(a) **read and reread** texts to find cues that
  suggest new possibilities for interpretation, (b) participate in **interpretive discussions**,
  (c) **use writing both as a tool** to support interpretive work and as a means to communicate
  interpretive arguments, and (d) maintain an **openness to new interpretations** and a willingness
  to revisit previous conclusions in the light of new evidence."
- **Why it is the strongest of the three:** it directly contradicts the folk belief that insight is
  a talent you either have or lack. It frames interpretation as a *practice* — a set of habits that
  can be taught and rehearsed. That is precisely the mastery argument applied to the top band, and it
  is the paragraph the article should be built on.

## 3. WHAT NOT TO CLAIM
1. ⛔ **Do not say research shows students "can't think conceptually".** Nothing here supports a
   deficit claim about students. The evidence supports the opposite: these are skills, and skills are
   trained.
2. ⛔ **Do not quote Cain & Oakhill** until the paper is read — the citation is recorded without a
   quotation on purpose.
3. ⚠ **None of this research is about GCSE English.** It is reading-comprehension and ELA research.
   State the application as our reasoning, exactly as with the mocks article.
4. ⚠ **Do not conflate "perceptive" with "clever".** The mark scheme rewards insight that is
   *evidenced* — "judicious use of precise references". Insight without textual grounding scores
   badly, and the article should say so.

## 4. THE ARTICLE THIS SUPPORTS
Slots into the series as a strong candidate — either its own piece or the back half of piece 2:

**Working title: "Why a clear, accurate essay can still be a grade 6."**
- Open with the mark-scheme ladder in plain words: clear → thoughtful → conceptualised. Most students
  are aiming at the first rung and assume it is the whole ladder.
- What "conceptualised" means in practice: writing about the *idea* the text explores, not just the
  events — and holding that idea across the whole essay.
- What "perceptive" means: seeing what the text implies but never states — and then *proving it* from
  the words on the page.
- Why it is hard, honestly: it is a different level of comprehension (Graesser & McNamara), and
  inference is a separate skill from understanding the plot (Cain & Oakhill).
- The hopeful ending, which is the whole point: interpretation is **a practice, not a talent** (ETS
  2020) — reread for new cues, argue in discussion, write to think, stay open to revising.
- Ties back to the series' spine: this is the clearest case of a skill that a fixed calendar cannot
  deliver, because it only develops through repeated attempts with correction.
