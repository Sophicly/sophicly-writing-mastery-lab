**Article 1 is already the foundations article.** `ARTICLE-DRAFT-why-school-alone-rarely-produces-a-

A second article built on the karate belt would be article 1 with a different analogy. The handoff
already flags articles 3 and 4 as uncomfortably close cousins; this would be a third.

**But Neil's story is not actually a foundations story, and that is the interesting part.** Re-read
what he found when he probed the grade-8 student:

> *"I started asking how the exam is marked. What it's for? What it's testing? How the examiners
> actually award marks? And basically he couldn't articulate anything precisely from the mark scheme
> pretty much at all."*

The presenting complaint was a gap in teaching (a cover teacher for eighteen months). What the
diagnosis actually turned up was something else: a student getting **grade 8s** who could not say
what he was being marked on. That is not a hole in the foundations. That is **not knowing what the
building is for.**

⭐ **And assessment literacy is almost entirely uncovered by the existing series.** The words "mark
scheme", "marking criteria" and "assessment criteria" appear **once each in two of the five drafts**,
in passing. It is simultaneously our biggest content gap and the thing Sophicly most distinctively
does.

**Recommendation: the karate material strengthens article 1; the new article is assessment
literacy.** See §5 for the decision Neil needs to make.

Two claims, and they need separating because the evidence for them differs:
- **(a)** Students often work to satisfy the teacher rather than the criteria.

## §3 CONFIRMED — safe to print

### A. ⭐ Standards cannot be fully written down. This is the load-bearing finding.
**Royce Sadler (1989), *Formative Assessment and the Design of Instructional Systems*** — one of the
most cited papers in assessment. His argument: for a student to improve, they must be able to
**monitor the quality of their own work while producing it**, which requires holding a concept of
what good work looks like that is *roughly the same as the teacher's*. Sadler's further point is the
one that matters commercially for us: **achievement standards are partly tacit and cannot be fully
captured in written descriptors.** A mark scheme is a summary of a judgement, not the judgement.

⭐ **The consequence, and it is counter-intuitive: handing a student the mark scheme does not
produce assessment literacy.** Every school in the country has issued the criteria. The student in
Neil's story had almost certainly been shown them.

### B. What does work: applying the criteria to real work
**Rust, Price & O'Donovan (2003), *Improving students' learning by developing their understanding of
assessment criteria and processes*** (Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education). The intervention
that produced **measurably improved performance** was not publishing the criteria. It was
**exemplars, marking practice, and dialogue** — students judging real pieces of work against the
criteria and arguing about the result, so the tacit half transfers alongside the explicit half.
O'Donovan, Price and Rust developed this into the account now standard in the field: explicit
criteria and tacit, experience-based understanding are **complementary**, and students only grasp a
standard when they get both.

⭐ **This is a description of what Sophicly already does** — marking every piece against the
examiner's criteria, model answers, students working in the language of the mark scheme. We can
claim it with a citation rather than as a marketing line.

### C. The pressure half — and the twist is better than Neil's version
**Beilock & Carr, and the "choking under pressure" literature.** Performance pressure consumes
working memory. Worry occupies the same limited resource the task needs, so strategies that lean on
holding information in mind degrade exactly when the stakes rise.

⭐ **The counter-intuitive finding: it is students with HIGH working memory who choke.** They are the
ones who normally rely on working-memory-intensive strategies, so they have the most to lose when
pressure takes that capacity away. Students using simpler, more automatic strategies are less
affected.

**That is the grade-8 student in Neil's story, precisely.** The child who has been coasting on
recall and on remembering what the teacher said is not the weakest in the room — often they are
among the strongest, which is why the disappointing result is such a shock to the family.

⚠ **Caveat that must be stated:** this research is mostly laboratory work using maths problems. The
step to "GCSE English exam hall" is an **inference**, and the article must present it as one — the
same discipline article 5 uses for the mocks research.

### D. Knowing how to monitor your own work is high-impact and cheap
**EEF Toolkit — metacognition and self-regulation: +7 months** (some EEF pages now say +8; use +7,
the widely published figure, and say "about seven"). Rated high impact, low cost, strong evidence,
and **most effective when embedded in a specific subject rather than taught as a generic skill** —
which is an argument for doing it inside English rather than in a study-skills session.

## §4 NOT SUPPORTED / HANDLE WITH CARE

1. ⛔ **The "did brilliantly at school, got a grade 5" statistic does not exist in usable form.**
   Neil's observation of the post-results-day Facebook groups is real and I believe it, but I could
   not find a clean, citable UK figure for GCSE school-assessment-versus-exam divergence. The
   available predicted-grade research is mostly **A-level** and mostly about university applicants
   (one study of medical-school applicants: predictions accurate in 48.8% of cases, **over**-predicted
   in 44.7%, under-predicted in 6.5%). A "16% accurate" figure circulates and I could not trace it to
   a sound source. ✅ **Use the phenomenon as a description parents will recognise, never as a
   statistic.** Build the argument on the mechanism instead.
2. ⛔ **"Schools don't teach the mark scheme."** Untrue and needlessly insulting. They publish it,
   display it, and hand it out. Sadler explains why that is not sufficient, and that is a far
   stronger and fairer argument.
3. ⛔ **Anything implying the student in Neil's story was badly taught.** He had a cover teacher for
   eighteen months; he also had grade 8s. The article's point is that a grade 8 can coexist with not
   knowing what you are being marked on.
4. ⛔ **Naming or identifying any student.** Standing rule.
5. ⚠ **Khan's mindset half, the pyramid/automation section, and the "closer to 100 percent" figure**
   — all already ruled out in the Khan source file. Unchanged.

## §6 IF OPTION A — the article

**Working title:** *The student who got a grade 8 and could not say why*
**Alternatives:** *Marked by a stranger* · *What is actually being measured*

**Angle.** A child can do everything the school asks, please every teacher, collect strong marks all
year, and still walk into a hall to be judged by someone who has never met them, against a document
they have never really used. The gap between "what my teacher wants" and "what the criteria reward"
is invisible while a teacher is the audience. It becomes visible on results day.

**Shape:**
1. Open on the diagnostic — a student with grade 8s who could not say how the exam awards marks.
   Anonymous, generalised, no school named.
2. Why that is normal rather than shocking: the audience all year is a person, not a document.
3. Sadler: you cannot improve work you cannot judge, and judging requires holding the same idea of
   quality the examiner holds.
4. The twist parents will not expect: **being given the mark scheme does not fix it.** Standards are
   partly tacit. Every school issues the criteria; issuing them is not teaching them.
5. What does work — exemplars, marking real work, arguing about the judgement (Rust/Price/O'Donovan);
   and the EEF's ~7 months for teaching students to monitor their own work, best done inside the
   subject.
6. The pressure section: why a strategy of remembering instructions collapses under exam conditions,
   and why the students most exposed are often the strong ones. Stated as an inference from
   laboratory work, honestly.
7. Honest limits: no statistic for the results-day phenomenon; the pressure research is not from
   exam halls.
8. Close: a student who knows the criteria has something to fall back on when their memory of the
   lesson deserts them. Bridge to weekly marking against the real criteria and model answers.
