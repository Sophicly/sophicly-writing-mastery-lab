# PART 1 — WORD CHOICE AS CRAFT

The professional consensus, stated plainly: **the noun and the verb carry the meaning; the adjective and the adverb are seasoning.** All four craft authorities converge on this.

## 1.1 Concrete nouns are the base (Hale)

- **Principle:** A concrete/specific noun projects an image straight into the senses; an abstract noun makes the reader do conversion work and stays fuzzy. Build description from *named things*, not stacked adjectives.
- **GCSE application:** Replace category words with named things — not "some food" but "a fried egg"; not "a vehicle" but "a Ford Transit." The imagery comes from the noun, not from adjectives piled in front of it.
- **Quotes:**
  - "Not all nouns need be plain, but they must all be strong." — Hale
  - "Prefer the specific to the general… **Prefer the concrete to the abstract.**" — Hale (endorsing Fowler / Strunk & White)
  - "many writers make the mistake of loading up adjectives. But the best concentrate on **noun-filled detail**." — Hale

## 1.2 Dynamic verbs are the engine (Hale, Clark, Hart, Everett)

- **Principle:** More than any other word, the verb decides whether prose has power. A strong verb does three jobs at once — creates action, saves words, reveals who is acting. Weak "to be" verbs (is/are/was/were) and the passive drain momentum.
- **The single most teachable method — the verb-circling pass:** devote one rewrite to verbs alone; circle every *is/are/was/were*, replace as many as possible.
- **Show, don't tell, lives in the verb:** swap *felt/seemed* for a physical action that proves the feeling.
- **Find a strong verb by causality:** ask "what *made* this happen?" and put the cause in as the subject doing an active verb.
- **Quotes:**
  - "More than any other part of speech… it is the verb that determines whether a writer is a wimp or a wizard." — Hale
  - "The pros make strong nouns and dynamic verbs the heart of their style; verbs make their prose quiver." — Hale
  - "A dependence on *is* and its family screams 'rough draft.'" — Hale
  - "Strong verbs create action, save words, and reveal the players." — Clark, *Writing Tools*
  - "The more concrete the verb, the more likely it is to evoke the real world. 'Dashed' is more specific than 'ran.' 'Strode' is more specific than 'walked.'" — Hart
  - "Never use the passive where you can use the active." — Orwell, quoted in Clark
  - "It is much better to *show* the action than *tell* the action." — Everett
- **Before → after:**
  - "There were leaves all over the ground" → "Leaves covered the ground." (7 words → 4; Strunk & White via Clark)
  - "She felt nervous" → "Her hands trembled." (show, not tell)
  - "He walked to the door" → "He strode / trudged / crept to the door." (specificity)
  - "The window was broken by James" → "James shattered the window." (passive → active)
- **Use the passive DELIBERATELY** to spotlight a victim: "America's most populous bird **was wiped out**." Do not teach "passive is banned."

## 1.3 Adjective / adverb restraint (King, Le Guin, Hale, Clark)

- **Principle:** Reach for a modifier only when a precise noun or a strong verb genuinely can't carry the meaning. A modifier propping up a weak word is a symptom of the wrong word — fix the word, don't add the qualifier.
- **King, the adverb passage (verbatim, confirmed):**
  > "The adverb is not your friend."
  > "Adverbs… are words that modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs. They're the ones that usually end in *-ly*… seem to have been created with the timid writer in mind."
  > "Consider the sentence *He closed the door firmly*… isn't *firmly* an extra word? Isn't it redundant?"
  > "I believe **the road to hell is paved with adverbs**, and I will shout it from the rooftops… they're like dandelions. If you have one on your lawn, it looks pretty and unique. If you fail to root it out… you find five the next day."
  > "The best form of dialogue attribution is *said*, as in *he said, she said*."
- **Le Guin (verbatim):** "narrative prose… needs more muscle than fat." / "Ornate, fancy adjectives are out of fashion… Do you want to stop the flow? Is it worth it?"
- **The two honest images for a 14-year-old** (grounded, not fabricated):
  - **Muscle vs fat** (Le Guin): nouns and verbs are the muscle; modifiers are the fat. A little makes it tasty; too much and the writing can't run the distance.
  - **Dandelions** (King): one adverb looks pretty; leave them and the lawn becomes weeds.
  - **The "salt" framing is OURS** (see Accuracy §1): "Make the noun and the verb do the job. Add a describing word only when they honestly can't — the way you add salt: a pinch, on purpose, never a handful." Present unattributed.
- **Before → after:**
  - "He closed the door firmly." → "He slammed the door." (King's own: cut *firmly*, upgrade the verb)
  - "She walked quickly and angrily across the room." → "She stormed across the room."
  - "a very big, scary, old house" → "a derelict Victorian asylum" (specific noun describes)
  - "'Get out,' he said angrily." → "'Get out.' He said it and did not look up."
- **Clark's precise test — cut the adverb that REPEATS the verb, keep the one that CHANGES it:** "She smiled happily" (cut *happily* — *smiled* already means it) vs "She smiled sadly" (keep — it changes the meaning).

## 1.4 Diction, precision & connotation — the craft↔analysis BRIDGE (Clark, Steinemann)

This is the section that answers Neil's craft↔analysis link directly: **the same denotation/connotation distinction a student uses to *analyse* a writer's word choice (AO2 reading) is the one they use to *craft* their own.** Teach it once, apply both ways.

- **Precision / le mot juste:** the near-synonym is not neutral; every word carries associations the writer is accountable for.
  - "the difference between just the right word and almost the right word is the difference between lightning and the lightning bug." — Twain, via Clark
  - "The denotation of a word conveys its literal meaning. The connotation… the associations attached… Connotation can often add a layer of opinion to what appears to be a neutral report." — Clark, *Glamour of Grammar*
- **Connotation as a craft choice = bias as an analysis target** (the model demonstration):
  > "Richard Nixon was caught hiding in a limousine. / George McGovern was sighted striding into a sedan… A neutral report might have indicated that both of them 'got into their cars,' but the writers chose words with **vibrating connotations**." — Clark, *Glamour of Grammar*
  This single example teaches *both* "how does the writer's word choice reveal attitude?" (reading) and "choose your verb to plant an attitude" (writing).
- **Register — Anglo-Saxon vs Latinate:** "we often have two words for the same thing: a short word from an Anglo-Saxon root, and a longer word from a French and Latin root: as in *lively* and *vivacious*." — Clark. Blunt Anglo-Saxon = concrete punch (creative writing); Latinate = formal abstraction (essay register). Choosing between them *is* controlling tone.
- **Words to cut (the convergent list):**
  - **Filter words** (saw/heard/felt/noticed/seemed) — "form a barrier that distances readers." Fix: "Bertie felt the warm sand" → "The sand trickled between Bertie's toes."
  - **Crutch/filler words** (just, really, actually, basically, very) — "The fastest fix is deletion."
  - **Intensifiers that shrink meaning** (very, completely, extremely): "The blast **completely** destroyed the office" → "The blast destroyed the office."
  - **Bloated phrases:** "by means of" → "via"; "in spite of the fact that" → "although."
  - "I am dead to adverbs; they cannot excite me." — Twain, via Steinemann

# PART 2 — SENTENCE POWER

Cross-cutting rule from the GCSE slides, and it matches Sophicly's four-fold-effects law: **"Mere identification gets you nothing."** Every sentence technique is taught effect-first (what it does to the reader), never spot-the-label.

## 2.1 Already on the table (reinforce, do NOT re-add)
Simple/compound/complex; short sentence/fragment for effect; parallelism & rule of three / list of three; asyndeton & polysyndeton (Ay/Py — shipped v2.6.88); varying sentence length for pace; rhetorical question.

## 2.2 GENUINELY NEW / under-taught — highest value to add
Ranked by leverage for a GCSE student:

1. **Cumulative (loose) sentence** — base clause + trailing free-modifier phrases that zoom in. *"The dog trembled, ears flat, tail tucked, one paw lifted off the frozen ground."* Landon's most transferable tool for description; rarely named on GCSE tables.
   - "a sentence that takes a new step with each new modifying phrase we add… addition, movement, levels of generality, and texture." — Landon
   - Counter-myth (important): "the base clause… is [not] responsible for advancing its most important proposition… the *added* phrases can carry the emotional weight." — Landon
2. **Periodic (suspensive) sentence** — delay the main idea to the last word for suspense. *"After the noise, after the running, after the long silence, she finally understood."*
   - "the periodic sentence delays statements of the central idea until… right before the period… to create a special climax." — *Art of Styling Sentences*
   - (Direct tie-in to the new **Suspense `Sz`** cell — suspense at sentence scale.)
3. **Balanced sentence** ("rhythm of twos") — two mirrored halves across a semicolon, one/two words changed. *"We looked for shelter; the shelter looked for us."*
   - "A balanced sentence hinges in the middle… drawing power from the tension between repetition and variation." — Landon
4. **Absolute phrase** — noun + participle bolted on, no grammatical hook; a cinematic simultaneous close-up. *"He stood at the window, his hands shaking, the letter unopened."*
5. **Emphatic appositive after colon/dash** — rename for a punch at the end. *"There was one thing left in the box: a key."*
6. **"Landing" the key word (primacy/recency)** — put the salient word first or last, never buried. A rule, not a device.

## 2.3 Style principles (rules, not devices) — Moran, Hart, Casagrande, Baig
- **The end is the stress position:** "put the important stuff at the end… what stays in your listener's mind is the last thing you said… a sentence has a special snap if its last syllable is stressed." — Moran. Model: *"I binned that brainless book."*
- **Vary sentence length; long then short:** "The simplest structural element in prose rhythm is sentence length." — Hart. Move: long build → 2–4 word hammer.
- **Read it aloud:** "First I write a sentence… Then I look at it and say it aloud, to see if it sings." — Moran.
- **Economy:** cut every word that doesn't earn its place; "at this present moment in time" → "now."
- **Keep the verb a verb** (don't bury it in a noun): "She decided" not "She made a decision." — Casagrande.
- **Delete the empty opener** ("There was / It is") to free the real subject + a strong verb: "There were tears on her face" → "Tears streaked her face." — Hart.

## 2.4 Quotable lines for students (verbatim, attributed)
- "A sentence is more than its meaning. It is a line of words where logic and lyric meet." — Moran
- "You think you care what this book is about, but really you care how it sounds." — Moran
- "Good writing has a rhythm that pleases in its own right." — Hart
- "The road to hell is paved with adverbs, and I will shout it from the rooftops." — King
- "verbs make their prose quiver." — Hale
