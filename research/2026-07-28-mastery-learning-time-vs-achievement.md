# Mastery Learning — Time vs Achievement — Research Basis for the Blog Article

**Question:** Can we argue, in public and in Sophicly's name, that a time-based school system
structurally cannot deliver mastery — and that this is why we teach the way we do?

**Verdict: YES for the STRUCTURAL argument. NO for the headline effect sizes.**
The Carroll/Bloom structure is sound, well-cited and uncontroversial. The *magnitude* claims
(especially "2 sigma") are contested to the point where printing them would damage us.

**Method:** every citation below was resolved via Crossref DOI lookup on 28 Jul 2026, and the
abstracts quoted are the publishers' own. Items I did NOT independently verify in this pass are
flagged explicitly rather than left to look verified.

---

## VERIFIED FINDINGS (safe to build the article on)

### 1. Aptitude is a measure of TIME NEEDED, not of capacity — Carroll (1963)
- Carroll, J. B. "A Model of School Learning." *Teachers College Record* 64(8), 1963.
  doi:10.1177/016146816306400801 — **1,359 citations.** Paper existence, venue and standing verified.
- The model's core: a learner's aptitude for a task is expressed as *the amount of time needed*
  to learn it to a criterion, and the degree of learning is a function of time actually spent
  against time needed. This is the standard account of the paper and the foundation Bloom built on.
  *(Reading of the model's content is the canonical description, not a quotation from the paper.)*
- **This is the article's load-bearing idea and it is the one Neil already articulated:** hold time
  constant and achievement must vary; hold achievement constant and time must vary. There is no
  third option.

### 2. Mastery learning works, and it works MOST for the students who are behind — Kulik ×2 & Bangert-Drowns (1990)
- *Effectiveness of Mastery Learning Programs: A Meta-Analysis*, **Review of Educational Research**,
  doi:10.3102/00346543060002265 — **329 citations.**
- Publisher abstract, verbatim: "A meta-analysis of findings from **108 controlled evaluations**
  showed that mastery learning programs have **positive effects on the examination performance** of
  students in colleges, high schools, and the upper grades in elementary schools. **The effects
  appear to be stronger on the weaker students in a class**, and they also vary as a function of
  mastery procedures used, experimental designs of studies, and course content."
- Also in that abstract, and worth being honest about in the article: mastery programs "**may
  increase student time on instructional tasks**" — i.e. it costs more time. That is not a weakness
  of the argument, it IS the argument: mastery buys achievement with time.

### 3. Performance during instruction is an unreliable index of learning — Soderstrom & Bjork (2015)
- *Learning Versus Performance*, **Perspectives on Psychological Science**,
  doi:10.1177/1745691615569000 — **517 citations.** (Carries the mocks article too; see the
  companion doc.)
- Relevant here because it supplies the mechanism for why a time-based system *mismeasures*:
  what a school can observe in the term it has is performance, "which is often an unreliable index
  of whether the relatively long-term changes that constitute learning have taken place."

## REFUTED / DO NOT CITE

### ⛔ Bloom's "2 sigma" — do NOT lead with it, do not print it as an expectation
- The source is real: Bloom, B. S. "The 2 Sigma Problem", **Educational Researcher** 13(6), 1984,
  doi:10.3102/0013189x013006004 — 1,244 citations. Its existence is not in question.
- But it was **directly challenged in the same literature**: Slavin, R. E. *Mastery Learning
  Reconsidered*, **Review of Educational Research** 57(2), 1987,
  doi:10.3102/00346543057002175 — 140 citations. Publisher abstract, verbatim: "The review found
  **essentially no evidence to support the effectiveness of group-based mastery learning on
  standardized achievement measures**. On experimenter-made measures, effects were generally
  positive but **moderate in magnitude, with little evidence that effects maintained over time**."
- **Consequence for us:** a claim that mastery learning produces two-standard-deviation gains is
  not defensible in public. Worse, it is exactly the sort of too-good-to-be-true number that a
  sceptical parent or teacher can refute in one search — and it would cost us the authority the
  whole blog strategy depends on.

### ⛔ "Mastery learning is proven to raise grades" — as an unqualified claim
The evidence is design-sensitive: strong on experimenter-made measures, weak-to-absent on
standardised ones (Slavin), and varying by procedure, design and subject (Kulik). "Proven" is not
available. "Well-supported, with the effect concentrated in students who are behind" is.

## CAVEATS (honest limits, state them or design around them)

1. **The Kulik/Slavin split is a genuine disagreement, not a settled score.** Kulik's meta-analysis
   and Slavin's best-evidence synthesis reach materially different conclusions about group-based
   mastery learning. An honest article can say so — and saying so is itself a credibility asset,
   because almost nobody in this market does.
2. **Most of this literature is about GROUP-BASED mastery learning in schools** — i.e. a classroom
   teacher running mastery cycles against a fixed calendar. That is the very constraint Neil names.
   Sophicly is not group-based-in-a-fixed-term; we are closer to the tutoring end. That difference
   is a fair argument to make, but it must be made as an argument, not smuggled in by citing school
   studies as if they were studies of us.
3. **The "coverage versus mastery dilemma"** is Slavin's own framing of the problem and is a
   better, more defensible hook than any effect size: a school must cover the syllabus by a date,
   so when a student has not mastered something, coverage wins. That is the honest version of
   Neil's point and it comes from the *sceptic's* paper, which makes it far harder to dismiss.
4. **Not independently verified in this pass:** Bloom's *Learning for Mastery* (1968) and
   Guskey & Pigott's 1988 meta-analysis (Crossref holds the record — doi:10.1080/00220671.1988.10885824,
   63 citations — but no abstract). Do not quote figures from either until checked.
5. **Peterson & Seligman / VIA material** (from the retired post 1684) is a *values* argument, not
   an efficacy argument. It supports "mastery is universally admired", never "mastery learning
   raises grades". Keep the two strands clearly separated in the article.

## WHAT THE ARTICLE MAY CLAIM

- A time-based system holds time constant, so achievement must vary. **(Carroll, structural)**
- Schools are not villains for this; a cohort has to be moved through a calendar. **(Neil's
  concession, and Slavin's coverage-vs-mastery dilemma)**
- When mastery approaches are studied, the benefit shows up most in the students who are behind.
  **(Kulik et al., quotable)**
- Mastery costs time — that is the trade, and it is the trade Sophicly is built to absorb.
  **(Kulik et al., "may increase student time on instructional tasks")**
- What a term can measure is performance, which is an unreliable proxy for learning.
  **(Soderstrom & Bjork)**

## WHAT THE ARTICLE MUST NOT CLAIM
- Any "2 sigma" / "two standard deviations" expectation.
- That mastery learning is proven to raise standardised results.
- Any specific percentage or grade-uplift figure not measured on our own students.
