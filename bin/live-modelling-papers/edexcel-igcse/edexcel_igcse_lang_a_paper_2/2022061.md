## Source A
**Title:** Night
**Author:** Alice Munro
**Context:** Remind yourself of Night, taken from the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Anthology,.

1   When I was young, there seemed to be never a childbirth, or a burst appendix, or any other
2   drastic physical event that did not occur simultaneously with a snowstorm. The roads would
3   be closed, there was no question of digging out a car anyway, and some horses had to be
4   hitched up to make their way into town to the hospital. It was just lucky that there were
5   horses still around – in the normal course of events they would have been given up, but the
6   war and gas rationing had changed all that, at least for the time being.

7   When the pain in my side struck, therefore, it had to do so at about eleven o’clock at
8   night, and a blizzard had to be blowing, and since we were not stabling any horses at the
9   moment, the neighbors’¹ team had to be brought into action to take me to the hospital.
10  A trip of no more than a mile and a half but an adventure all the same. The doctor was
11  waiting, and to nobody’s surprise he prepared to take out my appendix. …

12  So I lay, minus my appendix, for some days looking out a hospital window at the snow
13  sifting in a somber way through some evergreens. I don’t suppose it ever crossed my
14  head to wonder how my father was going to pay for this distinction. (I think he sold a
15  woodlot that he had kept when he disposed of his father’s farm. He would have hoped to
16  use it for trapping or sugaring. Or perhaps he felt an unmentionable nostalgia.)

17  Then I went back to school, and enjoyed being excused from physical training for
18  longer than necessary, and one Saturday morning when my mother and I were alone
19  in the kitchen she told me that my appendix had been taken out in the hospital, just as
20  I thought, but it was not the only thing removed. The doctor had seen fit to take it out
21  while he was at it, but the main thing that concerned him was a growth. A growth, my
22  mother said, the size of a turkey’s egg.

23  But don’t worry, she said, it’s all over now.

24  The thought of cancer never entered my head and she never mentioned it. I don’t think
25  there could be such a revelation today without some kind of question, some probing
26  about whether it was or it wasn’t. Cancerous or benign – we would want to know at
27  once. The only way I can explain our failure to speak of it was that there must have been
28  a cloud around that word…

29  So I did not ask and wasn’t told and can only suppose it was benign or was most skillfully
30  got rid of, for here I am today. And so little do I think of it that all through my life when
31  called upon to list my surgeries, I automatically say or write only “Appendix”. …

32  In the heat of early June I got out of school, having made good enough marks to free me
33  from the final examinations. I looked well, I did chores around the house, I read books as
34  usual, nobody knew there was a thing the matter with me.

35  Now I have to describe the sleeping arrangements in the bedroom occupied by my
36  sister and myself. It was a small room that could not accommodate two single beds side
37  by side, so the solution was a pair of bunk beds, with a ladder in place to help whoever
38  slept in the top bunk climb into bed. That was me. When I had been younger and prone
39  to teasing, I would lift up the corner of my thin mattress and threaten to spit on my little
40  sister lying helpless in the bunk below. Of course my sister – her name was Catherine –
41  was not really helpless. She could hide under her covers, but my game was to watch until
42  suffocation or curiosity drove her out, and at that moment to spit or successfully pretend
43  to spit on her bared face, enraging her.

44  I was too old for such fooling, certainly too old by this time. My sister was nine when
45  I was fourteen. The relationship between us was always unsettled. When I wasn’t
46  tormenting her, teasing her in some asinine way, I would take on the role of sophisticated
47  counsellor or hair-raising storyteller…

48  I don’t mean to say that I was entirely in control of her, or even that our lives were
49  constantly intertwined. She had her own friends, her own games. …

50  In the month June, as I have said, I was free of school and left on my own, as I don’t
51  remember being in quite the same way in any other time of my growing-up. I did some
52  chores in the house, but my mother must have been well enough, as yet, to handle most
53  of that work. Or perhaps we had just enough money at the time to hire what she – my
54  mother – would call a maid, though everybody else said hired girl. I don’t remember, at
55  any rate, having to tackle any of the jobs that piled up for me in later summers, when I
56  fought quite willingly to maintain the decency of our house. It seems that the mysterious
57  turkey egg must have given me some invalid status, so that I could spend part of the
58  time wandering about like a visitor.

59  Though not trailing any special clouds. Nobody in our family would have got away with
60  that. It was all inward – this uselessness and strangeness I felt. …

61  It must have been just that every moment of the day was not filled up with jobs, as it was
62  in summers before and after.

63  So maybe that was the reason that I had begun to have trouble getting to sleep. At first,
64  I think, that meant lying wide awake maybe till around midnight and wondering at how
65  wide awake I was, with the rest of the household asleep. I would have read, and got tired
66  in the usual way, and turned out my light and waited. Nobody would have called out to
67  me earlier, telling me to put out my light and get to sleep. For the first time ever (and this
68  too must have marked a special status) I was left to make up my own mind about such a
69  thing.

70  It took a while for the house to change from the light of day and from the household
71  lights turned on late into the evening. Leaving behind the general clatter of things to be
72  done, hung up, finished with, it became a stranger place in which people and the work
73  that dictated their lives fell away, their uses for everything around them fell away, all the
74  furniture retreated into itself and no longer existed because of anybody’s attention.

75  You might think this was a liberation. At first, perhaps it was. The freedom. The
76  strangeness. But as my failure to fall asleep prolonged itself, and as it finally took hold
77  altogether until it changed into the dawn, I became more and more disturbed by it. I
78  started saying rhymes, then real poetry, first to make myself go under but then hardly of
79  my own volition. The activity seemed to mock me. I was mocking myself, as the words
80  turned into absurdity, into the silliest random speech.

81  I was not myself.

82  I had been hearing that said of people now and then, all my life, without thinking what it
83  could mean.

84  So who do you think you are, then?

85  I had been hearing that too, without attaching to it any real menace, just taking it as a
86  sort of routine jeering.

87  Think again.

88  By this time it wasn’t sleep I was after. I knew mere sleep wasn’t likely. Maybe not even
89  desirable. Something was taking hold of me and it was my business, my hope, to fight it
90  off. I had the sense to do that, but only barely, as it seemed. Whatever it was was trying to
91  tell me to do things, not exactly for any reason but just to see if such acts were possible.
92  It was informing me that motives were not necessary.

93  It was only necessary to give in. How strange. Not out of revenge, or for any normal
94  reason, but just because you had thought of something.

95  And I did think of it. The more I chased the thought away, the more it came back. No
96  vengeance, no hatred – as I’ve said, no reason, except something like an utterly cold deep
97  thought that was hardly an urging, more of a contemplation, could take possession of
98  me. I must not even think of it but I did think of it.

99  The thought was there and hanging in my mind.

100 The thought that I could strangle my little sister, who was asleep in the bunk below me
101 and whom I loved more than anybody in the world.

102 I might do it not for jealousy, viciousness, or anger, but because of madness, which could
103 be lying right beside me there in the night. Not a savage madness either, but something
104 that could be almost teasing. A lazy, teasing, half-sluggish suggestion that seemed to
105 have been waiting a long time.

106 It might be saying why not. Why not try the worst?

107 The worst. Here in the most familiar place, the room where we had lain for all our
108 lives and thought ourselves most safe. I might do it for no reason I or anybody could
109 understand, except that I could not help it.

110 The thing to do was to get up, to get myself out of that room and out of the house. I
111 went down the rungs of the ladder and never cast a single look at my sister where she
112 slept. Then quietly down the stairs, nobody stirring, into the kitchen where everything
113 was so familiar to me that I could make my way without a light. The kitchen door was
114 not really locked – I am not even sure that we possessed a key. A chair was pushed under
115 the doorknob so that anybody trying to get in would make a great clatter. A slow careful
116 removal of the chair could be managed without making any noise at all.

117 After the first night I was able to make my moves without a break, so that I could be
118 outside, as it seemed, within a couple of smooth seconds.

119 Of course there were no streetlights – we were too far from town.

120 Everything was larger. The trees around the house were always called by their names –
121 the beech tree, the elm tree, the oak tree, the maples always spoken of in the plural and
122 not differentiated, because they clung together. Now they were all intensely black. So
123 were the white lilac tree (no longer with its blooms) and the purple lilac tree – always
124 called lilac trees not bushes because they had grown too big.

125 The front and back and side lawns were easy to negotiate because I had mown them
126 myself with the idea of giving us some townlike respectability. …

127 Back and forth I walked, first close to the house and then venturing here and there as I
128 got to rely on my eyesight and could count on not bumping into the pump handle or
129 the platform that supported the clothesline. The birds began to stir, and then to sing - as
130 if each of them had thought of it separately, up there in the trees. They woke far earlier
131 than I would have thought possible. But soon after those earliest starting songs, there
132 got to be a little whitening in the sky. And suddenly I would be overwhelmed with
133 sleepiness. I went back into the house, where there was suddenly darkness everywhere,
134 and I very properly, carefully, silently, set the tilted chair under the doorknob, and
135 went upstairs without a sound, managing doors and steps with the caution necessary,
136 although I seemed already half asleep. I fell into my pillow, and I woke late – late in our
137 house being around eight o’clock.

138 I would remember everything then, but it was so absurd – the bad part of it indeed was
139 so absurd – that I could get rid of it fairly easily. My brother and sister had gone off to
140 their classes in the public school, but their dishes were still on the table, a few bits of
141 puffed rice floating in the excess milk.

142 Absurd.

143 When my sister got home from school we would swing in the hammock, one of us at
144 either end.

145 It was in that hammock that I spent much of the days, which possibly accounted for my not
146 getting to sleep at night. And since I did not speak of my night difficulties, nobody came up
147 with the simple information that I’d be better off getting more action during the day.

148 My troubles returned with the night, of course. The demons got hold of me again. I knew
149 enough soon to get up and out of my bunk without pretending that things would get
150 better and that I would in fact go to sleep if I just tried hard enough. I made my way as
151 carefully out of the house as I had done before. I became able to find my way around
152 more easily; even the inside of the rooms became more visible to me and yet more
153 strange. …

154 The east wall of the kitchen had no windows in it but it had a door opening on a stoop
155 where we stood to hang out the heavy wet washing, and haul it in when it was dry and
156 smelling all fresh and congratulatory, from white sheets to dark heavy overalls.

157 At that stoop I sometimes halted in my night walks. I never sat down but it eased me to
158 look towards town, maybe just to inhale the sanity of it. All the people getting up before
159 long, having their shops to go to, their doors to unlock and milk bottles to take inside,
160 their busyness.

161 One night – I can’t say whether it could be the twentieth or the twelfth or only the eighth
162 or the ninth that I had got up and walked – I got a sense, too late for me to change my
163 pace, that there was somebody around the corner. There was somebody waiting there
164 and I could do nothing but walk right on. I would be caught if I turned my back, and it
165 would be worse that way than to be confronted.

166 Who was it? Nobody but my father. He too sitting on the stoop looking towards town
167 and that improbable faint light. He was dressed in his day clothes – dark work pants, the
168 next thing to overalls but not quite, and dark, rough shirt and boots. He was smoking a
169 cigarette. One he rolled himself, of course. Maybe the cigarette smoke had alerted me to
170 another presence, though it’s possible that in those days the smell of tobacco smoke was
171 everywhere, inside buildings and out, so there was no way to notice it.

172 He said good morning, in what might have seemed a natural way except that there was
173 nothing natural about it. We weren’t accustomed to giving such greetings in our family.
174 There was nothing hostile about this – it was just thought unnecessary, I suppose, when
175 we would see each other off and on all day.

176 I said good morning back. And it must have really been getting towards morning
177 or my father would not have been dressed for a day’s work in that way. The sky may
178 have been whitening but hidden still between the heavy trees. The birds singing, too.
179 I had taken to staying away from my bunk till later and later, even though I didn’t get
180 comfort from doing so as I had at first. The possibilities that had once inhabited only
181 the bedroom, the bunk beds, were taking up the corners everywhere.

182 Now that I come to think of it, why wasn’t my father in his overalls? He was dressed as if
183 he had to go into town for something, first thing in the morning.

184 I could not continue walking, the whole rhythm of it had been broken.

185 “Having trouble sleeping?” he said.

186 My impulse was to say no, but then I thought of the difficulties of explaining that I was
187 just walking around, so I said yes.

188 He said that was often the case on summer nights.

189 “You go to bed tired out and then just as you think you’re falling asleep you’re wide
190 awake. Isn’t that the way?”

191 I said yes.

192 I knew now that he had not heard me getting up and walking around on just this one
193 night. The person whose livestock was on the premises, whose earnings such as they
194 were lay all close by, and who kept a handgun in his desk drawer, was certainly going to
195 stir at the slightest creeping on the stairs and the easiest turning of the knob.

196 I am not sure what conversation he meant to follow then, as regards to my being
197 awake. He seems to have declared wakefulness to be a nuisance, but was that to be all?
198 I certainly did not intend to tell him more. If he had given the slightest intimation that
199 he knew there was more, if he’d even hinted that he had come here intending to hear
200 it, I don’t think he’d have got anything out of me at all. I had to break the silence out of
201 my own will, saying that I could not sleep. I had to get out of bed and walk.

202 Why was that?

203 I did not know.

204 Not bad dreams?

205 No.

206 “Stupid question,” he said. “You wouldn’t get chased out of your bed on account of good
207 dreams.”

208 He let me wait to go on, he didn’t ask anything. I meant to back off but I kept talking. The
209 truth was told with only the slightest modification.

210 When I spoke of my little sister I said that I was afraid I would hurt her. I believed that
211 would be enough, that he would know enough of what I meant.

212 “Strangle her,” I said then. I could not stop myself, after all.

213 Now I could not unsay it, I could not go back to the person I had been before.

214 My father had heard it. He had heard that I thought myself capable of, for no reason,
215 strangling little Catherine in her sleep.

216 He said, “Well.”

217 Then he said not to worry. He said, “People have those kinds of thoughts sometimes.”

218 He said this quite seriously and without any sort of alarm or jumpy surprise. People have
219 these kinds of thoughts or fears if you like, but there’s no real worry about it, no more
220 than a dream, you could say.

221 He did not say, specifically, that I was in no danger of doing such a thing. He seemed
222 more to be taking it for granted that such a thing could not happen. An effect of the
223 ether, he said. Ether they gave you in the hospital. No more sense than a dream. It could
224 not happen, in the way that a meteor could not hit our house (of course it could, but the
225 likelihood of its doing so put it in the category of couldn’t).

226 He did not blame me, though, for thinking of it. Did not wonder at me, was what he said.

227 There were other things he could have said. He could have questioned me further about
228 my attitude to my little sister or my dissatisfactions with my life in general. If this were
229 happening today, he might have made an appointment for me to see a psychiatrist. (I
230 think that is what I might have done for a child, a generation and an income further on.)

231 The fact is, what he did worked as well. It set me down, but without either mockery or
232 alarm, in the world we were living in.

233 People have thoughts they’d sooner not have. It happens in life.

234 If you live long enough as a parent nowadays, you discover that you have made mistakes
235 you didn’t bother to know about along with the ones you do know about all too well.
236 You are somewhat humbled at heart, sometimes disgusted with yourself. I don’t think
237 my father felt anything like this. I do know that if I had ever taxed him, with his use on
238 me of the razor strap or his belt, he might have said something about liking or lumping
239 it. Those strappings, then, would have stayed in his mind, if they stayed at all, as no more
240 than the necessary and adequate curbing of a mouthy child’s imagining that she should
241 rule the roost.

242 “You thought you were too smart,” was what he might have given as his reason for
243 the punishments, and indeed you heard that often in those times, with the smartness
244 figuring as an obnoxious imp that had to have the sass beaten out of him. Otherwise
245 there was the risk of him growing up thinking he was smart. Or her, as the case might be.

246 However, on that breaking morning he gave me just what I needed to hear and what I
247 was even to forget about soon enough.

248 I have thought that he was maybe in his better work clothes because he had a morning
249 appointment to go to the bank, to learn, not to his surprise, that there was no extension to
250 his loan. He had worked as hard as he could but the market was not going to turn around
251 and he had to find a new way of supporting us and paying off what we owed at the same
252 time. Or he may have found out that there was a name for my mother’s shakiness and that
253 it was not going to stop. Or that he was in love with an impossible woman.

254 Never mind. From then on I could sleep.
