## Source A
**Title:** Significant Cigarettes (from The Road Home)
**Author:** Rose Tremain
**Context:** Remind yourself of Significant Cigarettes (from The Road Home), taken from the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Anthology, which is provided in the.

1   On the coach, Lev chose a seat near the back and he sat huddled against the window,
2   staring out at the land he was leaving: at the fields of sunflowers scorched by the dry
3   wind, at the pig farms, at the quarries and rivers and at the wild garlic growing green at
4   the edge of the road.

5   Lev wore a leather jacket and jeans and a leather cap pulled low over his eyes and his
6   handsome face was grey-toned from his smoking and in his hands he clutched an old red
7   cotton handkerchief and a dented pack of Russian cigarettes. He would soon be forty-
8   three.

9   After some miles, as the sun came up, Lev took out a cigarette and stuck it between
10  his lips, and the woman sitting next to him, a plump, contained person with moles like
11  splashes of mud on her face, said quickly: ‘I’m sorry, but there is no smoking allowed on
12  this bus.’

13  Lev knew this, had known it in advance, had tried to prepare himself mentally for the
14  long agony of it. But even an unlit cigarette was a companion – something to hold on
15  to, something that had promise in it – and all he could be bothered to do now was to
16  nod, just to show the woman that he’d heard what she’d said, reassure her that he wasn’t
17  going to cause trouble; because there they would have to sit for fifty hours or more,
18  side by side with their separate aches and dreams, like a married couple. They would
19  hear each other’s snores and sighs, smell the food and drink each had brought with
20  them, note the degree to which each was fearful or unafraid, make short forays into
21  conversation. And then later, when they finally arrived in London, they would probably
22  separate with barely a word or a look, walk out into a rainy morning, each alone and
23  beginning a new life. And Lev thought how all of this was odd but necessary and already
24  told him things about the world he was travelling to, a world in which he would break his
25  back working – if only that work could be found. He would hold himself apart from other
26  people, find corners and shadows in which to sit and smoke, demonstrate that he didn’t
27  need to belong, that his heart remained in his own country.

28  There were two coach-drivers. These men would take turns to drive and to sleep. There
29  was an on-board lavatory, so the only stops the bus would make would be for gas. At gas
30  stations, the passengers would be able to clamber off, walk a few paces, see wild flowers
31  on a verge, soiled paper among bushes, sun or rain on the road. They might stretch up
32  their arms, put on dark glasses against the onrush of nature’s light, look for a clover leaf,
33  smoke and stare at the cars rushing by. Then they would be herded back onto the coach,
34  resume their old attitudes, arm themselves for the next hundred miles, for the stink
35  of another industrial zone, or the sudden gleam of a lake, for rain and sunset and the
36  approach of darkness on silent marshes. There would be times when the journey would
37  seem to have no end.

38  Sleeping upright was not something Lev was practised in. The old seemed to be able to
39  do it, but forty-two was not yet old. Lev’s father, Stefan, sometimes used to sleep upright,
40  in summer, on a hard wooden chair in his lunch break at the Baryn sawmill, with the hot
41  sun falling onto the slices of sausage wrapped in paper on his knee and onto his flask
42  of tea. Both Stefan and Lev could sleep lying down on a mound of hay or on the mossy
43  carpet of a forest. Often, Lev had slept on a rag rug beside his daughter’s bed, when she
44  was ill or afraid. And when his wife, Marina, was dying, he’d lain for five nights on an area
45  of linoleum flooring no wider than his outstretched arm, between Marina’s hospital bed
46  and a curtain patterned with pink and purple daisies, and sleep had come and gone in a
47  mystifying kind of way, painting strange pictures in Lev’s brain that had never completely
48  vanished.

49  Towards evening, after two stops for gas, the mole-flecked woman unwrapped a hard-
50  boiled egg. She peeled it silently. The smell of the egg reminded Lev of the sulphur
51  springs at Jor, where he’d taken Marina, just in case nature could cure what man had
52  given up for lost. Marina had immersed her body obediently in the scummy water, lain
53  there looking at a female stork returning to its high nest, and said to Lev: ‘If only we were
54  storks.’

55  ‘Why d’you say that?’ Lev had asked.

56  ‘Because you never see a stork dying. It’s as though they didn’t die.’

57  If only we were storks.

58  On the woman’s knee a clean cotton napkin was spread and her white hands smoothed it
59  and she unwrapped rye bread and a twist of salt.

60  ‘My name is Lev,’ said Lev.

61  ‘My name is Lydia,’ said the woman. And they shook hands, Lev’s hand holding the
62  scrunched-up kerchief, and Lydia’s hand rough with salt and smelling of egg, and
63  then Lev asked: ‘What are you planning to do in England?’ and Lydia said: ‘I have some
64  interviews in London for jobs as a translator.’

65  ‘That sounds promising.’

66  ‘I hope so. I was a teacher of English at School 237 in Yarbl, so my language is very
67  colloquial.’

68  Lev looked at Lydia. It wasn’t difficult to imagine her standing in front of a class and
69  writing words on a blackboard. He said: ‘I wonder why you’re leaving our country when
70  you had a good job at School 237 in Yarbl?’

71  ‘Well,’ said Lydia. ‘I became very tired of the view from my window. Every day, summer
72  and winter, I looked out at the school yard and the high fence and the apartment block
73  beyond, and I began to imagine I would die seeing these things, and I didn’t want this.
74  I expect you understand what I mean?’

75  Lev took off his leather cap and ran his fingers through his thick grey hair. He saw Lydia
76  turn to him for a moment and look very seriously into his eyes. He said: ‘Yes, I understand.’

77  Then there was a silence, while Lydia ate her hard-boiled egg. She chewed very quietly.
78  When she’d finished the egg, Lev said: ‘My English isn’t too bad. I took some classes in
79  Baryn, but my teacher told me my pronunciation wasn’t very good. May I say some words
80  and you can tell me if I’m pronouncing them correctly?’

81  ‘Yes, of course,’ said Lydia.

82  Lev said: ‘Lovely. Sorry. I am legal. How much please. Thank you. May you help me.’

83  ‘May I help you,’ corrected Lydia.

84  ‘May I help you,’ repeated Lev.

85  ‘Go on,’ said Lydia.

86  ‘Stork,’ said Lev. ‘Stork’s nest. Rain. I am lost. I wish for an interpreter. Bee-and-bee.’

87  ‘Be-and-be?’ said Lydia. ‘No, no. You mean “to be, or not to be” ’.

88  ‘No,’ said Lev. ‘Bee-and-bee. Family hotel, quite cheap.’

89  ‘Oh, yes, I know. B & B.’

90  Lev could now see that darkness was falling outside the window and he thought how,
91  in his village, darkness had always arrived in precisely the same way, from the same
92  direction, above the same trees, whether early or late, whether in summer, winter or
93  spring, for the whole of his life. This darkness – particular to that place, Auror – was how,
94  in Lev’s heart, darkness would always fall.

95  And so he told Lydia that he came from Auror, had worked in the Baryn sawmill until
96  it closed two years ago, and since then he’d found no work at all and his family – his
97  mother, his five-year-old daughter and he – had lived off the money his mother made
98  selling jewellery manufactured from tin.

99  ‘Oh,’ said Lydia. ‘I think that’s very resourceful, to make jewellery from tin.’

100 ‘Sure,’ said Lev. ‘But it isn’t enough.’

101 Tucked into his boot was a small flask of vodka. He extracted the flask and took a long
102 swig. Lydia kept eating her rye bread. Lev wiped his mouth with the red handkerchief
103 and saw his face reflected in the coach window. He looked away. Since the death of
104 Marina, he didn’t like to catch sight of his own reflection, because what he always saw in
105 it was his own guilt at still being alive.

106 ‘Why did the sawmill at Baryn close?’ asked Lydia.

107 ‘They ran out of trees,’ said Lev.

108 ‘Very bad,’ said Lydia. ‘What other work can you do?’

109 Lev drank again. Someone had told him that in England vodka was too expensive to
110 drink. Immigrants made their own alcohol from potatoes and tap water, and when Lev
111 thought about these industrious immigrants, he imagined them sitting by a coal fire in
112 a tall house, talking and laughing, with rain falling outside the window and red buses
113 going past and a television flickering in a corner of the room. He sighed and said: ‘I will
114 do any work at all. My daughter Maya needs clothes, shoes, books, toys, everything.
115 England is my hope.’

116 Towards ten o’clock, red blankets were given out to the coach passengers, some of whom
117 were already sleeping. Lydia put away the remnants of her meal, covered her body with
118 the blanket and switched on a fierce little light above her under the baggage rack and
119 began reading a faded old paperback, printed in English. Lev saw that the title of her
120 book was The Power and the Glory. His longing for a cigarette had grown steadily since
121 he’d drunk the vodka and now it was acute. He could feel the yearning in his lungs and
122 in his blood, and his hands grew fidgety and he felt a tremor in his legs. How long before
123 the next gas stop? It could be four or five hours. Everyone on the bus would be asleep by
124 then, except him and one of the two drivers. Only they would keep a lonely, exhausting
125 vigil, the driver’s body tensed to the moods and alarms of the dark, unravelling road; his
126 own aching for the comfort of nicotine or oblivion – and getting neither.

127 He envied Lydia, immersed in her English book. Lev knew he had to distract himself
128 with something…. In desperation, he took from his wallet a brand new British twenty-
129 pound note and reached up and switched on his own little reading light and began to
130 examine the note. On one side, the frumpy Queen, E II R, with her diadem, her face grey
131 on a purple ground, and on the other, a man, some personage from the past, with a dark
132 drooping moustache and an angel blowing a trumpet above him and all the angel’s
133 radiance falling on him in vertical lines. ‘The British venerate their history,’ Lev had been
134 told in his English class, ‘chiefly because they have never been subjected to Occupation.
135 Only intermittently do they see that some of their past deeds were not good.’

136 The indicated lifespan of the man on the note was 1857–1934. He looked like a banker,
137 but what had he done to be on a twenty-pound note in the twenty-first century? Lev
138 stared at his determined jaw, squinted at his name written out in a scrawl beneath the
139 wing collar, but couldn’t read it. He thought that this was a person who would never have
140 known any other system of being alive but Capitalism. He would have heard the names
141 Hitler and Stalin, but not been afraid – would have had no need to be afraid of anything
142 except a little loss of capital in what Americans called the Crash, when men in New York
143 had jumped out of windows and off roofs. He would have died safely in his bed before
144 London was bombed to ruins, before Europe was torn apart. Right to the end of his days,
145 the angel’s radiance had probably shone on this man’s brow and on his fusty clothes,
146 because it was known across the world: the English were lucky. Well, thought Lev, I’m
147 going to their country now and I’m going to make them share it with me: their infernal
148 luck. I’ve left Auror and that leaving of my home was hard and bitter, but my time is
149 coming.
