## Source A
**Title:** The Necklace
**Author:** Guy de Maupassant
**Context:** Remind yourself of The Necklace, taken from the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Anthology, which is provided in the Source Booklet.

1   She was one of those pretty, delightful girls who, apparently by some error of Fate,
2   get themselves born the daughters of very minor civil servants. She had no dowry, no
3   expectations, no means of meeting some rich, important man who would understand,
4   love, and marry her. So she went along with a proposal made by a junior clerk in the
5   Ministry of Education.

6   She dressed simply, being unable to afford anything better, but she was every whit as
7   unhappy as any daughter of good family who has come down in the world. Women have
8   neither rank nor class, and their beauty, grace, and charm do service for birthright and
9   connections. Natural guile, instinctive elegance, and adaptability are what determines
10  their place in the hierarchy, and a girl of no birth to speak of may easily be the equal of
11  any society lady.

12  She was unhappy all the time, for she felt that she was intended for a life of refinement
13  and luxury. She was made unhappy by the run-down apartment they lived in, the peeling
14  walls, the battered chairs, and the ugly curtains. Now all this, which any other woman of
15  her station might never even have noticed, was torture to her and made her very angry.
16  The spectacle of the young Breton peasant girl who did the household chores stirred sad
17  regrets and impossible fancies. She dreamed of silent antechambers hung with oriental
18  tapestries, lit by tall, bronze candelabras, and of two tall footmen in liveried breeches
19  asleep in the huge armchairs, dozing in the heavy heat of a stove. She dreamed of great
20  drawing-rooms dressed with old silk, filled with fine furniture which showed off trinkets
21  beyond price, and of pretty little parlours, filled with perfumes and just made for intimate
22  talk at five in the afternoon with one’s closest friends who would be the most famous
23  and sought-after men of the day whose attentions were much coveted and desired by all
24  women.

25  When she sat down to dinner at the round table spread with a three-day-old cloth, facing
26  her husband who always lifted the lid of the soup-tureen and declared delightedly: ‘Ah!
27  Stew! Splendid! There’s nothing I like better than a nice stew ...’, she dreamed of elegant
28  dinners, gleaming silverware, and tapestries which peopled the walls with mythical
29  characters and strange birds in enchanted forests; she dreamed of exquisite dishes
30  served on fabulous china plates, of pretty compliments whispered into willing ears and
31  received with Sphinx-like smiles over the pink flesh of a trout or the wings of a hazel hen.

32  She had no fine dresses, no jewellery, nothing. And that was all she cared about; she felt
33  that God had made her for such things. She would have given anything to be popular,
34  envied, attractive, and in demand.

35  She had a friend who was rich, a friend from her convent days, on whom she never called
36  now, for she was always so unhappy afterwards. Sometimes, for days on end, she would
37  weep tears of sorrow, regret, despair, and anguish.

38  One evening her husband came home looking highly pleased with himself. In his hand
39  he brandished a large envelope.

40  ‘Look,’ he said, ‘I’ve got something for you.’

41  She tore the paper flap eagerly and extracted a printed card bearing these words:

42  ‘The Minister of Education and Madame Georges Ramponneau request the pleasure
43  of the company of Monsieur and Madame Loisel at the Ministry Buildings on the
44  evening of 18 January.’

45  Instead of being delighted as her husband had hoped, she tossed the invitation peevishly
46  onto the table and muttered: ‘What earthly use is that to me?’

47  ‘But, darling, I thought you’d be happy. You never go anywhere and it’s an opportunity,
48  a splendid opportunity! I had the dickens of a job getting hold of an invite. Everybody’s
49  after them; they’re very much in demand and not many are handed out to us clerks. You’ll
50  be able to see all the big nobs there.’

51  She looked at him irritably and said shortly: ‘And what am I supposed to wear if I do go?’

52  He had not thought of that. He blustered: ‘What about the dress you wear for the theatre?
53  It looks all right to me …’ The words died in his throat. He was totally disconcerted and
54  dismayed by the sight of his wife who had begun to cry. Two large tears rolled slowly out
55  of the corners of her eyes and down towards the sides of her mouth.

56  ‘What’s up?’ he stammered. ‘What’s the matter?’

57  Making a supreme effort, she controlled her sorrows and, wiping her damp cheeks,
58  replied quite calmly: ‘Nothing. It’s just that I haven’t got anything to wear and
59  consequently I shan’t be going to any reception. Give the invite to one of your colleagues
60  with a wife who is better off for clothes than I am.’

61  He was devastated. He went on: ‘Oh come on, Mathilde. Look, what could it cost to get
62  something suitable that would do for other occasions, something fairly simple?’

63  She thought for a few moments, working out her sums but also wondering how much
64  she could decently ask for without drawing an immediate refusal and pained protests
65  from her husband who was careful with his money. Finally, after some hesitation, she
66  said: ‘I can’t say precisely, but I daresay I could get by on four hundred francs.’

67  He turned slightly pale, for he had been setting aside just that amount to buy a gun and
68  finance hunting trips the following summer in the flat landscape around Nanterre with a
69  few friends who went shooting larks there on Sundays. But he said: ‘Very well. I’ll give you
70  your four hundred francs. But do try and get a decent dress.’

71  The day of the reception drew near and Madame Loisel appeared sad, worried, anxious.
72  Yet all her clothes were ready. One evening her husband said: ‘What’s up? You haven’t half
73  been acting funny these last few days.’

74  She replied: ‘It vexes me that I haven’t got a single piece of jewellery, not one stone, that I
75  can put on. I’ll look like a church mouse. I’d almost as soon not go to the reception.’

76  ‘Wear a posy,’ he said. ‘It’s all the rage this year. You could get two or three magnificent
77  roses for ten francs.’

78  She was not convinced. ‘No. …There’s nothing so humiliating as to look poor when you’re
79  with women who are rich.’

80  But her husband exclaimed: ‘You aren’t half silly! Look, go and see your friend, Madame
81  Forestier, and ask her to lend you some jewellery. You know her well enough for that.’
82  She gave a delighted cry: ‘You’re right! I never thought of that!’

83  The next day she called on her friend and told her all about her problem. Madame
84  Forestier went over to a mirror-fronted wardrobe, took out a large casket, brought it over,
85  unlocked it, and said to Madame Loisel: ‘Choose whatever you like.’

86  At first she saw bracelets, then a rope of pearls and a Venetian cross made of gold and
87  diamonds admirably fashioned. She tried on the necklaces in the mirror, and could hardly
88  bear to take them off and give them back. She kept asking: ‘Have you got anything else?’

89  ‘Yes, of course. Just look. I can’t say what sort of thing you’ll like best.’

90  All of a sudden, in a black satinwood case, she found a magnificent diamond necklace,
91  and her heart began to beat with immoderate desire. Her hands shook as she picked
92  it up. She fastened it around her throat over her high-necked dress and sat looking at
93  herself in rapture. Then, diffidently, apprehensively, she asked: ‘Can you lend me this?
94  Nothing else. Just this.’

95  ‘But of course.’

96  She threw her arms around her friend, kissed her extravagantly, and then ran home,
97  taking her treasure with her.

98  The day of the reception arrived. Madame Loisel was a success. She was the prettiest
99  woman there, elegant, graceful, radiant, and wonderfully happy. All the men looked at
100 her, enquired who she was, and asked to be introduced. All the cabinet secretaries and
101 under-secretaries wanted to waltz with her. She was even noticed by the Minister himself.

102 She danced ecstatically, wildly, intoxicated with pleasure, giving no thought to anything
103 else, swept along on her victorious beauty and glorious success, and floating on a cloud
104 of happiness composed of the homage, admiration, and desire she evoked and the kind
105 of complete and utter triumph which is so sweet to a woman’s heart.

106 She left at about four in the morning. Since midnight her husband had been dozing in
107 a small, empty side-room with three other men whose wives were having an enjoyable
108 time.

109 He helped her on with her coat which he had fetched when it was time to go, a modest,
110 everyday coat, a commonplace coat violently at odds with the elegance of her dress.
111 It brought her down to earth, and she would have preferred to slip away quietly and
112 avoid being noticed by the other women who were being arrayed in rich furs. But Loisel
113 grabbed her by the arm: ‘Wait a sec. You’ll catch cold outside. I’ll go and get a cab.’

114 But she refused to listen and ran quickly down the stairs. When they were outside in the
115 street, there was no cab in sight. They began looking for one, hailing all the cabbies they
116 saw driving by in the distance.

117 They walked down to the Seine in desperation, shivering with cold. There, on the
118 embankment, they at last found one of those aged nocturnal hackney cabs which only
119 emerge in Paris after dusk, as if ashamed to parade their poverty in the full light of day.
120 It bore them back to their front door in the rue des Martyrs, and they walked sadly up to
121 their apartment. For her it was all over, while he was thinking that he would have to be at
122 the Ministry at ten.

123 Standing in front of the mirror, she took off the coat she had been wearing over her
124 shoulders, to get a last look at herself in all her glory. Suddenly she gave a cry. The

125 necklace was no longer round her throat!

126 Her husband, who was already half undressed, asked: ‘What’s up?’

127 She turned to him in a panic: ‘I … I … Madame Forestier’s necklace … I haven’t got it!’

128 He straightened up as if thunderstruck: ‘What? … But … You can’t have lost it!’

129 They looked in the pleats of her dress, in the folds of her coat, and in her pockets. They
130 looked everywhere. They did not find it.

131 ‘Are you sure you still had it when you left the ballroom?’ he asked.

132 ‘Yes, I remember fingering it in the entrance hall.’

133 ‘But if you’d lost it in the street, we’d have heard it fall. So it must be in the cab.’

134 ‘That’s right. That’s probably it. Did you get his number?’

135 ‘No. Did you happen to notice it?’

136 ‘No.’

137 They looked at each other in dismay. Finally Loisel got dressed again. ‘I’m going to go
138 back the way we came,’ he said, ‘to see if I can find it.’ He went out. She remained as
139 she was, still wearing her evening gown, not having the strength to go to bed, sitting
140 disconsolately on a chair by the empty grate, her mind a blank.

141 Her husband returned at about seven o’clock. He had found nothing.

142 He went to the police station, called at newspaper offices where he advertised a reward,
143 toured the cab companies, and tried anywhere where the faintest of hopes led him. She
144 waited for him all day long in the same distracted condition, thinking of the appalling
145 catastrophe which had befallen them.

146 Loisel came back that evening, hollow-cheeked and very pale. He had not come up with
147 anything.

148 ‘Look,’ he said, ‘you’ll have to write to your friend and say you broke the catch on her
149 necklace and you are getting it repaired. That’ll give us time to work out what we’ll have
150 to do.’

151 She wrote to his dictation.

152 A week later they had lost all hope.

153 Loisel, who had aged five years, said: ‘We’ll have to start thinking about replacing the
154 necklace.’

155 The next day they took the case in which it had come and called on the jeweller whose
156 name was inside. He looked through his order book.

157 ‘It wasn’t me that sold the actual necklace. I only supplied the case.’

158 After this, they trailed round jeweller’s shops, looking for a necklace just like the other
159 one, trying to remember it, and both ill with worry and anxiety.

160 In a shop in the Palais Royal they found a diamond collar which they thought was
161 identical to the one they were looking for. It cost forty thousand francs. The jeweller was
162 prepared to let them have it for thirty-six.

163 They asked him not to sell it for three days. And they got him to agree to take it back for
164 thirty-four thousand if the one that had been lost turned up before the end of February.

165 Loisel had eighteen thousand francs which his father had left him. He would have to
166 borrow the rest.

167 He borrowed the money, a thousand francs here, five hundred there, sometimes a
168 hundred and as little as sixty. He signed notes, agreed to pay exorbitant rates of interest,
169 resorted to usurers and the whole tribe of moneylenders. He mortgaged the rest of his
170 life, signed papers without knowing if he would ever be able to honour his commitments,
171 and then, sick with worry about the future, the grim poverty which stood ready to
172 pounce, and the prospect of all the physical privation and mental torture ahead, he went
173 round to the jeweller’s to get the new necklace with the thirty-six thousand francs which
174 he put on the counter.

175 When Madame Loisel took it round, Madame Forestier said in a huff: ‘You ought really to
176 have brought it back sooner. I might have needed it.’

177 She did not open the case, as her friend had feared she might. If she had noticed the
178 substitution, what would she have thought? What would she have said? Would she not
179 have concluded she was a thief?

180 Then began for Madame Loisel the grindingly horrible life of the very poor. But quickly
181 and heroically, she resigned herself to what she could not alter: their appalling debt
182 would have to be repaid. She was determined to pay. They dismissed the maid. They
183 moved out of their apartment and rented an attic room.

184 She became used to heavy domestic work and all kinds of ghastly kitchen chores. She
185 washed dishes, wearing down her pink nails on the greasy pots and saucepans. She
186 washed the dirty sheets, shirts, and floorcloths by hand and hung them up to dry on a
187 line; each morning she took the rubbish down to the street and carried the water up,
188 pausing for breath on each landing. And, dressed like any working-class woman, she
189 shopped at the fruiterer’s, the grocer’s, and the butcher’s, with a basket over her arm,
190 haggling, frequently abused and always counting every penny.

191 Each month they had to settle some accounts, renew others, and bargain for time.

192 Her husband worked in the evenings doing accounts for a shopkeeper and quite
193 frequently sat up into the early hours doing copying work at five sous¹ a page.

194 They lived like this for ten years.

195 By the time ten years had gone by, they had repaid everything, with not a penny
196 outstanding, in spite of the extortionate conditions and including the accumulated
197 interest.

198 Madame Loisel looked old now. She had turned into the battling, hard, uncouth
199 housewife who rules working-class homes. Her hair was untidy, her skirts were askew,
200 and her hands were red. She spoke in a gruff voice and scrubbed floors on her hands
201 and knees. But sometimes, when her husband had gone to the office, she would sit by
202 the window and think of that evening long ago when she had been so beautiful and so
203 admired.

204 What might not have happened had she not lost the necklace? Who could tell? Who

205 could possibly tell? Life is so strange, so fickle! How little is needed to make or break us!

206 One Sunday, needing a break from her heavy working week, she went out for a stroll on
207 the Champs-Élysées². Suddenly she caught sight of a woman pushing a child in a pram. It
208 was Madame Forestier, still young, still beautiful, and still attractive.

209 Madame Loisel felt apprehensive. Should she speak to her? Yes, why not? Now that she
210 had paid in full, she would tell her everything. Why not? She went up to her.

211 ‘Hello, Jeanne.’

212 The friend did not recognize her and was taken aback at being addressed so familiarly
213 by a common woman in the street. She stammered: ‘But … I’m sorry … I don’t know …
214 There’s some mistake.’

215 ‘No mistake. I’m Mathilde Loisel.’

216 Her friend gave a cry: ‘But my poor Mathilde, how you’ve changed!’

217 ‘Yes, I’ve been through some hard times since I saw you, very hard times. And it was all on
218 your account.’

219 ‘On my account? Whatever do you mean?’

220 ‘Do you remember that diamond necklace you lent me to go to the reception at the
221 Ministry?’

222 ‘Yes. What about it?’

223 ‘Well I lost it.’

224 ‘Lost it? But you returned it to me.’

225 ‘No, I returned another one just like it. And we’ve been paying for it these past ten years.
226 You know, it wasn’t easy for us. We had nothing. … But it’s over and done with now, and
227 I’m glad.’

228 Madame Forestier stopped. ‘You mean you bought a diamond necklace to replace mine?’

229 ‘Yes. And you never noticed the difference, did you? They were exactly alike.’ And she
230 smiled a proud, innocent smile.

231 Madame Forestier looked very upset and, taking both her hands in hers, said:

232 ‘Oh, my poor Mathilde! But it was only an imitation necklace. It couldn’t have been worth
233 much more than five hundred francs! …’
