## Text One (Unseen)
**Title:** Tin Bath Tournament
**Author:** Ben Fogle
**Context:** In this extract, the writer, Ben Fogle, describes taking part in the World Tin Bath Championship on the Isle of Man. Printed in the Source Booklet, adapted from The Accidental Adventurer, Ben Fogle, Penguin Random House 2012.

1  The sky was a palette of greys that melted into the gun‑metal ocean. Seagulls circled
2  overhead, their cawing drowned out by the cacophony of clinking halyards¹ against
3  yacht masts.

4  The sun burst through a small gap in the clouds, momentarily illuminating the water.
5  A flag snapped in the brisk breeze. The cobblestones glistened from a recent downpour,
6  sparkling against the grey‑and‑white world around.

7  A medieval castle loomed up from the waters, a reminder of more turbulent times, while
8  a flotilla of dinghies and rowing boats, tethered to the harbour wall like loyal dogs,
9  waited to ferry their owners to their boats and ships. A small fleet of fishing boats was
10 unloading its catch.

11 It was early in the morning, but already the small harbour was a hive of activity, as
12 hundreds of people started to crowd against the harbour wall. Bright yellow rainhats and
13 red waterproof jackets added a splash of colour to the dank scene.

14 Nervously, I made my way to the water’s edge. The tide was low and the waves lapped
15 against the concrete ramp. Gingerly I slid my feet along the slimy surface to avoid
16 slipping on the coating of green algae. I used the super‑glued barnacles as footholds as I
17 made my way down, clenching my teeth tightly as my bare toes kissed the frigid waters.
18 Clutching my paddle, I gently lowered myself into the small vessel and wedged myself in
19 with my knees against the gunwales². My legs were shaking with the cold as I edged out
20 into the chilly Irish Sea.

21 The crowd had grown, as thousands descended on the small harbour for this sporting
22 spectacular. Here I was, just back from rowing the Atlantic Ocean with James Cracknell,
23 the Olympic medallist, and by association I had become the hot favourite to win. I think
24 they had mistaken me for a top oarsman and I distinctly heard chants of ‘Ben, Ben,
25 Ben … ‘. Gingerly I paddled my way to the start line. My heart was pounding. A news
26 helicopter circled above, while film crews and photographers bobbed around in
27 small boats.

28 Several dozen competitors were lined up, muscles flexed in tight vests, steely
29 determination glinting in eyes. Next to me a tall, lean man practised his stroke.

30 I was competing against some of the best. Many had spent years training and planning
31 for this unique event. As always, I just seemed to have turned up at the last moment.
32 I stared straight ahead.

33 The claxon sounded. The start of a race is like the opening salvo³ of battle. It is the
34 moment friends become foes. All focus is on winning at all costs. The noise of clinking
35 halyards and the roar of the crowd was drowned out by the silence of my focus; it is like
36 being submerged under water: noises are muffled as the adrenalin floods through the
37 body and fills the ears.

38 I speared my paddle into the grey surf, scooped at the water furiously. Left, right, left,
39 right, left, right … I was mesmerised by the rhythm. The water turned white as dozens of
40 paddles whipped it into a cappuccino froth of bubbles.

41 I was neck and neck with the leader of the pack and making good headway, but then
42 I seemed to start slowing down. Something was very wrong. With each scoop of the
43 paddle, water rushed over the gunwales and into my vessel. I pulled more carefully
44 at the paddle, but still great waves of water overwhelmed my craft and the bow was
45 dangerously close to the waterline. I leaned back to counterbalance, but now the sea
46 was pouring into the stern. Water lapped around my ankles, then my knees. I scooped it
47 with my hands and tried to bail as fast as I could. It was no good. Before I knew it, I began
48 to tumble backwards, my knees lost their grip against the side and my hands were
49 thrown into the air as my vessel and I flipped upside down. I felt a rush of salty water up
50 my nostrils as I tumbled into the icy Irish Sea. I could hear the crowd and their gales of
51 laughter over the water as I clung desperately to my vessel to stop it sinking.

52 I was in the middle of Peel Harbour on the Isle of Man in the annual World Tin Bath
53 Championship. I was racing against firemen and farmers, doctors and bankers, all
54 competing in their ‘pimped‑up’ tin baths. My ‘pimping’ had involved a lick of green paint,
55 which was already starting to chip and peel. My craft, like me, was starting to fall apart
56 and somehow I’d managed to sink, ten seconds into the race.

57 I hauled the bath to the harbour wall and clung on to the metal rung of a ladder.
58 I emptied out the water and gently lowered myself back into the tiny vessel. I have a
59 mental discipline at times like this which makes me talk to myself. ‘Focus, Ben,’ I berated
60 myself as I tried to catch up. Left, right, left, right.

61 ‘Come on, Ben, you rowed the Atlantic!’ hollered a local. But something was wrong. Once
62 again I was scooping too much water into the bath, and before I knew it I was in the
63 water again. There was another roar of laughter.

64 It is moments like this that plunge me helplessly back to my childhood and my constant
65 failure to achieve anything. I hauled myself back to the harbour wall. I could hear the
66 chanting of the crowd. My cheeks were flushed with embarrassment as once again I
67 clambered aboard the tiny bath. If there’s one thing I have learned over the years it is that
68 stubbornness is a very powerful trait. It has served me well.

69 Once again I dipped my paddle into the water. Far ahead of me, most of the baths had
70 finished. I was a child again, paddling alone. It was only a few hundred metres but it felt
71 like an ocean. I limped home, the last over the line. Humiliated and defeated.

72 I dragged my tin bath up the slipway to more laughter from the crowd. A wave of nausea
73 overwhelmed me as I staggered forwards. The world began to spin and my vision
74 narrowed to a pinhole before I passed out.

75 ‘Ben? Ben?’ It seemed as if people were still chanting my name, only this time it was
76 two concerned‑looking paramedics. I had come to in an ambulance, confused and
77 disorientated. I was swaddled in a foil blanket and hooked up to a heart‑rate monitor.
78 I had collapsed from hypothermia⁴. That could have been a perfect excuse for coming last
79 in the race, except for the fact that it was the middle of summer, children were paddling
80 in the same water and I was very publicly about to head to Antarctica to take part in the
81 first race to the South Pole since Scott and Amundsen a hundred years before …

**Glossary:**
- ¹ halyards: ropes used for raising and lowering sails
- ² gunwales: the top edge of a boat
- ³ salvo: simultaneous firing of guns
- ⁴ hypothermia: where body temperature gets dangerously low

## Text Two (Anthology)
**Title:** From A Game of Polo with a Headless Goat
**Author:** Emma Levine
**Context:** In this extract, the writer, Emma Levine, writes about a donkey race which she observed in Karachi, Pakistan. Printed in the Source Booklet, adapted from A Game of Polo with a Headless Goat by Emma Levine, published by Andre Deutsch.

1  We drove off to find the best viewing spot, which turned out to be the crest of the hill so
2  we could see the approaching race. I asked the lads if we could join in the ‘Wacky Races’
3  and follow the donkeys, and they loved the idea. ‘We’ll open the car boot, you climb
4  inside and point your camera towards the race. As the donkeys overtake us, we’ll join the
5  cars.’ ‘But will you try and get to the front?’ ‘Oh yes, that’s no problem.’

6  The two lads who had never been interested in this Karachi sport were suddenly fired up
7  with enthusiasm. We waited for eternity on the brow of the hill, me perched in the boot
8  with a zoom lens pointing out. Nearly one hour later I was beginning to feel rather silly
9  when the only action was a villager on a wobbly bicycle, who nearly fell off as he cycled
10 past and gazed around at us.

11 Several vehicles went past, and some donkey‑carts carrying spectators. ‘Are they coming?’
12 we called out to them. ‘Coming, coming,’ came the reply. I was beginning to lose faith in
13 its happening, but the lads remained confident.

14 Just as I was assuming that the race had been cancelled, we spotted two approaching
15 donkey‑carts in front of a cloud of fumes and dust created by some fifty vehicles roaring
16 up in their wake. As they drew nearer, Yaqoob revved up the engine and began to inch
17 the car out of the lay‑by. The two donkeys were almost dwarfed by their entourage¹; but
18 there was no denying their speed – the Kibla donkey is said to achieve speeds of up to
19 40 kph, and this looked close. The two were neck‑and‑neck, their jockeys perched on top
20 of the tiny carts using their whips energetically, although not cruelly.

21 The noise of the approaching vehicles grew; horns tooting, bells ringing, and the special
22 rattles used just for this purpose (like maracas, a metal container filled with dried beans).
23 Men standing on top of their cars and vans, hanging out of taxis and perched on lorries,
24 all cheered and shouted, while the vehicles jostled to get to the front of the convoy.

25 Yaqoob chose exactly the right moment to edge out of the road and swerve in front of
26 the nearest car, finding the perfect place to see the two donkeys and at the front of the
27 vehicles. This was Formula One without rules, or a city‑centre rush hour gone anarchic; a
28 complete flouting of every type of traffic rule and common sense.

29 Our young driver relished this unusual test of driving skills. It was survival of the fittest,
30 and depended upon the ability to cut in front of a vehicle with a sharp flick of the
31 steering wheel (no lane discipline here); quick reflexes to spot a gap in the traffic for a
32 couple of seconds; nerves of steel, and an effective horn. There were two races – the
33 motorized spectators at the back; in front, the two donkeys, still running close and
34 amazingly not put off by the uproar just behind them. Ahead of the donkeys, oncoming
35 traffic – for it was a main road – had to dive into the ditch and wait there until we had
36 passed. Yaqoob loved it. We stayed near to the front, his hand permanently on the horn
37 and his language growing more colourful with every vehicle that tried to cut in front. …

38 The road straightened and levelled, and everyone picked up speed as we neared the end
39 of the race. But just as they were reaching the finishing line, the hospital gate, there was a
40 near pile‑up as the leading donkey swerved, lost his footing and he and the cart tumbled
41 over. The race was over.

42 And then the trouble began. I assumed the winner was the one who completed the race
43 but it was not seen that way by everyone. Apart from the two jockeys and ‘officials’ (who,
44 it turned out, were actually monitoring the race) there were over a hundred punters
45 who had all staked money on the race, and therefore had strong opinions. Some were
46 claiming that the donkey had fallen because the other one had been ridden too close
47 to him. Voices were raised, fists were out and tempers rising. Everyone gathered around
48 one jockey and official, while the bookmakers were trying to insist that the race should
49 be re‑run.

50 Yaqoob and Iqbal were nervous of hanging around a volatile situation. They agreed to
51 find out for me what was happening, ordering me to stay inside the car as they were
52 swallowed up by the crowd. They emerged some time later. ‘It’s still not resolved,’ said
53 Iqbal, ‘but it’s starting to get nasty. I think we should leave.’ As we drove away, Yaqoob
54 reflected on his driving skills. ‘I really enjoyed that,’ he said as we drove off at a more
55 sedate pace. ‘But I don’t even have my licence yet because I’m underage!’

56 They both found this hilarious, but I was glad he hadn’t told me before; an inexperienced,
57 underage driver causing a massive pile‑up in the middle of the high‑stakes donkey race
58 could have caused problems.

**Glossary:**
- ¹ entourage: a group of people attending or surrounding a person
