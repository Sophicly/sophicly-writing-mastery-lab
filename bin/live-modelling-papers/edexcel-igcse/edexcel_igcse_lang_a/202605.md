Ralston portrays himself as experienced with rock-climbing, making his later incident extremely shocking. He starts in media res saying "I come to another drop-off", immediately throwing the readers into the action and establishing his experience through the use of "another". He has clearly been doing this for a while. By using complex terminology such as "refrigerator chockstone" and "stemming or chimneying", Ralston reveals the extent of his knowledge about rock-climbing; this is not just a hobby, he knows almost everything about it. Ralston wants the readers to understand that his accident was not caused by a lack of knowledge or experience and he exposes the severity of the situation as even someone who has done rock-climbing many times previously has been caught in such a situation. He reveals how he has even been in situations similar to this one as he describes how he "kick[s] at the boulder to test how stuck it is", emphasising how he has been in such situations before. The verb "test" elevates him to an almost teacher-like figure examining the boulder to test its strength, reinforcing the extremity of the situation he is in as he has clearly been in situations similar before.

Ralston conveys the brutality of the incident, revealing his agony. The boulder "ensnares my right arm", portraying a grotesque image in the readers' mind. Through the use of the verb "ensnare", Ralston likens the rock to a predatory figure whilst he is the innocent prey. By using present tense, he allows the reader to feel as though they are experiencing the accident with him, intensifying their shock and allowing them to envision the brutality of it. He describes how the "next three seconds play out at a tenth of their normal speed", highlighting the intensity of the situation as the actions seem to play out in slow-motion, almost like a movie scene. By referencing time, Ralston highlights his vulnerability and lack of control; he is no longer able to do anything. He says "good God, my hand" with the use of the dental sound 'd' revealing his frustrations and by calling out to God, it is almost like a plea, a symbol of desperation. He is fully out of control and must now resort to the higher powers to help him. His shock is conveyed when he "stare[s] at the sight of my arm", emphasising the disbelief as he is stuck in a painful and agonising situation that he never would have expected to happen to someone as experienced as him.

Yet Ralston still perseveres, attempting to save himself and his arm from being damaged any further. He yells to himself "Get yourself out of there!" conveying his desperation. Through the use of the imperatives and exclamation, it is almost as if Ralston is condemning himself, like a parent telling off their child. He may be beginning to blame himself for getting into this situation, highlighting how he is so helpless that he now tries to find something to blame. He attempts to remove the boulder by "pushing with my left hand", emphasising his desperate attempts. Despite being stuck with no hope, he still uses all his strength and "adrenaline" to get himself free. He even gets "good leverage", showing how he has tried everything possible and reinforcing his experience as he tries to do what he knows will work best. However all his efforts are extremely futile with the extract ending with "nothing." The shift from long sentences to one short, blunt sentence reflects his full loss of hope and encapsulates his helplessness. This ending is extremely poignant and serves as a reminder that even the most experienced of people can fail in what they love and know so much about.

## Text Two (Anthology)
**Title:** 127 Hours: Between a Rock and a Hard Place
**Author:** Aron Ralston (2004)
**Context:** 21st Century non-fiction. In this first-hand account, Ralston describes how a boulder crushed his right hand while he was climbing and hiking alone in a canyon in Utah. He had not informed anyone of his hiking plans.

1  I come to another drop-off. This one is maybe eleven or twelve feet high, a foot higher and
2  of a different geometry than the overhang I descended ten minutes ago. Another
3  refrigerator-sized chockstone is wedged between the walls, ten feet downstream from and
4  at the same height as the ledge. It gives the space below the drop-off the claustrophobic
5  feel of a short tunnel. Instead of the walls widening after the drop-off, or opening into a
6  bowl at the bottom of the canyon, here the slot narrows to a consistent three feet across at
7  the lip of the drop-off and continues at that width for fifty feet down the canyon.
8  Sometimes in narrow passages like this one, it's possible for me to stem my body across
9  the slot, with my feet and back pushing out in opposite directions against the walls.
10 Controlling this counterpressure by switching my hands and feet on the opposing walls, I
11 can move up or down the shoulder-width crevice fairly easily as long as the friction contact
12 stays solid between the walls and my hands, feet, and back. This technique is known as
13 stemming or chimneying; you can imagine using it to climb up the inside of a chimney.
14 Just below the ledge where I'm standing is a chockstone the size of a large bus tire, stuck
15 fast in the channel between the walls, a few feet out from the lip. If I can step onto it, then
16 I'll have a nine-foot height to descend, less than that of the first overhang. I'll dangle off
17 the chockstone, then take a short fall onto the rounded rocks piled on the canyon floor.
18 Stemming across the canyon at the lip of the drop-off, with one foot and one hand on each
19 of the walls, I traverse out to the chockstone. I press my back against the south wall and
20 lock my left knee, which pushes my foot tight against the north wall. With my right foot, I
21 kick at the boulder to test how stuck it is. It's jammed tightly enough to hold my weight. I
22 lower myself from the chimneying position and step onto the chockstone. It supports me
23 but teeters slightly. After confirming that I don't want to chimney down from the
24 chockstone's height, I squat and grip the rear of the lodged boulder, turning to face back
25 upcanyon. Sliding my belly over the front edge, I can lower myself and hang from my fully
26 extended arms, akin to climbing down from the roof of a house.
27 As I dangle, I feel the stone respond to my adjusting grip with a scraping quake as my
28 body's weight applies enough torque to disturb it from its position. Instantly, I know this is
29 trouble, and instinctively, I let go of the rotating boulder to land on the round rocks below.
30 When I look up, the backlit chockstone falling toward my head consumes the sky. Fear
31 shoots my hands over my head. I can't move backward or I'll fall over a small ledge. My
32 only hope is to push off the falling rock and get my head out of its way.
33 The next three seconds play out at a tenth of their normal speed. Time dilates, as if I'm
34 dreaming, and my reactions decelerate. In slow motion: the rock smashes my left hand
35 against the south wall; my eyes register the collision, and I yank my left arm back as the
36 rock ricochets; the boulder then crushes my right hand and ensnares my right arm at the
37 wrist, palm in, thumb up, fingers extended; the rock slides another foot down the wall with
38 my arm in tow, tearing the skin off the lateral side of my forearm. Then silence.
39 My disbelief paralyzes me temporarily as I stare at the sight of my arm vanishing into an
40 implausibly small gap between the fallen boulder and the canyon wall. Within moments, my
41 nervous system's pain response overcomes the initial shock. Good God, my hand. The
42 flaring agony throws me into a panic. I grimace and growl. My mind commands my body,
43 'Get your hand out of there!' I yank my arm three times in a naive attempt to pull it out.
44 But I'm stuck.
45 Anxiety has my brain tweaking; searing-hot pain shoots from my wrist up my arm. I'm
46 frantic, and I cry out. My desperate brain conjures up a probably apocryphal story in which
47 an adrenaline-stoked mom lifts an overturned car to free her baby. I'd give it even odds
48 that it's made up, but I do know for certain that right now, while my body's chemicals are
49 raging at full flood, is the best chance I'll have to free myself with brute force. I shove
50 against the large boulder, heaving against it, pushing with my left hand, lifting with my
51 knees pressed under the rock. I get good leverage with the aid of a twelve-inch shelf in
52 front of my feet. Standing on that, I brace my thighs under the boulder and thrust upward
53 repeatedly, grunting, 'Come on... move!' Nothing.
