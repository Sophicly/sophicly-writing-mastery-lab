## Text One (Unseen)
**Title:** War Doctor
**Author:** David Nott
**Context:** In this extract, the writer, David Nott, describes his experiences as a doctor in Gaza during a time of war. Printed in the Source Booklet, adapted from War Doctor, David Nott, Picador. Image © atbaei/Getty Images.

1  I have travelled the world in search of trouble. It is a kind of addiction, a pull I find hard
2  to resist.

3  It stems partly from the desire to use my knowledge as a surgeon to help people and
4  partly from the thrill of just being in those terrible places, of living in a zone where most
5  people have neither been nor want to go.

6  In July 2014, the Israel-Gaza war began. It was a dire situation for the civilians on both
7  sides. I wanted to be immersed in what was going on. A week after the war started, my
8  mobile rang. It was the International Committee of the Red Cross: would I go to Gaza
9  City? It was a no-brainer. That same day, I was on a plane to Tel Aviv.

10 I was embedded in the Shifa hospital in Gaza City, and quickly got down to work. It
11 was pure, hardcore war-trauma surgery, mostly dealing with the effects of bomb-blast
12 injuries. There was a mass-casualty event most days, and it became quite normal to
13 receive something like 60 or 70 patients.

14 One night I was woken at about one in the morning when my bed bounced off the floor.
15 There had been an enormous explosion only metres away. The hospital the next morning
16 was chaos.

17 As I walked around assessing the injuries, I came across a girl who looked about seven
18 years old. She was lying on her own in the corner. She looked grey, and, to be honest, I
19 thought she was dead. I checked her vital signs: her airway was clear but her breathing
20 was very shallow. She had a wound to her left arm, which was bandaged, but that wasn’t
21 all: her small bowel was hanging outside her body. This little girl was dying, and needed
22 to go to theatre immediately.

23 The Red Cross anaesthetist was an excellent Italian fellow called Mauro Torre. He and I
24 pushed the girl into the operating theatre ourselves and got her ready. I watched her
25 being put to sleep and then went to scrub up.

26 Suddenly, the door of the operating theatre flew open. It was the hospital security
27 manager.

28 ‘We’ve got intel that says the hospital’s going to be attacked by shelling in five minutes.
29 Everyone out.’

30 Everybody else in the room began making a beeline for the door, joining the rest of the
31 staff heading out of the hospital as fast as they could.

32 By this time, the little girl was asleep, and on the ventilator. She was a pitiful sight. The
33 time she had left to live could be counted in minutes, not hours, whether there was an
34 airstrike or not.

35 Lots of things went through my mind. The most burning thought was that I could not
36 leave this little girl to die on her own. She was an innocent child and did not deserve
37 such a fate. I had thought I might die during the shell attack on the safe house the
38 previous evening. But maybe this was the moment it ended. And if it was, did I need to
39 save myself? The answer, of course, was no.

40 I was on my own in the world, with no parents, no siblings, no wife, no children. I made a
41 conscious decision to stay.

42 I turned to Mauro and said, ‘You can go, you don’t need to stay.’

43 ‘No, David, I will stay with you.’

44 Our eyes locked. It was a look that conveyed so much: part fear, part trepidation; a
45 mixture of regret, respect and farewell.

46 He, too, was a veteran of many missions, also unmarried with no dependants. I suppose
47 we had both been thinking the same thing.

48 So we stayed with the little girl, waiting for the bomb to drop or the missile to strike. I
49 calmly prepared the girl’s abdomen with iodine, picked up the green drapes and clipped
50 them into position. There was no rush; we took our time.

51 The familiar process took my mind off the situation we were in, and by the time I’d
52 removed her spleen Mauro told me twenty minutes had passed. Still no attack. There was
53 nothing to do but carry on. We completed the operation, repairing the holes in her small
54 bowel, and making her abdomen look normal again. I then turned my attention to her
55 left arm.

56 Two hours later, the theatre was still deserted. We decided to wake her up. As we were
57 doing this, people started to drift back in, amazed to see us still there. Clearly, there was
58 to be no attack.

59 I am not sure where the information had come from but I was told that it was a credible
60 source and that’s why everybody had panicked and left. I only knew that our little girl
61 was alive.

62 I went to see her every day after that, and got to know her family well. Her name is Aysha
63 and the photograph I have of me standing by her hospital bed, both of us smiling, says
64 it all.

65 Perhaps it had been irresponsible of Mauro and me to stay, but I felt in that moment that
66 the girl’s welfare took priority. It wasn’t a logical decision, it was based purely on emotion
67 – compassion for her, and anger at the forces of war ranged against her. I was so sick of
68 seeing badly injured children that I could not bear to see another one and stand idly by.
69 Staying with her was a pointless act of defiance against the warmongers, but it would
70 have been impossible to do otherwise. I was prepared to die, and I would rather have
71 died than lived with myself knowing I’d left her alone.

## Text Two (Anthology)
**Title:** From A Passage to Africa
**Author:** George Alagiah
**Context:** In this extract, the writer, George Alagiah, describes his thoughts about what he witnessed as a television reporter in war-torn Somalia. Printed in the Source Booklet, adapted from A Passage to Africa, George Alagiah.

1  I saw a thousand hungry, lean, scared and betrayed faces as I criss-crossed Somalia
2  between the end of 1991 and December 1992, but there is one I will never forget.

3  I was in a little hamlet just outside Gufgaduud, a village in the back of beyond, a place
4  the aid agencies had yet to reach. In my notebook I had jotted down instructions on how
5  to get there. ‘Take the Badale Road for a few kilometres till the end of the tarmac, turn
6  right on to a dirt track, stay on it for about forty-five minutes — Gufgaduud. Go another
7  fifteen minutes approx. — like a ghost village.’ …

8  In the ghoulish manner of journalists on the hunt for the most striking pictures, my
9  cameraman … and I tramped from one hut to another. What might have appalled us when
10 we’d started our trip just a few days before no longer impressed us much. The search for
11 the shocking is like the craving for a drug: you require heavier and more frequent doses
12 the longer you’re at it. Pictures that stun the editors one day are written off as the same old
13 stuff the next. This sounds callous, but it is just a fact of life. It’s how we collect and compile
14 the images that so move people in the comfort of their sitting rooms back home.

15 There was Amina Abdirahman, who had gone out that morning in search of wild, edible
16 roots, leaving her two young girls lying on the dirt floor of their hut. They had been sick
17 for days, and were reaching the final, enervating stages of terminal hunger. Habiba was
18 ten years old and her sister, Ayaan, was nine. By the time Amina returned, she had only
19 one daughter. Habiba had died. No rage, no whimpering, just a passing away — that
20 simple, frictionless, motionless deliverance from a state of half-life to death itself. It was,
21 as I said at the time in my dispatch, a vision of ‘famine away from the headlines, a famine
22 of quiet suffering and lonely death’.

23 There was the old woman who lay in her hut, abandoned by relations who were too
24 weak to carry her on their journey to find food. It was the smell that drew me to her
25 doorway: the smell of decaying flesh. Where her shinbone should have been there was a
26 festering wound the size of my hand. She’d been shot in the leg as the retreating army of
27 the deposed dictator … took revenge on whoever it found in its way. The shattered leg
28 had fused into the gentle V-shape of a boomerang. It was rotting; she was rotting. You
29 could see it in her sick, yellow eyes and smell it in the putrid air she recycled with every
30 struggling breath she took.

31 And then there was the face I will never forget.

32 My reaction to everyone else I met that day was a mixture of pity and revulsion¹. Yes,
33 revulsion. The degeneration of the human body, sucked of its natural vitality by the twin evils
34 of hunger and disease, is a disgusting thing. We never say so in our TV reports. It’s a taboo
35 that has yet to be breached. To be in a feeding centre is to hear and smell the excretion of
36 fluids by people who are beyond controlling their bodily functions. To be in a feeding centre
37 is surreptitiously² to wipe your hands on the back of your trousers after you’ve held the
38 clammy palm of a mother who has just cleaned vomit from her child’s mouth.

39 There’s pity, too, because even in this state of utter despair they aspire to a dignity that is
40 almost impossible to achieve. An old woman will cover her shrivelled body with a soiled
41 cloth as your gaze turns towards her. Or the old and dying man who keeps his hoe next
42 to the mat with which, one day soon, they will shroud his corpse, as if he means to go out
43 and till the soil once all this is over.

44 I saw that face for only a few seconds, a fleeting meeting of eyes before the face turned
45 away, as its owner retreated into the darkness of another hut. In those brief moments
46 there had been a smile, not from me, but from the face. It was not a smile of greeting, it
47 was not a smile of joy — how could it be? — but it was a smile nonetheless. It touched
48 me in a way I could not explain. It moved me in a way that went beyond pity or revulsion.

49 What was it about that smile? I had to find out. I urged my translator to ask the man
50 why he had smiled. He came back with an answer. ‘It’s just that he was embarrassed to
51 be found in this condition,’ the translator explained. And then it clicked. That’s what the
52 smile had been about. It was the feeble smile that goes with apology, the kind of smile
53 you might give if you felt you had done something wrong.

54 Normally inured³ to stories of suffering, accustomed to the evidence of deprivation, I
55 was unsettled by this one smile in a way I had never been before. There is an unwritten
56 code between the journalist and his subjects in these situations. The journalist observes,
57 the subject is observed. The journalist is active, the subject is passive. But this smile had
58 turned the tables on that tacit agreement. Without uttering a single word, the man had
59 posed a question that cut to the heart of the relationship between me and him, between
60 us and them, between the rich world and the poor world. If he was embarrassed to
61 be found weakened by hunger and ground down by conflict, how should I feel to be
62 standing there so strong and confident?

63 I resolved there and then that I would write the story of Gufgaduud with all the power
64 and purpose I could muster. It seemed at the time, and still does, the only adequate
65 answer a reporter can give to the man’s question.

66 I have one regret about that brief encounter in Gufgaduud. Having searched through my
67 notes and studied the dispatch that the BBC broadcast, I see that I never found out what
68 the man’s name was. Yet meeting him was a seminal moment in the gradual collection of
69 experiences we call context. Facts and figures are the easy part of journalism. Knowing
70 where they sit in the great scheme of things is much harder. So, my nameless friend, if
71 you are still alive, I owe you one.

**Glossary:**
- ¹ revulsion: disgust
- ² surreptitiously: secretly
- ³ inured: hardened
