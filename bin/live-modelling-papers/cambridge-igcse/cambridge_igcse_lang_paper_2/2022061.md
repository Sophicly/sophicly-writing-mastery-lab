## Text A
**Title:** The joys of bilingualism
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The following text, written by a parent, is about being able to speak more than one language.

1  Everyone knows that it’s intriguing to watch your children change over the years. But to hear
2  them alter their language, over the course of a few weeks, is almost surreal. Until we moved to
3  their mother’s country, Italy, my children had always lived in England and their English was what
4  you would expect: fluent and full of pre-teen playground slang.

5  What’s breathtaking since we moved isn’t just their language acquisition, it’s the way their
6  personalities subtly shift. Our eldest daughter, abrupt at the best of times, is strangely sweet
7  in Italian; the middle child, our funny girl in English, is precise and serious; and the youngest is
8  even more boisterous in Italian.

9  I, too, feel altered. If you’re a writer, and words are your currency, hopefully you’re eloquent. But
10 when you live in a language not your own, however fluent you are, your accent is ever obvious
11 and you lack the nuances. Humour is hard. In a language as melodious as Italian, you will always
12 be blunt. The children, though, will hopefully become what I can never be: truly bilingual.

13 Until recently, bilingualism was considered damaging to a child’s development. If, for example,
14 immigrants maintained a mother tongue at home, it would impede integration at school and
15 probably lead to academic regression. These ideas changed with growing research on the brains
16 and behaviour of bilingual children leading some to believe that bilingualism may offer some
17 advantages.

18 Research also suggests that being bilingual increases your ability to empathise, to see a situation
19 from another’s perspective and there’s some evidence that bilingual people develop dementia
20 later than monolinguals. A flexibility of mind makes multi-tasking less problematic for bilinguals,
21 apparently.

22 It would be very easy to become smug, but there are clear drawbacks as well as advantages.
23 The verbal aptitude of bilinguals in each language is generally less than that of monolingual
24 speakers; the vocabulary size is smaller and there is a slower response time for understanding
25 and producing words.

26 It’s as if some bilinguals can speak, say, 95 per cent of two languages, rather than a 100 per cent
27 of one. If children are to be articulate and sophisticated language users, that missing 5 per cent
28 is a major hole, especially if you want them fully to appreciate the culture of both. And people
29 lose old languages, or don’t gain new ones, for reasons that have nothing to do with a careful
30 measurement of potential benefits.

31 In another language, you don’t just learn new words, or sounds, but new notions. It’s like putting
32 on different spectacles and seeing the world with different eyes. You gain a different perspective
33 and sometimes, if you’re lucky, you become more, rather than less, eloquent.

## Text B
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The following text is about being bilingual.

1  Being able to switch between two or more languages in conversation is pretty cool. This week a
2  study from the University of Washington found that bilingual babies will have developed ‘better’
3  brains before they even utter a first word. But it’s not all brain development and top grades.

4  It’s true people will find you cultured, worldly and intelligent, even though you probably didn’t have
5  a choice about learning an extra language. You either picked it up as a baby or you were forced
6  to learn it when you moved to another country. But bilingual people are rarely equally proficient in
7  both languages and it’s easy to make mistakes in one language or the other. It takes real effort
8  to keep both if you don’t use them equally. Many people lose one of their languages over time.

9  An extra language makes you more marketable for employers since the 21st century world is a
10 globalised economy. If employees can speak another language they can interact with more of the
11 world, provided they have the range of vocabulary and fluency for a professional setting. Some
12 bilinguals have been educated in a language they don’t speak outside of school or college. They
13 might be fluent there but not so much in the big, wide world.

14 Your first language can keep you in touch with your roots wherever you go, through books or
15 through music. Although I moved to the UK when I was 10 and absorbed much of the surrounding
16 culture, I’ll never forget where I’m from since my mother frequently likes to remind me of proverbs
17 that really don’t make much sense in English.

18 Apparently, those who speak different languages can experience personality changes when
19 switching from one to another. That explains why my friends say I sound angry when I’m on the
20 phone to my parents!

## Q1
**Marks:** 40
**AOs:** AO1, AO2, AO3

Read Text A and Text B in the insert and then answer Section A, Question 1 on this question paper.

A relative is considering moving to another country where they and their teenage children will have to learn a new language. Write a letter to your relative giving your views about whether or not this is a good idea. In your letter you should:

• evaluate the ideas, opinions and attitudes in both texts

• explain how successful you think the move will be for the adults and their children.

Base your letter on what you have read in both texts, but be careful to use your own words. Address both of the bullet points. Write about 250 to 350 words. Up to 15 marks are available for the content of your answer, and up to 25 marks for the quality of your writing. **[40]**
