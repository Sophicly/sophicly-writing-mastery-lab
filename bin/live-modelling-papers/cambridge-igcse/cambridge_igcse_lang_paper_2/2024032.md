## Text A
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The following passage is part of a magazine article about overthinking.

1  It’s not at all uncommon for my friends to tell me, ‘Don’t overthink it, just go with it.’

2  ‘Just going with it’ is not something I do. I must really understand what I’m doing and then I think
3  through almost every possibility and eventuality. And I plan. When people say things like: ‘Who
4  could have imagined that would happen?’ about some entirely predictable outcome, my most
5  common response is ‘I could’. I have realised that for most people I am an overthinker, but for
6  me, it is others who underthink – I just think.

7  I’ve always been a natural observer, able to take the emotional temperature of a room, able
8  to watch people’s micro-movements, listen to their language, their tone. Sometimes, today, my
9  children think I’m a mind reader, but of course I’m not. I’ve just noticed what’s been said, what’s
10 gone on, and I’ve overthought what they might do or say. So sometimes I answer a question
11 before they ask it and they think I have a superpower.

12 Straight out of school, I joined the military, where you had to think not once but several times
13 about the simplest task because everything was a potential trap or some otherwise-overlooked
14 detail might later become vital. Check everything, always. During one lecture, someone came in
15 to give a message to the lecturer and afterwards we were told to describe that person who had
16 just flitted in while we were concentrating on something else.

17 Of course, overthinking can be exhausting. I must be careful to have boundaries and to give
18 myself time off because burnout is never far away; I also have to be careful not to end up doing
19 people’s thinking or memory storage for them. Because, like all emotions and ways of working,
20 there’s a plus and a minus side. Other people get used to you doing all their planning and thinking
21 for them.

22 On the whole, I love being an overthinker. It’s enormously enriching and brings me a very
23 rewarding mental world. I go with it now rather than fight it. Examining every aspect of everything
24 can bring with it the most amazing insights.

## Text B
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The following passage is an extract from an article.

1  Thinking about something in endless circles is exhausting. While everyone overthinks
2  occasionally, chronic overthinkers spend most of their waking time ruminating, putting pressure
3  on themselves, making them less able to change.

4  Overthinking can take many forms, but endlessly deliberating when making a decision and then
5  questioning that decision is common. While hours might be spent deciding, the actual decision
6  is often never made. ‘People confuse overthinking with problem-solving,’ says one psychologist.
7  ‘But what ends up happening is we just sort of go in a loop. We’re not really solving the problem.’

8  In attempting to read what’s in other people’s minds, overthinkers can create difficulties for
9  themselves and others. People who overthink consistently run commentaries in their heads about
10 what they say and do, and fret anxiously about how others think of them. They find it difficult to
11 make an objective choice or arrange things in the best way because they constantly worry about
12 how their actions will be perceived. Straightforward communication proves impossible because
13 overthinkers are consumed by self-doubt. ‘What ifs’ and ‘shoulds’ dominate their thinking, as if
14 an invisible jury is sitting in judgement on their lives. Trying to predict the future by reading into
15 the smallest details to avoid potentially bad outcomes can debilitate them. Overthinking can keep
16 you stuck in the past, unable to make important decisions and pre-occupied with trying to avoid
17 annoying others.

18 Over time, most people learn to harness and control their overthinking habit. They might always
19 plan and even worry, but they gradually realise that it could be an advantage. Getting to that
20 point, however, can be a difficult journey.

You and a friend have been asked to work together to organise an event in school or college. Your friend is an overthinker. Write a letter to your friend, suggesting how you could work successfully together. In your letter you should:

• evaluate the ideas, attitudes and opinions given in the texts

• consider some of the ways that each of you could help to make the event a success.

Base your letter on what you have read in both texts, but be careful to use your own words. Address both of the bullet points. Write about 250 to 350 words. Up to 15 marks are available for the content of your answer, and up to 25 marks for the quality of your writing. **[40]**
