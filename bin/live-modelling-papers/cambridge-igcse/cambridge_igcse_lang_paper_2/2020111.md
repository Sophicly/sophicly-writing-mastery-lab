## Text A
**Title:** eSports in the Olympic games?
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** eSports is the short name for electronic sports, where players play video games against each other with fans watching them. This article discusses whether eSports should be included in the Olympic Games.

1  eSports currently enjoys enormous attention and funding. Last year, the global audience for
2  eSports grew by millions with global revenue for the eSports industry huge and still rising. In
3  many countries, audiences pack stadiums and concert venues to watch teams compete against
4  each other in a range of video game tournaments. People from around the world also watch
5  from the comfort of their homes thanks to entire channels dedicated to streaming live footage of
6  people playing video games.

7  Fans argue that eSports athletes are athletes in the real sense of the term – citing shooting,
8  archery and dressage as examples of Olympic sports where the skills and talents of individuals
9  are recognised. It takes dexterity, intelligence, reflexes and coolness under pressure to succeed
10 in gaming. eSports athletes are legitimate competitors who happen to wield a joystick. How’s that
11 so different from a pole vault?

12 Experts on sports law are cautious: ‘Many sports want to get Olympic recognition. It’s a public
13 demonstration to the world, and a surge of interest often follows. Although eSports is growing,
14 that still doesn’t necessarily make it widely accepted.’

15 eSports is young, its players and audiences are young. The dismissal of eSports, its sponsors
16 argue, is just another example of older people scoffing at what occupies young people as ‘fads’
17 or unimportant activities. eSports is an opportunity for young people to take an existing passion,
18 competitive gaming, and make something more of it, even earning a living from it – after all, this
19 is often given as a reason to celebrate and support sports in general.

20 eSports, it is claimed, is the real deal. But arguments around popularity and difficulty only go so
21 far. Becoming a prima ballerina is so difficult it’s virtually impossible: as a sporting endeavour it
22 is far harder than qualifying for the Olympics in any sport. Should dancing to Swan Lake be an
23 Olympic sport? No. This activity knows to stay in its own lane.

24 There’s a logistical problem too, one that threatens eSports as an Olympic event far more
25 than narrow-minded traditionalists like myself. The different games played in various eSports
26 competitions have definitive lifespans and age quickly. Games are frequently replaced by sequels
27 and upgraded versions. It is an impressive effort to call playing video games ‘eSports’ and hope
28 that saying it enough will convince people it is actually a sport. But, no. Moving a joystick simply
29 has no athleticism, physicality or exertion – things that make something a sport. Competitive
30 eating sounds good too, but no one is suggesting that features in the Olympics.

## Text B
**Title:** Resistance is futile: eSports is massive ... and growing
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** This text discusses the popularity of eSports.

1  Imagine a stadium full of screaming fans. Zoom in on the contestants, sweating from the intensity
2  of the competition. This is not football, basketball or boxing. It’s eSports, and the ‘athletes’ are
3  headset-wearing, energy-drink-guzzling video-gamers. Couldn’t happen? Think again.

4  More than 20 years after the first video game tournaments, top eSports tournaments now draw
5  audiences that rival the biggest traditional sporting events; popular midweek live streams routinely
6  attract more than 100 000 online viewers. Massive global brands have weighed in as sponsors.
7  Gaming is what every traditional sports league is desperate to become: young, global, digital and
8  increasingly diverse. eSports is here. It is real. It is growing – and we have the numbers to prove
9  it.

10 So how big is this gaming thing? Let us start with this: according to market research so many
11 millions of people watched or played eSports that if the eSports nation were actually a real
12 nation, it would be the fifth largest in the world. eSports has long been biggest in Asia. Now North
13 America and Europe also claim tens of millions of eSports fans, with numbers growing by 21 per
14 cent a year.

15 The most popular video-streaming site boasts 55 million users and is arguably the most important
16 contributor to eSports’ recent growth. It offers streams of games and tournaments, and access to
17 gaming’s stars. It’s also where the next generation of would-be gamers post their own streams.

18 Apparently someone’s watching.

19 Although the gamer stereotype is young, single, male and living in some sort of basement, the
20 reality is that more than half of eSports fans are employed full time, 44 per cent are parents and
21 38 per cent are women. eSports fans do tend to be young. Just 28 per cent are over 35, a selling
22 point to advertisers looking to reach the next generation of consumers.

23 People watch this thing? Well, in a word, yes. Today, eSports’ biggest tournaments rival practically
24 any sporting event.
