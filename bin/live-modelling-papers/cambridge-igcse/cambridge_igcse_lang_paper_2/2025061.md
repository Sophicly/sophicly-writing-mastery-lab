# Topic 2025061: Cambridge IGCSE 0500 Paper 2 — June 2025 (variant 1) · Directed Writing (article) · Composition
**Type:** language_paper

**Teaching Point:** Cambridge IGCSE First Language English Paper 2 — Directed Writing and Composition, June 2025 (variant 1). A real Cambridge past paper. Section A Question 1 [40] = directed writing, 250–350 words, 15 reading + 25 writing; this paper’s form is the article. ## Text A
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The following is a blog post from a headteacher about voluntary work for older students.

1  I’m in my sixties now, but when I was in my last two years of schooling, still in my teens, my
2  school had a programme of voluntary work to be undertaken by older students. We were
3  assigned placements where we attended an afternoon each week, making ourselves useful for
4  a few hours in elderly people’s homes or local charities such as foodbanks, activity centres for
5  disabled people or programmes for disadvantaged children. It wasn’t popular among students
6  and there was much absenteeism, but the core values behind it were sound. I want to establish a
7  similar scheme, though organised and run with more rigour and commitment, in my own school.

8  Young people aren’t all entitled and technology-obsessed, but there’s a lot missing in their lives
9  these days. Their education is also viewed by some as narrow and focused on grades and
10 competition. There’s no room to teach compassion, an awareness of less fortunate people and
11 an ethos of service rather than self-fulfilment. I’m sure we can come up with a range of potential
12 placements here in our town where students can learn the value of other attributes and while it’s
13 not the main reason I want to introduce the scheme, these are skills which will help to prepare
14 them for the future.

15 Of course, there will have to be careful vetting of charities or organisations to make sure they
16 can provide valuable experiences for young volunteers, but it’s also essential that our students
17 learn to navigate their own way through without close supervision by teachers. Teachers have
18 enough work and responsibilities, after all! And while we’ll try to offer some choice in the settings
19 where students can volunteer, they won’t be able to avoid it. They can’t choose not to do maths
20 and as another, equally important part of the school curriculum, they won’t be able to opt out of
21 volunteering.

22 My intention is that students should dedicate themselves to helping other human beings, not
23 undertake basic administration to save the organisation time or sit in an office on-screen all
24 afternoon on fund-raising promotions. There will have to be some adjustments to the academic
25 timetable of the school to accommodate this new initiative, but I’m sure students will relish
26 the opportunity to get out from behind their desks. It’s a chance to discover aspects of their
27 community that many of them know nothing about and develop skills we can’t teach them in the
28 classroom.

## Text B
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The following is a reply to the headteacher from a parent.

1  Volunteering is a good thing. That’s obvious and many adults in our community volunteer in a
2  whole range of settings here in our town, deriving much satisfaction from helping others and
3  making a genuine difference in people’s lives. It’s different from working a regular job. It’s not
4  about making a living or building a successful career: it’s about freely giving something which
5  money can’t buy: your time. Most of us don’t volunteer in a formal way, however. We shop for
6  elderly neighbours, visit relatives in hospital or clear the snow from the steps of our neighbours’
7  houses. We see a need and we use our time to help.

8  Many young people learn from their families about serving others. I’m sceptical about whether
9  volunteer schemes for school students really capture that spirit of freely giving your time to help
10 others. Resentful students worrying about how they’re going to complete academic studies
11 before their exams while they ‘volunteer’, or just missing out on sport, probably don’t make good
12 charity workers. When adults volunteer, they match their skills and available time with the need
13 they see. If we’re not comfortable working with young children, we help in other ways. If we find
14 some settings just too distressing, we can use our talents on vital campaigning and fund-raising.

15 Of course it’s good for young people to be nudged out of their comfort zones and learn new skills.
16 Many charities pay workers these days because working with people in difficult circumstances
17 involves training, aptitude and an understanding of good practice, as well as a caring nature. I’m
18 not sure that adding a self-conscious, inexperienced teenager to their responsibilities will help
19 them or the vulnerable people they look after.

20 Maybe the school should just give them an afternoon off; they can help their own hard-pressed
21 families with household chores, looking after siblings or cooking a meal. After all, charity starts at
22 home, doesn’t it?

## Q1
**Marks:** 40
**AOs:** AO1, AO2, AO3

Read Text A and Text B in the insert and then answer Section A, Question 1 on this question paper.

Write an article for your school or college magazine about whether or not a volunteer scheme for older students would be a good idea. In your article you should:

• evaluate the ideas, attitudes and opinions about volunteering in both texts

• consider the effects a compulsory volunteering scheme in school would have on students, teachers and the wider community.

Base your article on what you have read in both texts, but be careful to use your own words. Address both of the bullet points. Write about 250 to 350 words. Up to 15 marks are available for the content of your answer and up to 25 marks for the quality of your writing. **[40]**
