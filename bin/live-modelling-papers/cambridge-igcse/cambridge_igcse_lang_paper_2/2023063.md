## Text B
**Title:** Should we rethink elite sports for kids?
**Author:** Unknown

1  Given the enormous incomes elite sportspeople can earn, it’s no wonder that in many countries
2  across the globe, programmes that aim to identify and develop future elite athletes have been
3  established. Once selected, potential elite footballers, cyclists, gymnasts and others can spend
4  many years being moulded and trained to fulfil their ambitions. These programmes, once entered,
5  can shape the lives of children, and those of their families, for a long time.

6  It’s thought by some coaches that specialising in a specific sport early in childhood is essential
7  to achieve elite levels of success. Just like ballet dancers and musicians, it pays to start early. In
8  sports such as gymnastics and diving, athletes usually peak in their late teens, so specialising
9  in childhood is considered crucial. Athletic careers are also inevitably shorter than most other
10 career choices and children, parents and trainers are often aware of that.

11 Many children who specialise early develop personal qualities of resilience and dedication that
12 would shame an adult. However, some psychologists suggest that if a child doesn’t have other
13 hobbies or doesn’t try new activities, it can make them feel like their identity is solely tied to the
14 sport they specialise in, which can affect their development detrimentally.

15 Delaying intense specialisation might give a young athlete more time to develop outside of their
16 sport. Some think that if young athletes can try a range of different sports, perhaps eventual
17 success at elite levels is more likely, not less. However, with safeguards in place, many child
18 athletes grow up to look back on their years of intense training with pride and gratitude, even if
19 they never make the grade in the elite world.
