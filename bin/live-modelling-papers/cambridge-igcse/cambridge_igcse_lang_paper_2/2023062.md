## Text A
**Title:** What are you sorry for?
**Author:** Unknown

1  Yesterday, after complaints I’d made to an online retailer for their very poor customer service,
2  I finally received an email in reply. ‘At last,’ I thought, ‘an apology.’ And indeed, it was an apology
3  of sorts: a careful set of stock phrases which expressed some limited awareness of their
4  shortcomings with a flourish at the end, hoping that I would ‘return very soon to purchase from
5  our unique range of bestselling rugs and soft furnishings.’ Now, I know saying sorry is never
6  easy, but I was left with the impression that this writer had been schooled in some business
7  training session that taught employees how to turn an apology into a marketing tool!

8  There’s a lot of apologising these days. But, like my email, is there any real substance to such
9  acts of contrition if they are merely designed to reflect well on whoever is making them?

10 Everyday apologies are part of polite discourse in many cultures, of course. We apologise for
11 interrupting people in meetings – as we interrupt them – and we often apologise for things that
12 aren’t even our fault, just to keep the wheels of civilised life turning. I taught my children to
13 apologise for their misdemeanours as soon as they could speak, though it rarely prevented further
14 incidents of snatching toys from their siblings or having a tantrum in a shop. However, in adult
15 life, and especially at work, apologies (even for glaringly obvious errors) are often considered the
16 ultimate weakness.

17 Perhaps the online company who offered me their ‘apologies’ yesterday for wasting my time,
18 sending me the wrong item, taking my money and refusing to return it for weeks were reluctant
19 to admit their faults for fear I would require some compensation as a token of their sincerity.
20 Actually, a real apology would have been sufficient, and I might well have been more disposed to
21 peruse their unique ranges again as a result. As things are, I’ll be staying well clear of their soft
22 furnishings and their grudging apologies.

## Text B
**Title:** The power of an apology
**Author:** Unknown

1  Saying ‘I’m sorry’, even when you’re not at fault, is an automatic reaction. You’ve probably said it
2  many times this week. Over-apologising for things you can’t control can make people think less
3  of you. Apologising for sneezing, being pushed into someone else or being interrupted could
4  backfire on you.

5  A leading psychotherapist says that over-apologising, or apologising because you think you
6  should, is harmful: you may think you’re presenting yourself as a caring person, but you’re
7  sending the message that you lack confidence and are ineffectual. ‘It can give some people
8  permission to treat you poorly,’ she warns.

9  Such behaviour can also lessen the impact of a necessary apology. If you say ‘I’m sorry’ for
10 every little thing, it will carry less weight for situations that warrant a sincere apology. We all know
11 someone who constantly apologises, and it can be irritating.

12 One study found that apologising for intentionally rejecting someone, such as cancelling plans
13 you’ve made, could make the other person feel that they have to forgive before they are ready.
14 Apologising for your own sake, rather than to acknowledge the hurt caused, can be perceived as
15 insulting.

16 Choosing not to apologise may even have psychological benefits. Researchers found that people
17 who refused to express remorse showed signs of greater self-esteem.

18 However, there are some circumstances in which a sincere apology is beneficial. A professor
19 of behavioural science explained that some apologies can encourage forgiveness and repair
20 relationships, but they require more than just saying ‘I’m sorry’: the person must express genuine
21 remorse and offer to make amends in a meaningful way. We protect ourselves from facing the
22 possibility that we messed up and from rejection. An apology, no matter how heartfelt, does not
23 always elicit forgiveness.

24 The debilitating effects of the shame we may feel when we’ve hurt another person can eat away
25 at us. By taking responsibility for our actions, we help rid ourselves of self-reproach and guilt.
26 Some people feel that genuinely apologising can help us develop self-respect if we have the
27 courage to admit we are wrong and don’t offer excuses. Saying sorry helps us remain emotionally
28 connected to others. Knowing we have wronged someone may cause us to distance ourselves
29 from the person, but once we have apologised, we can become closer.
