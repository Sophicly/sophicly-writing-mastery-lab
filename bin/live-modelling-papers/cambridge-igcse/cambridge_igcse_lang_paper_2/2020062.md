# Topic 2020062: Cambridge IGCSE 0500 Paper 2 — June 2020 (variant 2) · Directed Writing (speech) · Composition
**Type:** language_paper

**Teaching Point:** Cambridge IGCSE First Language English Paper 2 — Directed Writing and Composition, June 2020 (variant 2). A real Cambridge past paper. Section A Question 1 [40] = directed writing, 250–350 words, 15 reading + 25 writing; this paper’s form is the speech. ## Text A
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The post below is from an online education forum in which a Headteacher argues for study exchange visits to different countries by teenagers.

1  Many people in my country believe that study exchange visits by students to other countries
2  have limited educational value and offer little more than brief holidays subsidised by parents and
3  their often cash-strapped schools. Some say that older students need every minute in their own
4  schools to give them a chance of success in higher education and a competitive workplace in the
5  future.

6  In my school, however, we’ve recently instigated an ambitious programme of exchange visits
7  where students in the second term of their course can spend a few weeks living and studying in
8  a different country, while keeping up with some of their home school’s work online. It’s proving
9  incredibly popular. Students apply to spend four weeks studying in a partner school overseas.
10 They have to show enough maturity and sense of responsibility to represent their school and
11 their country, as well as benefit from the experience personally.

12 If we choose carefully, our students return from their exchange visits enriched by exposure to
13 new cultures and different educational methods and principles. One of our brightest 14-year-olds
14 recently returned from a four-week visit to study in a school overseas where the resources
15 available reflected the country’s struggling economy. She learned that the teacher, not the shiny
16 gadgetry, is the key to effective learning. Other students have returned with aspirations to work in
17 the countries they visited, fuelling their ambition and drive to succeed.

18 There is more to educating our students than stuffing their heads with facts and knowledge.
19 The opportunities provided by study exchange visits – to develop young people’s personalities,
20 resilience and sense of adventure – cannot be replicated in the classroom. We’ve had young
21 people return from studying in other countries with renewed motivation and enthusiasm, more
22 tolerant and open-minded about the wider world. Now, that’s what I call education!

## Text B
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** This post was written by another Headteacher in response to the Headteacher in Text A.

1  It’s said that travel broadens the mind and maybe it does. I don’t believe, however, that a school
2  should compromise on its primary role which is to equip students with the skills and knowledge to
3  do well for themselves, their families and their communities in later life.

4  A lot of learning happens in four weeks, and the huge time and effort involved in vetting host
5  families, planning and preparing for a lengthy exchange visit is very costly in teacher time. It’s
6  also a very rare 14-year-old, in my experience, who can leave their family, school and country for
7  four weeks and cope well. Adolescence, especially these days, can be a traumatic enough time
8  without such dubious ‘experiences’, and the school will be left to pick up the pieces.

9  Having taught in schools in different countries in the past, I know that educational practices and
10 principles vary widely across the globe. Students returning from four weeks studying abroad
11 may have some superficial views about the relative value of other ways of learning but their
12 real education happens in their own schools. How are 14-year-olds meant to judge what they
13 see elsewhere? A snapshot of a less challenging school environment could limit students’
14 expectations of themselves while a visit to a school with small classes, plush surroundings and
15 all manner of electronics may well induce resentment in young minds. For some, that resentment
16 may eventually drive them away from their own communities where their education can do most
17 good for others.

18 As for overseas students coming here to my school on an exchange visit, I would struggle to
19 persuade teachers to adapt lessons for someone whose progress, especially in examinations,
20 is not their responsibility. I wonder how the parents of students in my school would view the
21 teacher time expended on students who are temporary visitors. We have no way to measure
22 what tangible benefits our students would gain from a study exchange visit, but we might be
23 measuring the disadvantages in poorer grades. We already have close links with several foreign
24 schools with whom our students correspond regularly, developing their language skills and
25 broadening their horizons.

## Q1
**Marks:** 40
**AOs:** AO1, AO2, AO3

Read Text A and Text B in the insert and then answer Section A, Question 1 on this question paper.

Imagine you are the parent of a student who attends a school that is considering a study exchange programme. Write the speech you give at a meeting for parents and teachers about whether it is a good idea for the school to run a study exchange programme. In your speech you should:

• evaluate the ideas and opinions given in both texts

• give your own views, based on what you have read, about whether your child would benefit from a study exchange visit.

Base your speech on what you have read in both texts, but be careful to use your own words. Address both of the bullet points. Write about 250 to 350 words. Up to 15 marks are available for the content of your answer, and up to 25 marks for the quality of your writing. **[40]**
