## Text A
**Title:** We need more wilderness in education
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** In the following text the writer discusses a different approach to education.

1  Instead of obsessing about introducing the latest technology to schools and adopting the most
2  fashionable teaching methods, we should focus on wellbeing and encourage children to connect
3  with the natural world. Children have become cogs in a box-ticking machine.

4  This matters to me a great deal since becoming a father. I don’t want my children to feel the
5  same sense of failure I did if they don’t do well in a conventional classroom.

6  For some, our minds crumbled under pressure; classroom-based learning left me feeling
7  worthless and destroyed my confidence. The worse I did, the more pressure I felt. Eventually,
8  when I was rejected by my university choices, I spiralled into depression.

9  The wilderness rescued me. I have been shaped by my experiences in the great outdoors.
10 Feeling comfortable in the wild gave me back my confidence. There is a natural simplicity to
11 nature; it is far more tactile and tangible than the classroom. It’s a leveller; it strengthened my
12 character and set me back on track.

13 I’m not suggesting the abolition of the current system of education, but we could certainly allow
14 more time for children to explore the world around them. It doesn’t need a huge investment
15 either. Even if you don’t have easy access, wilderness camps and schools work with inner-city
16 children. Part of the beauty of wilderness schooling is that the overheads are low. You want a
17 classroom? Build a shelter from nature’s store. You want to eat? Forage for it. There are plenty of
18 places where children can roam freely or permission from the landowner can be sought.

19 There’s evidence that shows connecting with nature really works. It’s good for social and
20 emotional development, improves self-awareness, and makes young children more co-operative.
21 There is also evidence that the wilderness can reduce hyperactivity and has a soothing effect
22 on children. German visionary Kurt Hahn was one of the early pioneers of outdoor education.
23 His educational vision encompassed craftsmanship, community service, outdoor pursuits and
24 physical skills.

25 Scandinavia has led the way in this field, teaching by topics rather than subjects, such as nature’s
26 life cycle. Wilderness schooling involves team-work, working together in nature, not sitting in rows
27 while teachers lecture. Lessons are collaborative, geared towards improving communication,
28 character and resilience rather than staring through the window in boredom.

29 As my own children step onto the conveyor belt, I am determined to give them a rounded
30 education. We all have a responsibility to ensure every child has access to the same life-enhancing
31 opportunities. It’s time to turn everything on its head and to turn classrooms inside out.

## Text B
**Title:** Outdoor learning – a good idea or another educational fad?
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The following text discusses outdoor learning in schools.

1  Apparently, many people find the arguments for taking children out of the classroom and into the
2  great outdoors compelling. 85 per cent of parents in the UK, and many elsewhere too, say they
3  believe that young children should spend more school time away from their desks and learning
4  outdoors. Not that they necessarily want to give their children these experiences themselves, but
5  schools should do it. And not just taking your books outside to sit on the grass on a sunny day
6  either. The majority of a child’s school day, rain or shine, should be spent outdoors, building their
7  self-esteem by log-jumping or developing team-work skills by literally building bridges.

8  It’s true that most young kids like being outdoors. There’s evidence to show that even working
9  in a classroom with a view of greenery outside is better for their wellbeing. The argument that
10 the social and physical benefits of an outdoors education will sustain children as they grow older
11 is, maybe, convincing. But whether it’s the extremes of weather or the need to prove yourself to
12 universities and employers, there are limitations to adopting a whole-scale outdoors education
13 model, especially beyond the early years of schooling. What about the bookish kids, or those
14 with allergies or disabilities? What about the shift of resources from books and new technology
15 to outdoor spaces? There will have to be risk assessment processes and teacher retraining. How
16 can the same ethos of outdoor learning be maintained as pupils transition from one school to
17 another? Those same parents who wanted more outdoor learning for their young offspring might
18 wish they’d settled for a few more plants in the classroom instead.
