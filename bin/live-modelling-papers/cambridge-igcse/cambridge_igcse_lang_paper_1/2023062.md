## Text A
**Title:** Working a winter season at a ski resort
**Author:** Unknown

1  A great way to really enjoy winter is to work at a ski resort. Not all jobs require prior knowledge
2  of skiing or skill in snowboarding, though enthusiasm and a sense of adventure are essential.
3  If you have decided to work a season in the snow, the next step is the planning; you are
4  probably wondering where to start.

5  While some resorts will hire throughout the entire season as staff quit, more well-known
6  resorts tend to fill posts quickly before the season starts. When job opportunities are
7  advertised, the earlier you start applying, the broader the variety of positions and your choices
8  of location will be.

9  If you have your heart set on a particular resort, you will need to comply with their hiring
10 dates and be careful to put in your application in time. For northern hemisphere resorts, job
11 advertisements go up as early as July for a December start. For southern hemisphere resorts,
12 jobs typically go up around February for June. Check out the individual resort’s website for
13 further information on their specific hiring dates.

14 How long you want to stay may affect the visa you need to apply for, but the first consideration
15 is which part of the world you want to work in. Locations known for quality powder (snow)
16 or exhilarating slopes attract massive foreign crowds.

17 There’s no point denying it: powder is the best snow to ride. Dry and light, it does not bind
18 – you can’t make a snowball with loose powder snow. It can be great for learning on and
19 generally practising new ski or snowboard techniques without any unexpected bumps. It’s
20 often packed in thick layers that form a pillow for any crashes you decide to take! It allows
21 you to push yourself to ‘stomp that new trick’ and ‘hold your edge’. Fresh powder falls during
22 cold weather with small snowflakes.

23 Online guides can help you choose which job you’d like to apply for. You may also want to
24 investigate some of the recruitment organisations that source jobs for you. Some are pretty
25 effective. For an admittedly hefty fee they will bring employers to your home country and set
26 up an interview for you (basically guaranteeing a job unless you put in an exceptionally poor
27 interview) and give you priority access to staff housing. Most of this you can do yourself,
28 although saving money beforehand often means arriving without a job: that intervening period
29 can be quite costly. As always, the earlier the better when booking flights.

## Text B
**Title:** Seasonal work
**Author:** Unknown

1  Looking for a part-time job or full-time career? Have you considered seasonal work?

2  You could find work at a resort during the tourist season (think skiing or camping) or pick
3  berries or other produce. You could work with kids at summer camps or help out at a local
4  festival. There are seasonal jobs to fit every different season.

5  But here’s the question: are they worth it? Perhaps. For instance, let’s say you are looking
6  for work as a retail manager, but you don’t have much experience. Two months working as
7  a cashier could fill that gap. Just make sure that you’re thoughtful in how you present your
8  seasonal job on your résumé.

9  Most seasonal jobs don’t require much previous knowledge and suit any talent level, they
10 also tend to be low-paying. If you’re currently out of work, there’s minimal downside. If you’re
11 looking for a little extra spending cash to save up for a trip, it could also be a good move.

12 However, if you’re working on your career, you might need to consider whether or not the
13 extra hours make sense. Do you have a family who needs your time? Are you already
14 exhausted from your job or studies? Low pay can be a deal breaker and you’ll probably be
15 working a tough schedule. Holiday retail seasonal help as well as tourist-based jobs tend to
16 be heaviest on evenings and weekends.

17 Don’t expect to get hired for a seasonal job and get some good, detailed training before you
18 start. Due to the short nature of the season (whatever season it may be) you can expect to
19 get a quick how-to speech at best. For this reason, choose a seasonal job where you already
20 have some rudimentary knowledge. If your goal is to find out about the business, you may
21 have to learn on the job.

22 Whatever, seasonal jobs offer opportunities. It’s your choice, but hurry: those seasonal jobs
23 won’t last long.

## Text C
**Title:** Wanaka
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** Tony has been travelling the world doing seasonal work for the past year and has just started a new job working as a liftie (ski-lift attendant) for the first time near Wanaka, New Zealand.

1  Training commenced with a slideshow about every different job at the fashionable resort,
2  presumably to underline the fact that we’d got the worst of the outdoor ones. Indoor
3  employees, personal chefs and private ski-instructors lodged free on-site; ‘outsiders’ battled
4  for reasonably priced accommodation with hordes of backpackers travelling through; wealthier
5  guests stayed in the hotels.

6  Everyone was asking questions I didn’t understand. ‘What about rules on quick-laps?’ ‘What’s
7  the back-country policy?’ ‘How deep’s the base?’ ‘How much snow do you make?’ I was
8  clearly in over my head. Again! This time in snow, which gave the appearance of being soft
9  – but which, as any snow-sports fanatic will tell you, was more dangerous than it looked.

10 Here are the answers:

11 • No ‘quick-laps’ in week one. Apparently, they’re a trick used by experienced lifties who
12 take turns to run the lift they’re working on – each person doing two people’s jobs, while
13 their partner takes the lift to the top and skis or snowboards back down again. It’s a
14 great way to get a sneaky bit of extra riding in, and generally overlooked by bosses –
15 providing each person crewing the lift is capable of running it on their own. I wasn’t.
16 • ‘Back-country’ was everything outside the marked boundaries of the ski-area (avoided
17 for fear of avalanches).
18 • The base (snow-depth) was two metres, sometimes rising to four (requiring much
19 digging-out of cars and buildings).
20 • ‘Snow’ was ‘made’ nightly. Gigantic cannons shot this granular substance skywards
21 ensuring the mountain could be open for skiing whether it snowed naturally overnight
22 or not.

23 Our Lift Supervisor (‘Boss’) marched us around every lift in the ski-field. ‘So now you know
24 the lifts,’ he informed us. ‘See you all bright and early tomorrow!’

25 And that was it: training was over! I’d never pushed a button, had only the flimsiest concept
26 how a ski lift even worked, much less how to fix one if something went wrong, didn’t know
27 how to test the equipment, use the radio in case of emergency or assist guests on and off
28 the lift. Ski lifts spin all day, every hour transporting skiers and riders of all ability levels up
29 the mountain. I didn’t know how many things I didn’t know about my new job – but suspected
30 there were plenty.

31 Next morning, we assembled indoors, armed ourselves for combat and headed out again.
32 Our opponents were the lift chairs, which overnight had turned into fantastic frozen sculptures.
33 Relentless weather, coupled with dramatically sub-zero temperatures, transformed these
34 humble steel and wooden frames into a series of massive ice-monoliths, layered and carved
35 by snow and wind into intricate abstract shapes. They were breathtakingly beautiful, and a
36 pain to clear before we could ride them to our allocated stations at given points up the
37 (inventively named) Giant Ski Slope.

38 Walking back down from there took forever – experienced lifties snowboarded. It got cold
39 up top; when the weather closed in, I’d sit shivering in my tiny control-booth, fantasising
40 about life as a cleaner inside the ski-rental building.

41 One Wednesday, rumour had it we’d be closing early. Halfway up Giant Ski Slope, it seemed
42 mildly surprising we hadn’t already. The wind was raging, buffeting chairs and customers
43 alike. All afternoon a slushy drizzle had been slowly working its way through my layers. The
44 bus back from the mountain crawled out of the car park and attempted the descent, but it
45 was a total white-out. The huge windscreen filled with snow faster than the wipers could
46 clear it. We crunched into the uphill side of the mountain and stuck there. Gusts of wind,
47 revealing and concealing the narrow road ahead, confirmed that drifts of snow had blocked
48 it completely. Our driver radioed in to report our situation; we waited.

49 At 02:45, I staggered, still soaked to the skin, chilled, and exhausted, into my room.

50 ‘That was nothing!’ crowed Boss the next day.
51 ‘You should’ve been here for the Great Storm ’
