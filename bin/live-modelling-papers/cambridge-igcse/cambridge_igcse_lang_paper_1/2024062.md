## Text A
**Title:** Bring your child to work day
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** This online article has been written to introduce employers in large companies to the idea of a ‘Bring your child to work day’ (BYCTWD).

1  When you were a kid, did you ever wish to visit your parents’ workplace with them? ‘Bring
2  your child to work day’ is designed to give youngsters a glimpse into the working world’s
3  opportunities and challenges.

4  The initiative first started in Canada as part of an educational programme during which
5  parents could take their offspring to work for one day. It’s now hugely successful, with many
6  similar schemes globally. Rather than being on any set international date, a day is usually
7  chosen that is mutually convenient for participating companies with crucial deadlines and
8  their staff with school-age children.

9  Why not share the work you do at your workplace with your kids, so they can gain an insight
10 into the atmosphere of your workplace? If your company is following a work-from-home
11 policy, you can devise typical activities that you might do at your workplace and participants
12 can complete these with their child. Your employees also love having their children involved
13 in the business for a day and often work harder to impress them.

14 The scheme engages staff with children during tours of the building, reminding staff of
15 company rules and procedures as they answer youngsters’ questions, and encouraging
16 them to talk positively about the role they play at work – teaching communication skills that
17 might help children in the future. Young visitors see the different roles that are carried out
18 by people to run a business and might even find their interest in one of the fields.

19 Of course, you’ll need to plan schedules carefully. Will children be attending any work
20 meetings? If yes, you’ll need to ensure you keep enough time free in between for fun activities.
21 Will they only be attending family-friendly team activities? Your company may decide to
22 organise a single activity for the children, rather than having them at work for the entire day
23 – ensuring health and safety is important. Alternatively, a family-friendly activity may be part
24 of a longer day of events. Whatever you decide, include virtual activities that both in-office
25 and remote teams can participate in. For a memorable day, we suggest organising some
26 special BYCTWD activities – perhaps crafting or a magic show.

## Text B
**Title:** A change of direction
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The writer of this article has gone back to university to study for another degree at 50 years old.

1  When I first applied to university at 18, I was interested in psychology. My parents felt law
2  was more suitable. In 2016, after 25 years as a lawyer, I returned to university to study
3  psychology!

4  Classes initially were both terrifying and exciting – because of the career change and
5  uncertainty, knowing I was likely to be the oldest person in class, and the new journey. It
6  wasn’t easy meeting new people, learning new theories, but not as difficult as I’d expected,
7  and was very rewarding.

8  Learning new technology is hard as a 50-year-old. There were various online tools to
9  communicate with professors and other students. Most students today use technology and
10 find it easy. However, once I’d mustered the nerve to ask, help was always there. Younger
11 students found that explaining to me strengthened their understanding too.

12 As an ‘older student’, forget about ‘not fitting in’. Embrace your mature perspective as an
13 asset you bring to class. Speak up, ask questions, make comments – being at university
14 isn’t just about learning from books and professors, but learning from each other.

15 Admittedly, memorising new information was harder than I’d remembered, though the skills
16 and abilities required for study felt familiar. Being a parent at the same time, I had honed my
17 time-management skills during my legal career – invaluable when dealing with multiple
18 assignments and exams.

19 Sometimes it felt weird being taught by professors younger than my son, but they treated
20 me no differently from other students. Ironically, because I was more comfortable than my
21 peers seeking clarifications from professors, I was often sought out by the other students
22 when they were nervous about speaking up. For example, after one exam some students
23 felt one question was too hard. They asked me to voice their concerns. After I’d successfully
24 argued on the class’s behalf, the professor smiled good-naturedly saying, ‘Aren’t you all
25 lucky to have a lawyer as your representative?’

26 To capitalise on your university experience, resist the urge to just leave when class is over.
27 If you’re asked to socialise with your classmates, say yes! Make time to sit and study with
28 other students. As I continue my education in psychology, informal chats with classmates –
29 who I now consider friends – give me invaluable insights into younger generations, teaching
30 me greater empathy for the different world that they have grown up in.

## Text C
**Title:** Changing places
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** Sonja, a teacher who has been working in a school in England for several years, has volunteered for a work exchange. She is going to live and work for a few weeks in the village of Siurung, Nepal.

1  10 January

2  Today’s my birthday. I don’t know anyone well enough to say. There’ll be messages for me
3  online, but there’s no internet here. I hope no one back home is worried.

4  When I first arrived, I was enthusiastic about my decision and the work I am going to do, but
5  I’ve mixed feelings now: is it presumptuous and arrogant of me to imagine I have anything
6  to offer here? Ramesh assures me colleagues at his school are looking forward to meeting
7  me.

8  I’ve already learned how to say thank you and ‘malai bhaiyo’ (which signifies politely that I
9  have eaten enough), a phrase that will prove useful with such generous hosts and delicious
10 food.

11 12 January

12 First day at school.

13 Huddled under three blankets, I wake up early. Texts home still aren’t getting through.

14 I want to tell them about the narrow and snaking drive up the mountainside; the deeply
15 moving traditional welcome of exquisite flower garlands awaiting me in the village; the lanes
16 paved with impossible jigsaw puzzles of metamorphic rock and my accommodation – a
17 homestay room. Homestays are small huts less sturdy than the main houses. They offer
18 adequate, affordable accommodation to trekkers and extra income for local families. They’re
19 designed optimistically to be used in tourist season, not the middle of winter.

20 29 January

21 Ramesh also teaches Class 8. I’d told him how I loved their enthusiasm. He reassured me
22 they enjoy my lessons too: they like my ‘unusual’ teaching style and gain confidence from
23 being able to understand everything I say in English.

24 3 February

25 The weather’s definitely getting better – less cold. Ramesh proposes another hike after
26 school. Since arriving, I’ve been grateful for his kindness. He’s become like a brother. When
27 we’re in a group and everyone’s talking, he makes sure I’m not left out, translating for me
28 what’s going on.

29 Next week, there’s a wedding in Ramesh’s family!

30 9 February

31 With festivities starting tomorrow, priorities seem to be cleaning the house and washing
32 bedding. I feel immensely privileged to be part of this happy family occasion, but problems
33 of communication mean I’m not sure what to expect. Fortunately, Ramesh explains: this
34 wedding’s particularly important for the family, the final celebration of its kind, as his brother
35 Tulshi is the last of his siblings to get married. Some relatives have travelled far for the
36 festivities. The bride, Dipha, works in the capital city Kathmandu. She’ll arrive tomorrow
37 along with her own family and friends who will all stay here until the wedding celebrations
38 are over. I guess that explains why so much bedding was washed this morning!

39 My Northern European mind wonders what would have happened if it had rained today.
40 Ramesh looks confused, then amused, explaining that this isn’t the rainy season. Simple!

41 10 February

42 There’s a buffet: mini mountains of curried vegetables, accompanied by copious amounts
43 of rice! The village square teems and fizzes with people eating, drinking, laughing and children
44 of all ages running. Weddings always provide an opportunity for people who live far apart
45 to see each other, catch up, remark on how the children have grown. Everything here’s so
46 similar and so different from the world I’ve left behind.

47 12 February

48 A family trek is planned to a local beauty spot perched higher up the mountains. I am thrilled
49 to be invited. The sun is pouring out of the uninterrupted Himalayan sky. On the way up
50 Ramesh tells everyone that tigers sometimes roam this area – information I could happily
51 do without. I know Nepal is home to the Bengal tiger but was not expecting to meet one
52 lurking here. As I express concern, he giggles, reassuring me solemnly in English that these
53 tigers are ‘not so big’. Then I realise and giggle too. The valley and river below stretch
54 shimmering into the distance. I already know that leaving this place isn’t going to be easy.
