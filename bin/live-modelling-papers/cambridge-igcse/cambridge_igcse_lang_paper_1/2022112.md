## Text A
**Title:** Why dance?
**Author:** Unknown

1  There are few cultures on earth that don’t possess an urge to dance. Our ancestors painted
2  their cave walls with pictures of dancers. Even babies instinctively jig in time to music.

3  But how is it that such a simple physical act has the ability to lift the spirits?

4  You get a much bigger release of endorphins when you dance than during other forms of
5  exercise. It also connects with the emotional centres in the brain. For many people, dancing
6  prompts an emotional release. Often that’s uncomplicated happiness. Others cry. It’s cathartic
7  – a letting go of pent-up emotions.

8  Experiments have proved the cognitive benefits of dancing. Researchers took a group of
9  people into a laboratory where music was played for five minutes. Each had to choose from
10 three options: to sit and listen quietly, to cycle on an exercise bike or to get up and dance.
11 All were given cognitive tasks to perform before and after. Those who chose to dance
12 displayed improved problem-solving skills afterwards. The mood levels of the dancers also
13 went up.

14 Another big draw to dancing is the social element. The synchrony involved in dancing to a
15 beat along with other people is a powerful way for humans to bond socially. You can walk
16 into a dance class not knowing another soul, and quickly discover that dancing – at whatever
17 level – is a wonderful way of breaking down inhibitions.

18 Dancing is not just for those of a certain age or ability. Situations where you might dance
19 include anything from an impromptu jig at an overly quiet family wedding to a salsa-style
20 exercise regime. Those who dance, even just for fun, enjoy better mental well-being than
21 those who don’t.

22 These factors compensate for the fact that dancing is not typically a high-paying profession.
23 Dancers are professional people who adapt to long hours of perfecting their art. They perform
24 warm-up and cool-down exercises after a rigorous workout, knowing that this is the only way
25 to reduce the likelihood of an injury or the need to retire unnecessarily early.

## Text B
**Title:** Advice to dance teachers
**Author:** Unknown

1  So, you’re a dance teacher putting together your timetable for a new term. You teach students
2  in beginners, intermediate and advanced classes. Your timetable looks logical:

3  6pm – Beginners

4  7pm – Intermediate

5  8pm – Advanced.

6  Sadly, this is one of those hidden reasons for student dissatisfaction. Why? Because this
7  timetable turns your classes into performances. Here’s what happens

8  Your beginners are nervous. It’s your job to make them feel safe in a friendly and caring
9  class. About twenty minutes before the end of their class, when they are tired, hot in the
10 face, sweaty and self-conscious, the intermediate students start to arrive. After getting
11 changed, they hang around outside your studio, peering in at the beginners.

12 Don’t underestimate how humiliating this is for beginners. They are nowhere near
13 performance-ready, but the class suddenly has an audience of people that the beginners
14 know are more experienced than them.

15 The same then happens to the intermediate students when the advanced ones arrive

16 The remedy? Change the rank order of your classes.

17 Class sizes are important too. Large beginners’ classes mean students feel over-looked.
18 They should never feel that the teacher is too busy to help. It’s important to use the more
19 capable students to model a dance move they have mastered, but not at the expense of
20 other students’ self-confidence. Move rows of dancers forward one row at a time, so they
21 all get the chance to be the dancers on the front line. Don’t humiliate older children by drawing
22 attention to a younger child’s superior talent. It’s better to try to keep children in similar age
23 groups as much as possible. Oh, and do keep parents out of the studio – parental
24 competitiveness does not help children to thrive while learning to dance.

25 What about marketing? Cluttered websites are confusing. It is worth paying for professional
26 photography so that you don’t give a poor impression or reinforce stereotypes. One mistake
27 I see a lot of dance teachers make on their websites is using the old trick of saying ‘we’ about
28 your company when there is only one of you. A prospective student will be thinking “Hmm
29 I like her, but what’s this ‘we’ business? Does that mean if I turn up at her class, there might
30 be another teacher taking the class instead? And what if I don’t like them?” For a dance
31 school trying to attract nervous students, ‘I’, ‘me’, ‘my’ work much, much better.

## Text C
**Title:** The dancer
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** This text is taken from a longer narrative. Veda is a young girl with aspirations to become a dancer.

1  I pause in the doorway and bite my lip. ‘I got a letter saying I got through to the finals for the
2  dance competition. If I win, I win a place at the dance college,’ I finally say.

3  Grandma retreats into the kitchen. She has told me that she doesn’t think it’s her place to
4  interfere with her son and daughter-in-law. Pa’s eyes rove from Ma to me. He’s caught in
5  the middle.

6  Ma’s diamond earrings flash at me like angry eyes. ‘Veda, you need to study hard. If you
7  don’t do well in your exams this year ...’

8  For once my voice doesn’t stick in my throat. ‘I am studying hard. To be a dancer. I’m not
9  planning to become an engineer. Or a doctor.’ Or any profession that Ma finds respectable.

10 Ma launches into a lecture. ‘Dancing is no career. You need to study something useful in
11 college so that you can get a well-paid job.’

12 I sigh unnecessarily loudly. My dance teacher isn’t rich but his house is larger than ours.
13 Clearly he earns more than Ma at her bank job and Pa at his library.

14 Back when I was younger, I’d struggle to be better at school for Ma’s sake. But numbers
15 and letters soon grew too hard for me to hold and I grew far away from them, and Ma grew
16 out of patience.

17 All my life Ma’s dreamt I’ll do well at science and mathematics so that I could end up being
18 what she wanted to be: an engineer. All my life I’ve been waiting for her to appreciate my
19 compulsion to do the one thing I excel at: dance.

20 I think back to my conversations with Grandma. ‘Steps came to you early. Speech came
21 late,’ she’d say. She’d tell me how I used to heave myself by the restricting bars on my
22 prison-like cot at eight months, my limbs urgently craving release. I could shape thoughts
23 with my fingers, she said. While words stumbled in my throat, losing their way before reaching
24 my lips, my hands spoke my first sentences like lotus buds blossoming. They would shape
25 themselves effortlessly into the hand symbols of classical dancing.

26 Grandma always understood everything my hands said.

27 ********

28 My dance teacher sits cross-legged on the ground, tapping beats out on his hollow wooden
29 block with a stick.

30 I leap and land on my sure feet, excitement mounting as his rhythm speeds, challenging me
31 to repeat my routine faster. My heels strike the ground fast as fire-sparks. Streams of sweat
32 trickle down my neck. My braided hair flies free into the air, then whips sharply around my
33 waist. Nothing else fills me with such elation as chasing down soaring music, catching and
34 pinning rhythms to the ground with my feet, proud as a hunter rejoicing in his skill.

35 This brings me to the hardest pose of all. Balancing on my left leg, I extend my right upwards
36 in a vertical split. Then I bend my right knee, bring my right foot near my ear. Locking my
37 breath in my chest to control my trembling, I push myself to hold the pose for an entire
38 eight-beat cycle. A familiar thrill shoots up my spine. I enjoy testing my stamina, my balance.

39 My dance teacher’s stick clatters heavily to the floor. He claps spontaneously. ‘Perform like
40 that and you’re sure to win.’ I can see tears brimming like dew-drops in his eyes.

41 ******

42 Three days later, I arrive home. Grandma opens the door. I can hardly speak. ‘The judges
43 loved me! I have a place at the dance school. Grandma, I am so excited.’

44 Grandma hugs me. ‘I am delighted, Veda. You need to tell Ma and Pa but don’t worry: I have
45 been talking to Pa, and I think you can persuade them, if you answer their questions sensibly
46 and explain yourself.’
