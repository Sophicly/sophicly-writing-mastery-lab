## Text A
**Title:** The problem with plastics
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** This article warns of the dangers of plastics.

1  Plastics are wonder materials: adaptable and durable. We produce and use more
2  plastics than we do almost any other man-made materials, apart from steel, cement
3  and brick. Scientists calculate the total plastic ever made as 8.3 billion tonnes – as
4  heavy as one billion elephants – an astonishing mass of material.

5  Mass-manufacturing of plastics began in the 1950s. Plastics are now all around us, in
6  everything from food wrapping to aeroplane parts and flame retardants. It is precisely
7  plastics’ amazing qualities that present a growing problem.

8  ‘We’re rapidly heading towards “Planet Plastic”. If we don’t want to live in that kind of
9  world we have to rethink how we use materials, particularly plastic,’ explains
10 environmental scientist, Dr Ros Gaia. ‘None of the commonly used plastics are
11 biodegradable. You can permanently dispose of plastic waste by incinerating it – but
12 that’s complicated by concerns about health and emissions.’

13 Plastic items tend to be used for very short periods before being discarded. Waste
14 plastic is sent largely to landfill; much of it just litters the wider environment, including
15 the oceans.

16 Dr Gaia commented: ‘People need to realise that a plastic bottle could be recycled 20
17 times. Currently, poor design limits us. The whole point of recycling is keeping material
18 in use for ever if you can. Actually 90 per cent of the material that does get recycled
19 only gets recycled once.’

20 In the meantime, the waste mounts up. Recycling rates are increasing, and there are
21 new biodegradable alternatives, but manufacturing plastic is so cheap that there is little
22 incentive for change. Each year eight million tonnes of plastic end up in the oceans,
23 with clear evidence that some gets into the food chains because marine creatures ingest
24 small fragments of micro-plastics.

25 Dr Gaia explains: ‘We’re facing a tsunami of plastic waste. The global waste industry
26 needs to get its act together. We need a radical shift. On current trends, it will take until
27 2060 before more plastic gets recycled than landfilled and lost to the environment. We
28 can’t wait that long.’

## Text B
**Title:** It’s great that Blue Planet II is pushing hard on plastic pollution, but
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The writer of the article has viewed a documentary about the world’s oceans called Blue Planet II. The documentary was first aired as a series on television and attracted large numbers of viewers.

1  I gasped in awe at the latest stunning images of marine life in the marvellous television
2  documentary, Blue Planet II. Blue sharks dodged great whites to scavenge on oceanic
3  carrion; sperm whales dozed vertically then plunged to unfathomable depths to feed.
4  It was jaw-dropping stuff.

5  However, in this final part of the series, the narrator’s tone has changed. He has bad
6  news. He shows us grim images: turtles and tropical fish tangled in plastic debris and,
7  most heart-breaking of all, a mother pilot whale unable to let go of her long-dead infant.
8  ‘Today in the Atlantic waters whales have to share the ocean with plastic. A mother is
9  holding her newborn young – it’s dead,’ he said. ‘The mother’s milk may have been
10 contaminated by plastics.’

11 This had a big impact. One newspaper headline: ‘Shocked viewers vow never to use
12 disposable plastic again,’ captured the reaction of many. Online message boards buzzed,
13 demanding a ban on all plastics.

14 I know research suggests there could already be over five trillion pieces of plastic in
15 the ocean; that by 2050 there could be more plastic than fish; that birds are starving as
16 they fill their guts with plastic waste; that micro-plastic is in our seafood. I wanted to
17 shout, ‘Yes! Ban plastics!’ but the scientist in me resisted.

18 In the whole programme, no direct link was made between the death of this baby whale
19 and plastics; we saw pictures of whales and pictures of plastics in the sea, but no
20 evidence that this whale-mother’s milk actually contained contamination from plastics.
21 Nothing.

22 I convulsed with frustration at the idea of wildlife being killed by human waste, but was
23 incensed by the lack of direct evidence shown in the programme.

24 Some wildlife programmes have been criticised for passing off footage of captive animals
25 as hard-won material taken in the wild. If the facts are right, perhaps that’s OK – it
26 makes striking educational TV – but the linkages between this dead whale and plastic
27 pollution were at best circumstantial.

28 Campaigners argue that plastic and toxic chemicals are capable of killing young whales,
29 and everybody needs to realise the urgency of the pollution issue.

30 Urgent action is needed, but we need to keep up the pressure for change with good
31 evidence. I don’t want exposing the production fakery to become the story,
32 overshadowing the real story about plastic pollution. Please, programme makers, go
33 beyond brilliant images and gripping storylines. Documentaries can change views with
34 truth and scientific evidence.

## Text C
**Title:** The penguin lessons
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The narrator, Tom Michell, has borrowed his friend’s luxury holiday apartment by the sea. It is out of season, and the friend is not using the apartment himself. Tom is enjoying a few days relaxing and exploring in the quiet resort, and unexpectedly meets a new friend and companion.

1  A few fishing boats and pleasure craft rocked gently in the small harbour on the
2  fashionable western side of the point, basking serenely in the winter sunlight. Cries of
3  gulls and the smell of fish filled the air. Vibrant colours of boats and painted houses
4  played against the sapphire sea and azure sky.

5  Swimming in unison, shimmering shoals of sprats raced around the harbour, zigzagging,
6  dividing and reuniting. Waves of mesmerising light reflected off these iridescent fish.
7  There were penguins in the harbour, too. It was captivating to watch them fly through
8  the water, twisting and turning, snapping up sprats. I was only surprised that there were
9  not more penguins there feasting on such rich and easy pickings.

10 I turned and walked round the promontory to the eastern side. I had only been strolling
11 along the seashore for minutes, when I caught sight of the first of them: black, unmoving
12 shapes. Initially, I was aware of only a few but, as I walked on, they grew in number,
13 until the whole beach appeared to be covered with black lumps in a black carpet.
14 Hundreds of penguins lay dead in the sand, covered in thick, cloying oil and tar. Each
15 wave that broke piled another grim batch of carcasses on top of those already there.
16 The sight was dreadful, sickening and depressing.

17 I understood then why there were so few penguins in the harbour – only a lucky few
18 had avoided the oil slick. Consumed by dark thoughts, I continued walking. The pollution
19 along the beach extended as far as I could see.

20 I had not heard reports of any oil spill, but in those days regulations were less stringent.
21 After discharging cargo at their destinations, oil tankers would put to sea again and
22 wash out their tanks, creating vast, deadly floating oil slicks.

23 I had been walking briskly, unwilling to focus closely on the details of the dead creatures,
24 when, out of the corner of my eye, I saw a movement in the stillness on the beach. I
25 stopped. One valiant bird was alive: a single survivor struggling amid all that death.

26 Although it was lying on its belly and covered in tar like the others, this penguin was
27 holding its head up and moving its wings with little spasmodic jerks. Could I walk on
28 and abandon it to the poisonous oil and exhausting, suffocating tar? I decided that I
29 could not; I headed towards it.

30 I had no clear plan – in fact, no plan at all. As I approached the solitary penguin, it
31 struggled to its feet, flapping sticky wings and ready to fight for its life once more.

32 Amid all the obscenity, this single penguin sparking with anger stood there, eyeing me
33 suspiciously. How would I approach this filthy, aggressive bird? I scanned the
34 accumulated rubbish along the beach: bits of wood, plastic bottles, disintegrating fishing
35 net. As I moved away, the penguin settled back down on its tummy again. Hurriedly, I
36 gathered debris that I thought might be of assistance. Now, gladiator-like, I approached
37 my quarry. Sensing the renewed threat, it immediately reared up to full height. Its black,
38 malevolent eyes shone with pure loathing and venom. Its beak snapped shut with a
39 savage metallic clack. Swirling a piece of fishing net, I distracted the penguin and, with
40 the swiftness and bravery of Achilles, dropped the net over its head, pushed it over with
41 a stick and grabbed its feet.

42 Arriving back at the flat, I looked around and realised that I’d been carried away with
43 the idea of rescuing the penguin. The average penthouse holiday flat is rarely equipped
44 with the necessities for de-tarring penguins. I had not thought about the practicalities
45 that cleaning would involve. The flat was elegant, tasteful, like an advertisement from
46 a glossy magazine – the last place to bring a furious, oil-soaked penguin. The chances
47 of making a real mess, and getting injured into the bargain, seemed very real.
