## Text A
**Title:** Ships, boats and yachts
**Author:** Unknown

1  Used for sports and leisure, or to transport cargo, as well as for fishing and defence, ships
2  and boats are two of the earliest modes of transportation. To understand the differences
3  between ‘ships’ and ‘boats’, various aspects need consideration.

4  A boat is a small- to mid-sized vessel, with lower cargo-carrying capability than a ship. Ships
5  are specifically designed to carry either cargo or passengers, whereas the word ‘boat’ is a
6  more general term used for various watercraft and can be anything from a small rowing boat
7  upwards. Confusingly, plenty of captains affectionately call their ship a boat, no matter how
8  large or small it happens to be.

9  Meanwhile, a ‘yacht’ is a sail or power vessel used for pleasure, cruising or racing. There’s
10 no standard definition or design, but most have a cabin with a kitchen (galley). Allowing for
11 additional space for sleeping quarters, that means ‘yachts’ need to be at least ten metres in
12 length. Most yachts are boats but not all boats are yachts.

13 Yachts can get pretty big – reaching up to more than 60 metres long – though once they
14 start getting big, they can be further qualified as ‘mega yachts’ and ‘super yachts’. Some
15 very large yachts are actually ships that are called yachts.

16 Anyone with ambitions to buy their own yacht or boat may understand why sailing is called
17 ‘the sport of kings’ when they first see the price tag; boats are also expensive to maintain.
18 But is the cost justifiable?

19 Comparisons with cars often arise when discussing why boats are so expensive. Even a
20 top-of-the-range luxury automobile seems cheap when compared to a boat of a comparable
21 specification, but cars don’t have to be built to withstand a constantly wet environment.
22 Simply adding the word ‘marine’ to anything will double its cost: unlike cars, whose
23 manufacturing process is now almost wholly automated, boats have to be built mostly by
24 hand. Many people regard cars as a necessity, choosing only to hire boats for holidays rather
25 than head down the path of ownership. When manufacturers can’t lower their costs through
26 high-volume efficiency, it necessarily drives the price of each boat sold higher.

## Text B
**Title:** How to travel the globe and still spare the world
**Author:** Unknown

1  For years, I’ve spent every free moment exploring the world, from backpacking through
2  Southeast Asia to studying in Australia. But recently, I came to a horrible realisation: frequent
3  flying is an expensive habit and I’m not just talking money. No other means of transport
4  spews more CO2 into the atmosphere than planes.

5  It’s easy to argue that we shouldn’t travel so far – those of us with friends and family spread
6  across the globe want to see them in person occasionally and some people must travel for
7  work. There are of course greener ways to travel, though we can’t all sail across oceans on
8  solar-powered yachts.

9  If finances allow, don’t choose your flight for price and convenience: routes and airlines make
10 a difference environmentally. Flights with multiple stopovers and detours are often cheaper,
11 but release more carbon, so direct flights with modern airlines, using more efficient planes
12 are better. The growing trend of ‘carbon offsetting’ claims you can buy your way out of the
13 environmental damage you cause. I’m unconvinced. Basically, airlines give you information
14 telling you exactly how big your carbon footprint will be on their flight. You then pay a company
15 to invest in an environmental project such as producing energy from wind, but experts warn
16 that offset programs aren’t effective and may inadvertently encourage people to fly more.

17 If you absolutely must fly, make the most out of your trip by staying as long as possible.
18 Flying somewhere for a weekend creates a lot of emissions for little benefit. Instead, combine
19 shorter visits into one. Your fellow passengers can also make a difference. While it’s a treat
20 to be on an empty flight where you can stretch out over an entire row, airlines that tend to
21 fill their planes are greener options. An extra suitcase? When it comes to CO2 emissions,
22 every kilogram of luggage counts!

23 Effective regulation is needed. Airlines need to think outside of the box and work together
24 to find innovative solutions. The average occupancy of planes is currently only 80 per cent.
25 Ultimately, we need a globally connected data network that allocates available seats,
26 maximising efficiency and relocating resources. Some airlines are already exploring new
27 technologies, using big data and artificial intelligence to analyse billions of flight records to
28 find ways to save fuel, as well as developing viable alternative biofuel options, but they need
29 to go faster.

## Text C
**Title:** The big adventure
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** Having given up their settled way of life and spent their savings on buying a small yacht called Josephine, the writer and his wife, Abi, are planning to explore South America. They set out from Cape Town, South Africa, in April on the first stage of the journey to Rio de Janeiro, accompanied by a more experienced sailor, Dael.

1  Getting to Rio we assumed would be straightforward: head northwest, following the afternoon
2  sun, sail for long enough – say a month – and eventually bump into northern Brazil. Next,
3  turn left and sail down the coast – maybe for three days – until reaching Ilhabela where my
4  mother-in-law lived. There we’d holiday, before making final changes to the boat and heading
5  south on our ‘big adventure’. Simple. I would film everything, marketing the footage later.

6  Things could go wrong, as Dael enjoyed reminding us: fires on board, being run down by
7  cargo ships at night or colliding with whales and sinking. As it turned out, we were more
8  likely to be perpetually seasick or run out of supplies. It was, I felt, ultimately about attitude
9  – after all, traffic on the way to work can be dangerous.

10 Sailing across the Atlantic wasn’t like a road trip though (Dael’s observation). There’s nowhere
11 to pull over when you’re tired. Getting to Rio entailed crossing one of the planet’s loneliest
12 stretches of ocean. Josephine still had no autopilot and no radio to communicate with anyone
13 on land. Someone would have to be on deck at the wheel every minute, night and day. We
14 could expect to be sun-roasted, swamped by rogue waves and smacked by wayward
15 flying-fish.

16 Days before leaving, I sat up late at night talking with Torries, a friend from work. I confessed
17 to being terrified.

18 ‘I think it’s brilliant what you’re doing Andy,’ said Torries. ‘We all do. Everyone’s talking about
19 you.’

20 ‘That’s great, but – what am I doing, really? I now have no house, no income and I’m about
21 to sail across the Atlantic on an old, second-hand boat!’

22 We left Cape Town in a stiff breeze that whipped the tops of the glittering swells to dazzling
23 fragments of crystal in the bright sun; a pod of dolphins leaping and dancing on our bow
24 wave in celebration. Dael was on the foredeck leaning over the rail, almost touching them
25 as they swam. For once, he had no words. By night, it was my turn at the wheel. Guiding
26 the yacht as it thundered over the ocean under a full moon was an experience that etched
27 itself forever into my memory. Flying over the crests of the waves and soaring down the
28 valleys like an eagle, the exultant sense of liberation was absolute. Remembering Torries,
29 I laughed out loud.

30 ***

31 The coast just north of Ilhabela is punctuated by pretty, tranquil coves. Abi and I were looking
32 forward to a week of snorkelling and generally lounging about. But first, Dael pointed out, a
33 storm was coming. The channel between Ilhabela and the mainland is long and thin. Hills
34 on both sides provide the perfect funnel for the strong winds that develop over the South
35 Atlantic. The water is also very shallow, conducive to close, steep waves.

36 We went to bed early but didn’t sleep. At two in the morning, Josephine snapped her mooring
37 line. In the cockpit there was mayhem. Tarpaulins and ropes were flogging violently, and
38 the air seethed with driven rain and spray. Screaming winds made it impossible to
39 communicate as Josephine listed alarmingly, any chance of saving her sinking with every
40 wave slamming her side. Dael turned the ignition key after a backward glance and my heart
41 leapt with relief as the twenty-three-year-old engine roared into life. He gunned the throttle
42 with the wheel hard over, trying to twist Josephine round and up onto her keel. Battling,
43 Josephine heaved her bow across and pointed into the storm, taking the waves head-on
44 and shattering them into pellets of spray. Under full power, Josephine dragged herself from
45 the sand and came upright.

46 Next morning, we returned to our beach. High above the waterline lay the remains of another
47 eight-metre sailboat. Like us, she’d broken free in the storm and been rolled, split open and
48 dumped by the waves – a sobering reminder of the risks.
