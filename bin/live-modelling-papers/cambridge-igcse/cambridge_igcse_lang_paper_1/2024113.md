## Text A
**Title:** What are earthscrapers?
**Author:** Unknown

1  While ‘skyscraper’ is a well-known term for a very tall building that reaches up to the skies
2  from the ground, there is another term that is less well-known: ‘earthscraper’. Earthscrapers
3  are best described as inverted skyscrapers. They start at ground level and then extend
4  downwards an impressive distance from the surface.

5  Countless structures, such as car parks, arenas and laboratories, are buried underground,
6  but their shallow setting means that these are not earthscrapers. As far as we are aware,
7  no actual earthscrapers have been built yet. To date, these wondrous multifunctional
8  subterranean designs excite our imaginations only in fiction and architectural plans.

9  One proposal for an earthscraper was an entry to a Skyscraper Competition in 2012 by a
10 team of Mexican architects. The competition required entrants to design practical solutions
11 to the problems caused by restricted space and growing populations in cities. The ambitious
12 Mexican proposal imagined a ‘65-storey deep’ earthscraper placed beneath Mexico City’s
13 central square, with living, office and retail spaces as well as a 10-floor museum. This would
14 also conserve the city’s threatened historic buildings.

15 Building underground is no easy task, especially the deeper you go, where engineering
16 problems are potentially significant. Mexico has a hot climate and the immense glass top of
17 the earthscraper could well act as a greenhouse, so the provision of adequate ventilation
18 throughout the structure would need to be considered. The best in human ingenuity would
19 be demanded in teams both overseeing and implementing the project.

20 There are mixed feelings about constructing such earthscrapers in the world’s biggest cities.
21 Some experts speculate that they would be environmentally friendly and require very little
22 energy for heating and cooling, but this is not a commonly held view. Meanwhile governments,
23 who must weigh up spending priorities and the management of the inevitable increases in
24 traffic congestion and pollution above ground, have a lot to consider too.

## Text B
**Title:** Buying an underground house
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** In this text the narrator describes her family’s experiences when buying and living in an underground home.

1  We learnt quickly that a positive mindset was required when considering living underground.
2  Important things like researching locations and finding companies who offered home insurance
3  were challenging. We also soon realised that this was going to be much more expensive
4  than conventional above-land living. It wasn’t a scheme for the faint-hearted or those who
5  couldn’t invest a substantial amount of cash.

6  We were advised not to build our home in some splendidly remote hillside, but to find a
7  pre-built home in an underground community. This saddened us as we had indulged in a
8  romantic notion of complete independence and freedom, cut off from the hustle and bustle
9  of human life. It would have been a dream to design the house ourselves then oversee its
10 construction. But after reading online stories of people who’d thought like us and their house’s
11 subsequent leaks, mould and constant repairs, we sadly concluded that this was not a good
12 idea. After all, we reasoned, being part of a community would be more practical: advice and
13 a potential support network in case of problems were definite advantages.

14 So, when a couple living in an underground home needed to return to life above land for
15 family reasons, we jumped at the opportunity to buy their 2-year-old dwelling on the edge
16 of a smallish community of 20 similar homes. Being peripheral to the community rather than
17 at its heart seemed like a good compromise to my ideals. Not only that, but the homes in
18 the community also enjoyed an efficient drainage system, keeping them all soundly
19 water-proofed, while potential condensation issues had been resolved by effective insulation.
20 We could cross those concerns off our list.

21 We were so excited to move in! It was lovely. The huge dome-shaped glass entrance gave
22 us considerable natural light. People imagine that an underground home must be dark and
23 gloomy, but a good architect will ensure that this is not the case. There was also an open
24 area at the centre of the house, where we sat on warm evenings to watch the sky darken
25 above us. It was our own slice of the natural world, inaccessible to anyone else but us. How
26 thrilling!

27 We love wildlife. This is just as well, as taking up residence underground means you often
28 have these uninvited guests. To manage the situation, we installed a small pond for turtles
29 and the several varieties of frogs and toads that are found locally. Two king snakes - one of
30 which actually allows me to pet him - roam freely in one of the bedrooms and sometimes
31 burrow in a laundry basket.

## Text C
**Title:** Visiting Underground City
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The narrator is called Simon. He, Jodie and their 8-year-old son, Ben, are on holiday in a region called Tassos.

1  We descend the stairway into a dark labyrinth unfurling below us. The passage allows just
2  two visitors abreast, so we jostle shoulders and unforgiving walls. Lighting is muted: just a
3  few gentle torches discreetly hidden in unexpected crevices project alluring shades of tan,
4  gold and red onto the exposed stone walls. Now we are under the surface, the heat has also
5  dissipated: cold air pushes past us, a desperate thief escaping the scene of its crime.

6  ‘You may find the descent a bit difficult,’ our guide, Milo, says, ‘but think how much more
7  difficult it was without light and without someone like me showing you the way.’

8  Ben nods in silent agreement. He is impressed; I am glad we came.

9  This visit had been my idea earlier today. Ben had been lonely and, feeling guilty about
10 choosing a hotel without children’s activities, I’d googled ‘family-friendly trips’ on my phone.
11 Jodie had sniffed uncertainly when ‘Tassos’s thirteenth century Underground City’ showed.
12 ‘Oh, Simon, it’ll be stuffy in this heat,’ she’d complained.

13 Jodie was right about the heat outside. The sun had been unrelenting since our arrival, but
14 fortunately, most of Tassos is within a short distance of the coast. On our morning beach
15 visits, fresh breezes whipped the heaviness out of the air, while fun in the sea energised us.
16 But now, sitting in the still courtyard of our hotel, everywhere was parched and begged for
17 hydration. In front of me a dying tree had collapsed, its trunk riven at the base. Oddly, its
18 spindly uppermost branches still stretched skyward, while its lower boughs wept dry tears
19 into the cracked earth.

20 Jodie and I studied the Underground City website together. ‘Eight floors over 100 metres.
21 Friendly tour guide. Amazing!’ I said. ‘Let’s book it.’

22 The coach journey took an hour, and we were grateful that we’d paid the extra for an
23 air-conditioned vehicle.

24 Underground, the chill is pleasant. Jodie holds Ben’s hand firmly as we negotiate some
25 narrow twists and turns. Ahead, Milo warns us about a low roof, so we stoop slightly.

26 Then we enter a large chamber. Milo is waiting for us to gather round him. He smiles
27 expansively and beckons us closer. His eyes pick out Ben and a young girl, the only two
28 children present. ‘Do you know what this room was for?’ asks Milo.

29 Ben and the girl shake their heads, mouths open.

30 ‘It’s a school classroom,’ announces Milo. ‘The teacher probably stood where I am and the
31 children would sit on the floor. No desks, no whiteboards, definitely no computers or phones.’

32 Milo now addresses us all: ‘This was a sophisticated society. For safety reasons we can
33 only visit this area. You can see one of the many ventilation shafts above our heads and
34 there are wells like this one here to store fresh water. Families had their own living quarters –
35 a bit like modern day apartments. There were communal areas and shops.’

36 Half an hour later we resurface into the heat and board our coach back. Milo provides us
37 with refreshments, while Ben, sitting next to the girl he met in Underground City, is talking
38 animatedly. She’s called Anna. Anna and her mother are staying in a hotel that is not far
39 from ours and Jodie has already exchanged phone numbers with them. I notice how the
40 volcanic rock and soil that had made burrowing underground so easy once means that this
41 is not a land of fertile forests. A handful of lonely broken trees wither in the dry flat land as
42 far as the eye can see. An evening walk to see this open landscape under a vast sky is a
43 tempting idea. My camera would capture some spectacular panoramic vistas. Meanwhile,
44 Milo is telling us about a must-visit outdoor market selling local crafts. Not my favourite thing
45 to do, but Jodie looks excited. She and Anna’s mother exchange conspiratorial glances.
