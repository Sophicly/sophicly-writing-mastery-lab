## Text A
**Title:** Antarctica
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** This text gives information about Antarctica.

1  Antarctica is the fifth largest continent in terms of total area, larger than the continents
2  of both Oceania and Europe. The Antarctic Ice Sheet, the largest single piece of ice on
3  Earth, dominates the region. This ice sheet even extends beyond the continent when
4  snow and ice are at their most extreme in winter months.

5  The Antarctic region has an important role in global climate processes. It is an integral
6  part of the Earth’s heat balance (the relationship between the amount of solar heat
7  absorbed by Earth’s atmosphere and the amount of heat reflected back into space).

8  Ice is more reflective than land or water surfaces. The massive Antarctic Ice Sheet
9  reflects a large amount of solar radiation away from Earth’s surface. As global ice cover
10 (ice sheets and glaciers) decreases, the reflectivity of Earth’s surface also decreases.
11 This allows more incoming solar radiation to be absorbed by the Earth’s surface, causing
12 an unequal heat balance linked to global warming.

13 While Antarctica does not have permanent residents, the region is busy with research
14 scientists from dozens of different countries. Antarctica has no national borders, so the
15 entire continent is open for research. It has the cleanest air in the world, so atmospheric
16 monitoring done there provides more reliable data.

17 Most visitors to Antarctica are either increasingly adventurous tourists, on increasingly
18 affordable cruises, or researchers involved in national scientific programmes. In many
19 aspects the type of activities undertaken and the potential environmental impacts are
20 common to all visitors. Whatever their reason for being in Antarctica, these people will
21 want to visit the same spectacular scenery and witness threatened wildlife colonies.

22 Although nearly three times as many tourists visit Antarctica than researchers, the
23 number of person-days on the ground in Antarctica for national scientific programmes
24 far exceeds the number for tourism, which is nearly all ship based.

25 National programmes have involved the establishment of permanent or semi-permanent
26 stations, served by new roads and runways, and staffed by long-term (wintering) and
27 short-term (summer only) personnel. Only in recent years have environmental audits
28 carried out around scientific stations ensured that waste of all kinds is returned to the
29 country of origin wherever possible.

## Text B
**Title:** Crossing Antarctica
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** This text is an article about Wendy Searle who plans to break the women’s world speed record for skiing solo to the South Pole, unassisted and unaided – a huge challenge!

1  Five years ago, Wendy Searle hadn’t considered crossing Antarctica. Then she
2  organised the media campaign of an expedition to the South Pole by a team of military
3  personnel. It made her wonder if she could have a go herself.

4  Despite the fact that she had no previous polar training and had never skied before,
5  she successfully skied 560 kilometres across the Greenland ice sheet last year: ‘One
6  thing I learned is that my polar expedition will be incredibly tough, mentally.’

7  Searle will need to ski 1130 kilometres across the frozen continent of Antarctica in less
8  than 38 days, 23 hours and 5 minutes. ‘I can’t have any outside assistance and I have
9  to take everything with me in a special sledge called a pulk: all my food, my fuel, my
10 supplies, everything. I can’t have any food re-supplies or medical help. I cannot even
11 accept a cup of tea,’ she said. ‘I’ll be completely alone and, in all likelihood, I won’t see
12 anybody else.’

13 Searle is 1.53 metres tall and weighs just over 60 kilograms; the pulk will weigh over
14 80 kilograms. Training for the expedition, she needs to spend more than 10 hours a
15 week lifting weights and running up and down hills pulling a tyre, while also holding
16 down a full-time job. Knowing how difficult and dangerous the route is going to be forces
17 Searle to prioritise training: ‘That one day you don’t train might be the difference between
18 success and failure.’

19 Her family – her husband is a soldier and they have three teenage daughters and a
20 son – are doing all they can to support her. ‘My children are super-independent. They
21 even organise me. They’ll say, “Don’t forget parents’ evening and these are the
22 appointments I’ve got you.” They’re amazing.’ She’s determined to inspire them. ‘I want
23 my children to see that it’s OK to pursue something with a white-hot passion in a
24 single-minded way and focus on a goal.’

25 Searle is still raising money to fund the expedition. She feels it’s more difficult to attract
26 sponsorship as a female polar explorer: ‘People need to get behind female adventurers.
27 I asked a well-known TV agent why there aren’t more programmes about us and she
28 said, “Honestly, there just isn’t the audience.”’

29 Searle hopes her story will change perceptions.

## Text C
**Title:** Base station, Antarctica
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** This text is taken from a longer narrative. The narrator is a journalist who has been invited to spend time at a national scientific programme base station on Antarctica. She has travelled by ship with a new group of staff arriving at the base at the start of summer and is planning to stay long enough to experience the beginning of winter.

1  Base emitted a hushed grandeur, as if a vault door had been closed – all noise guarded
2  fiercely behind it. A gunshot-like crack echoed through the air – an iceberg rotating –
3  then a roar like a distant waterfall as another chunk of the ice shelf collapsed into the
4  bay. Rough squawks of skuas were magnified through the telescope of empty dry air.
5  There were no smells of soil, trees, nor grass. Only the scent of Antarctica – like two
6  stones rubbed together. Flint.

7  We passed truckloads of tinned-food boxes in huge hangars. In summer, Base could
8  accommodate a hundred people: scientists returning from tented field-camps or being
9  deployed to them, pilots flying them in and out, cooks, mechanics, even the occasional
10 VIP.

11 We were shown the laboratory, air-traffic control tower, stores where ski equipment
12 was repaired, generator hut and sewage treatment plant. It felt both exciting and daunting
13 to call this home for the next few months. There were no museums, cafés or restaurants.
14 No animals, Wi-Fi or children. No real strangers.

15 That evening we encountered more Base people – young, fit figures without that fume
16 of the outside that still clung to us. They sported the same padded boiler suits and
17 fleece jackets, and were keen to talk. ‘I don’t call the real world often, it’s best to just
18 live in the bubble,’ confided marine biologist Ben. I said I planned to stay until the very
19 last boat out. ‘Winter here’s long,’ he added.

20 Field training took days; we learned how to abseil into a crevasse, get ourselves out
21 and rescue someone who has fallen in. We hurled ourselves down the sheer face of a
22 glacier – to learn to break our fall with an ice-axe. We erected and dismantled pyramid
23 tents, cooked on camp-stoves, and learned how to load a sledge.

24 There wasn’t much time to think, let alone write, in the early weeks occupied by briefings,
25 obligatory group activities and trips into the bay to collect water samples.

26 Summer was term time. Each evening scientists gave informal lectures: scientists who
27 routinely dived beneath the ice among carnivorous leopard seals and orcas; scientists
28 in the ice-coring team who worked in an underground cavern, surfacing only to cook
29 and sleep in pyramid tents on the blasted plain of Berkner Island. I was learning more
30 in weeks than I had in entire years.

31 For the first time I began to think of the planet as an organism whose well-being I could
32 affect. Here I felt closer to it. I could hear its pulse. ‘In ice, depth equals time,’ explained
33 Xavier, a scientist working with the ice-coring team. ‘The further down you go, the further
34 back in time you go. The ice core shows that major changes have taken place within
35 Earth’s climate. There’s an irrefutable steady upward trend, directly linked to carbon
36 dioxide, and humans.’ I shivered involuntarily.

37 As winter approached, activities on Base diminished. For entertainment, our dwindling
38 group played cards or leafed through old magazines.

39 We were given new instructions by Base Commander Simon. We weren’t to walk off
40 Base without telling him first. The sea ice was hardening at the shore; fresh snowfalls
41 made it difficult to see where land stopped and sea began. We risked falling through.

42 Safety was taken seriously – endless rehearsals drilled into us how Antarctic tragedies
43 had more than one cause; mishaps or misjudgements which sealed people’s fate.

44 Temperatures dropped further. Snowstorms blanketed the runway. The last ship out
45 depended on the weather. People had overwintered unexpectedly here before.

46 Around us the world was hardening. In the bay it started as ice flowers, tiny crystal
47 formations. The creeping carpet of flowers knitted together, until soaked by seawater
48 it transformed into grey gruel, thickening into porridge. As temperatures plummeted,
49 porridge-ice welded itself together, and sounded like steel grinding. In gathering darkness
50 sea ice formed. The continent was closing in on itself, locking itself in. Winter was
51 beginning.
