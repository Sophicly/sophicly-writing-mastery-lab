## Text A
**Title:** About snakes
**Author:** Unknown

1  There are more than 3000 species of snakes on the planet. They’re found everywhere
2  except in Antarctica, Iceland, Ireland, Greenland, and New Zealand. About 600 species
3  are venomous, and only about seven per cent are able to kill or significantly wound a
4  human.

5  Most snakes live on land, but there are about 70 species that live in oceans. Sea snakes
6  and their cousins, kraits, are some of the most venomous snakes that exist, but they
7  pose little threat to humans because they’re shy and their fangs are too short to do
8  much damage.

9  Almost all snakes are covered in protective scales. They’re reptiles, so are cold-blooded
10 and must regulate their body temperature externally. Scales serve several purposes:
11 in arid climates they trap moisture and reduce friction as the snake moves.

12 Snakes also have forked tongues, which they flick in different directions to smell their
13 surroundings. That lets them know when danger—or food—is nearby.

14 Snakes have several other ways to detect a snack. Openings called pit holes in front
15 of their eyes sense the heat given off by warm-blooded prey. Bones in their lower jaws
16 pick up vibrations from rodents and other scurrying animals. When they do capture
17 prey, snakes can eat animals up to three times bigger than their head is wide because
18 their lower jaws unhinge from their upper jaws. Once in a snake’s mouth, the prey is
19 held in place by teeth that face inward, trapping it there.

20 Not quite as photogenic as pandas or polar bears, we hear much less about endangered
21 snakes than we do some other animals. But despite their remarkable evolutionary
22 adaptability, some snakes suffer the same fate as the more well-publicised species that
23 are considered endangered. Snakes aren’t hunted in great numbers and many live
24 without fear of predation, which generally leads conservationists to blame their decline
25 on environmental factors, mainly habitat destruction and climate change. Some snakes
26 are already extinct, more are critically endangered, including a number of sea snakes.
27 One theory claims changes in sea water temperatures may be a factor, but more
28 investigation is required.

## Text B
**Title:** Teaching people to hate snakes is a disaster for ecology
**Author:** Unknown

1  Did you know it’s World Snake Day on 16th July? We invite you to slither on down to
2  join the party, meet our snake experts and enjoy lots of fun snake-themed activities.

3  Humans often fear what they don’t understand. To most, snakes are a mystery. Snakes
4  rely on their ability to avoid detection, so we rarely see them. This leads to a void of
5  direct knowledge that’s filled by myth and media – portraying snakes as cold-blooded
6  killers and focusing on how dangerous some can be. Our aim is to tip the scales (excuse
7  the pun), educate and encourage guests to confront their fears.

8  During the event, there’ll be Zoo Chats – guests can get up close to, even touch, several
9  snake species. At 10:30 am, guests can watch the zoo’s largest snake resident – a
10 reticulated python – being weighed and measured. At midday, guests can venture over
11 to The Swamp to see and learn about recently hatched Grey-banded Kingsnake and
12 Jamaican Boa babies.

13 Though threatened by many of the same issues affecting other wildlife, including habitat
14 loss, climate change and disease, negative attitudes impeding efforts to address other
15 threats may be the biggest barrier to snake conservation.

16 In the United States, for example, public outcry based on fear and misinformation
17 recently halted a scientifically sound conservation plan for timber rattlesnakes. Another
18 project at the same location that involved releasing eagles was embraced by the
19 community. Rattlesnakes are no less important than eagles. In fact, they may help
20 reduce the incidence of Lyme disease, which affects thousands of people each year,
21 by reducing the number of rodents that harbour this disease. But emotions override
22 facts, it seems, where snakes are concerned.

23 Snakes play an integral role in maintaining balance in the ecosystem – in most
24 ecosystems on earth, snakes can be both predator and prey. When a large
25 prey-population attracts and sustains a large snake population, those snakes become
26 prey for birds, mammals and even other snakes! As predators, snakes keep
27 prey-populations in balance. Snakes provide an easy, environmentally friendly, free
28 and natural pest-control service.

29 But snakes are worth saving not because of what they can do for us, but because of
30 who they are. Snakes share many behaviours with us, behaviours we value. They have
31 friends. They take care of their kids and even their friends’ kids too.

32 Want to help us change how people view and treat snakes? Visit the World Snake Day
33 website.

## Text C
**Title:** Rainforest tales
**Author:** Unknown
**Context:** The writer of this online article is reviewing his guided visit around the Kalinga Centre for Rainforest Ecology (KCRE). Located near Agumbe, the five-acre site is nestled in the heart of the rainforest, in the Western Ghats mountain range.

1  The weather had been pleasant when we’d started off, but Agumbe, true to reputation,
2  welcomed us with a sudden downpour from nowhere. Visibility was low.

3  Though the camp site is equipped with solar power, we’d been told to carry fully charged
4  batteries for our cameras and torches. Telephone and internet connectivity is intermittent.
5  The closest hospital and pharmacy are 24km away. Huffing and puffing with our bags
6  and gear, we scrambled gratefully down the three flights of rocky steps added recently
7  to cater for less-experienced trekkers. At the bottom, we were greeted by a snoozing
8  Common Vine Snake, so inconspicuous that it took me quite some time of bobbing my
9  head to spot it. I still cherish that precise moment when I saw my first snake in the wild,
10 perfectly poised on its luxurious bed of green. I knew I would never behold snakes the
11 same way again.

12 After a sumptuous breakfast from our local host-family, we were keen and ready for
13 our first official rainforest walk. In the dining hall (also used for discussions and briefings)
14 our local guide handed me ugly brown socks that would be my saviours (though not a
15 100 per cent guarantee) from the leeches, reminding everyone this was not a tourist
16 resort. We needed to take care of our own belongings and ensure we kept electronic
17 equipment dry.

18 Three hours later, returning from the trek, I felt bubbles of amazement and wonder
19 rising. I’d seen gliding lizards fly effortlessly between trees, intricate dragonflies of infinite
20 varieties and delicately etched, golden frogs. The overcast sky, saturated to the brim,
21 had poured down heavily, drenching the forest, its native creatures, and the handful of
22 humans who happened to be there. Thereafter began the frenzy of activities and sounds
23 that engulfs the woods after a good rain – rhythmic sounds, musical, coordinated and
24 orchestrated, and pleasantly deafening.

25 Ah! My brimming heart and soothed soul enjoyed restful sleep in the tent that first night.
26 Bonfires and loud music are prohibited to avoid any disturbance to animals and hygienic
27 common bathrooms (with hot-water facilities) were appreciated. Everyone was expected
28 to wash their own plates and glasses after every meal. We were encouraged to separate
29 organic waste into the respective dustbins before retiring each night. All inorganic waste
30 went back with you.

31 Next morning revealed a forest blooming with fungi – a fairy-tale landscape of frilled
32 coral cups, delicate saucers and robust yellow umbrellas. And if that brightly coloured
33 confectionery wasn’t enough, a magnificent azure-coloured sphere, hard as shell, caught
34 our eye. No amount of speculation could have made me imagine that the little blue ball
35 was a millipede! We observed at a respectful distance, reminded that handling, agitating,
36 or disturbing any wildlife is not permitted and will warrant strict action. Collection of any
37 kind of flora or fauna is forbidden.

38 With no rains, the forest lay quieter that night, though croaking bush-frogs put their
39 vocal sacs to good use for our entertainment and a metre-long green Pit Viper hung
40 from a branch, as if casually posing for our photographs. My first ever ‘wildlife
41 photography’ attempt was fun. And I could start off with a Viper! I remembered advice
42 to exercise caution, control and care during observation and photography in the forest
43 to avoid stressing species or exposing them to danger.

44 The last walk on the final day through the rainforest, through leech territory, through
45 the sights and smells of moist earth and greens evoked an undefinable love in me. I’d
46 experienced the wonders of a rainforest, in a spell-binding weekend that drew to a close
47 with inspiring presentations about the place, its conservationist founder and his
48 tremendous work studying and saving magnificent King Cobras.

49 Ignorance had metamorphosed into sheer passion for creatures I’d been prejudiced
50 about. Curious to learn more about the life of such serpents and eager to accompany
51 experts on live snake-rescue calls, I’ve already signed up for the next workshop.
