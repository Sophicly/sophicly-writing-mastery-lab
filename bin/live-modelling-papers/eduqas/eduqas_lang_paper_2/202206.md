1  Knockout Punch
2  Mark Poulton’s Punch and Judy puppet show entertains the crowds every
3  summer on Weymouth seafront. He calls it his dream job, even though times
4  are harder now. Reporter Craig Arnott met up with him.
5  It’s Tuesday morning on Weymouth seafront and the sky is thick with grey clouds. I have only
6  strolled 50 yards when I bump into Weymouth’s Mr Punch and Judy man, Mark Poulton, who is one
7  of just three licensed Punch and Judy performers left in the UK. We’d met briefly the night before
8  when I watched his Monday evening show down on the beach. For the rest of the week, his shows
9  run four or five times daily throughout the summer.

10 As it threatens to rain, we dip
11 into a café where he tells me it’s
12 his dream job, the only one he
13 had ever wanted since watching
14 a Punch and Judy show with his
15 mother as a 5-year-old child. “We
16 came here for a week’s holiday,”
17 he explains. “I watched every
18 performance that week. As soon
19 as I got home, I transformed my
20 teddies into puppets and set up
21 my own show in the back garden.”
22 When he was just 16 he secured
23 his first summer season at
24 Llandudno, before taking his show
25 all over the country, and then being
26 invited to perform on Weymouth’s
27 sands in 2005.

28 He’s a master of his craft, having taught himself to carve his wooden puppets and design and make
29 their costumes. He says that Weymouth has had a Punch and Judy show since 1880, making it
30 an important part of the town’s history. “And I’m now a part of the town’s history and tradition too.
31 During the summer I draw big crowds,” he tells me proudly. “In fact, many people tell me they come
32 to Weymouth purely to see the rare sight of a Punch and Judy show still being performed on the
33 beach.” He looks up and smiles, a man in love with his work and with Weymouth. “It’s the perfect
34 home for Punch and Judy shows,” he says. “With its safe, sandy beach people are happy to come
35 and sit on the sand, but there are always people up on the seafront enjoying the show too.”

36 Now 48 years old, Mark knows that being Weymouth’s Punch and Judy man won’t make him rich
37 but that doesn’t put him off. “I love the thrill of the work and living off your wits. You’re constantly
38 pushing to find new routines that will attract an audience.” His seaside show is one of the last to
39 rely on audiences giving a donation when his assistant goes around with the collecting box.

40 Although his shows often draw audiences of over 200, he tells me that in 2016 he had threatened
41 to end his beach performances because of the poor behaviour of some children. “Obviously I
42 encourage their participation in the show, and I want them to shout and yell, but I had kids throwing
43 stones at the show and the parents just sat there and let them do it. I even had adults sunbathing
44 in front of the show, so people who wanted to watch couldn’t sit there. They refused to pay to watch
45 the show and some of the children were trying to deliberately stop the show by banging on the
46 show booth. I just became fed up and at the time it kind of destroyed my faith in humanity. I just
47 got a load of verbal abuse from the kids and their parents did nothing.” Fortunately for Weymouth,

## Source B
**Title:** London Labour and the London Poor
**Author:** Unknown (1851)
**Context:** Punch and Judy shows were popular in Victorian England. In the book, ‘London Labour and the London Poor’, published in 1851, a man who owned and performed a Punch and Judy puppet show gave an account of his work.

1  I am the owner of a Punch and Judy show, and I perform inside the booth behind the green cloth
2  front. I have a partner now to play the drum to let people know the performance is soon to start.
3  He stands outside the booth whilst I am performing inside it and he collects the money afterwards.
4  I have been five-and-twenty years now at the business and in the early days I made five pounds
5  regular but a good day now seldom gets beyond five shillings. That shows how times have
6  changed. When we perform a show in the street, we take on average only threepence.

7  We sometimes make better money from private shows. The best hours for these are in the morning
8  from nine till ten, because the children are at home, before they go out with the maids for a walk.
9  We do best in wet weather. You see, the wet keeps the children indoors all day, and then they need
10 something to quiet ’em a bit; and the mothers and fathers, to pacify the dears, pay us to perform
11 in the house. Sometimes, even if the mothers of the dear little children have given them an extra
12 penny to give us, well, the maids take it from them and keep it to buy fancy ribbons.

13 Monday is the best day for street performances; Friday is no day at all, because then the poor
14 people have spent all their money. We perform about twenty shows in a day. We start on our
15 rounds early in the morning and remain out till dark at night. We generally walk from twelve to
16 twenty miles every day, and carry the booth, which weighs a good half-hundredweight. After this
17 great exertion, my voice very often fails me, from speaking all day through.

18 I used to attend very wealthy gentlemen’s houses for their
19 children’s parties and get as much as two pounds for the
20 performance. Lord Townsend was particular partial to me
21 and I was booked regular for his children. But the business
22 gets slacker and slacker every season. Where I went to ten
23 children’s parties twenty years ago, I don’t go to two now.
24 People aren’t getting tired of our performances, but stingier –
25 that’s it. Everybody looks at their money now afore they part
26 with it, and haggle us down to shillings and sixpences instead
27 of pounds in the olden time.

28 When we do a street show, of those who watch us, soldiers
29 never have any money and when a gentleman stops to watch,
30 his footman will take some of what his master sends to pay
31 us. But the boys are the greatest nuisances we have to
32 contend with. Wherever we go we are sure of plenty of boys
33 for a hindrance. Why, the boys are such an obstruction to
34 our performance, that often we are obliged to stop the show
35 because of ’em. They’ll throw one another’s caps into the
36 booth while I’m inside it, and we can’t keep ’em from poking
37 their fingers through the cloth and making holes to peep
38 through. But the worst of all is, the most of ’em ain’t got a
39 halfpenny to bless themselves with. Bother ’em!
