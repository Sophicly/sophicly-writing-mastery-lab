## Source A
**Title:** We save people. It’s just our job
**Author:** Louise France
**Context:** The separate Resource Material for use with Section A is a newspaper article, ‘We save people. It’s just our job’, by Louise France. Read the newspaper article, ‘We save people. It’s just our job’ in the separate Resource Material.

1  ‘We save people. It’s just our job’
2  Last year, Aileen Jones became the first woman since 1888 to be awarded a
3  medal for bravery by the Royal National Lifeboat Institution (RNLI) when she
4  rescued two fishermen in a force eight gale. Reporter Louise France met up
5  with her.

6  You would never guess, walking along Porthcawl’s seafront, that this is one of the
7  most dangerous stretches of coastline in the world. But behind the volunteer running
8  the Royal National Lifeboat stall, a notice states: “This station has saved 10 lives
9  since January 2005.”

10 To be honest, neither would you guess that Aileen Jones,
11 the slightly built, modest woman I met, is a local hero
12 (a description she would most definitely wince at). This
13 42-year-old part-time teaching assistant and mother
14 of two is rather embarrassed about the attention she’s
15 received since 24 August 2004, when, in force eight winds
16 and 10-foot waves, she was in charge of the lifeboat that
17 rescued two fishermen. She has now become the first
18 woman to be awarded an RNLI medal in 116 years.

19 Aileen, like others in the RNLI, is always ready to run to
20 answer her alarm pager during the day or in the middle of
21 the night. And, as she explains over several mugs of tea,
22 she can think of nowhere else she’d rather be than out on
23 the lifeboat.

24 Why does she do it? “It’s something that I want to do. It’s good. We save people. It’s
25 just our job.” But doesn’t she get frightened? “If you’re scared, then you’re not the
26 right person to do it. It’s nerve-racking but at the same time you want to do it.”

27 For a week last August, the weather was unseasonably stormy. As she arrived
28 home from school at 11am, wondering what to cook her children for lunch, there
29 was a radio call for help from a boat in distress. “As soon as my alarm pager went
30 off, I dropped everything,” she remembers. “I knew a lot of people were at work, so I
31 rushed to the lifeboat station. The rule is, if you’re first to arrive at the lifeboat station,
32 you’ve got the responsibility of taking the boat out in those conditions, so that’s what
33 I did.”

34 All they knew was that two fishermen, one of them with an injured arm, were
35 somewhere near Nash Sands in their boat, Gower Pride. “It wasn’t a nice place to
36 be. The boat’s engines had failed and none of the anchors would hold. Whatever the
37 sea decided to do to them, the fishermen had no control over it.”

38 Meanwhile, the lifeboat was frequently thrown airborne by the waves. Aileen recalls,
39 “I came around the sandbank and thought we would not get close enough to the
40 boat. It was just too rough.” Time and again, they tried to throw a rope over to the
41 Gower Pride skipper, Gordon Woosnam. Finally, they managed it and began to
42 drag the boat away from the sandbank, despite the fact that they were being tossed
43 vertically in the water. “Imagine a seesaw,” says Aileen matter-of-factly, although
44 I’m sure this doesn’t convey the horror of it. But then, just as they were making
45 progress, the knot for the towline came undone and they were back to square one.

46 By this time, Gordon had refused to go on to the front of the boat because it was
47 too dangerous. Aileen made the decision to
48 try to manoeuvre the boat close enough so
49 that one of the lifeboat crew, Simon Emms,
50 could scramble on board. He waited for a gap
51 between the 10-foot-high waves and managed
52 to clamber across. Hanging on for dear life, he
53 edged towards the front of the boat and finally
54 attached another rope so the lifeboat could begin
55 to tow the Gower Pride back to safety. The whole
56 rescue lasted three-and-a-half hours.

57 How did she feel when she reached dry land? “You go through it in your mind – did I
58 do the right thing? You can’t get emotional about it. It’s what we do, what we’re here
59 for. It was just a bit rougher than average.”

60 Aileen may make light of it but three months later her bravery was rewarded by the
61 RNLI. A spokesman for the RNLI said, “Mrs Jones used all her skills in leadership
62 and boat handling in very dangerous conditions. Coupled with her own personal
63 attributes of selflessness and bravery, Mrs Jones and her crew were able to save
64 two lives.”

## Source B
**Title:** New York Tribune (1869)
**Author:** Unknown (1869)
**Context:** Ida Lewis lived with her family at the Lime Rock lighthouse in Newport, America and became famous for the many rescues she carried out over many years. The article below appeared in the New York Tribune newspaper in 1869.

1  Two weeks ago, towards the end of a stormy, cold March afternoon, the brave daughter of
2  the Lime Rock lighthouse keeper in Newport harbour performed a deed of great heroism,
3  although quite unwell at the time and suffering from a severe cold. The heroine of this
4  daring rescue was Ida Lewis, whose first rescue dates back to when she was only 17. On
5  that occasion, four lads capsized a small boat and were in imminent danger of perishing.
6  When Ida saw their situation, she set out in her rowboat and succeeded in picking them all
7  up and taking them safely back to shore.

8  Ida Lewis’ most recent rescue saved the lives of two soldiers. The rain fell that day in
9  blinding torrents, and a gale drove the waves across the harbour with a fury that taxed
10 the full strength and skill of the most experienced boatmen. In the midst of this storm, a
11 reckless boy, scarcely fourteen years old, had somehow gained possession of one of the
12 smallest and most unsafe sail-boats in the harbour. He then persuaded two soldiers to let
13 him sail them across from Newport to Fort Adams where they were stationed. Anxious
14 to avoid the dreary three-mile tramp by land in the ferocious storm, and believing the lad
15 could manage the boat, they trusted themselves to him.

16 Halfway across the harbour a sudden squall struck the sail and the boat capsized in an
17 instant, throwing all three into the foaming water, the huge waves rolling the boat over twice
18 and then dashing against the upturned boat. For a long half-hour the luckless occupants
19 clung desperately to the boat and wrestled against the rain and the fierce salt waves with
20 all the energy of despair; but finally the boy’s strength was exhausted, his hold relaxed and
21 he was gone, swept away in an instant.

22 Fortunately for the two men, Ida’s mother, looking out to sea from the lighthouse, had
23 seen the perilous situation the soldiers were in, clinging to the small boat in an exhausted
24 condition. She immediately reported the fact to Ida, who rushed outside without covering
25 on her head or feet and grabbed a coil of rope. The sea was heavy and a fearful gale was
26 raging, but jumping into her boat with little thought of her own safety, she called to her
27 younger brother to go with and aid her. In the perilous conditions the management of the
28 boat was extremely difficult, even to an experienced oarswoman like Ida.

29 In the water, fast becoming paralysed with cold and almost bereft of hope, the two soldiers
30 saw no choice but to clasp each other in a last embrace and sink to a watery grave –
31 when suddenly they saw the little rowboat, driven by Ida’s rapid strokes, coming over the
32 bounding waves toward them. On came Ida’s boat and as it reached the men, the quick-
33 witted woman turned the boat with a well-timed stroke, backing it up so that one man was
34 pulled to safety over the back of the boat by Ida’s younger brother. With another strong
35 backward pull on the oars, the other soldier was lifted aboard. The next moment, the boat
36 with its freight of rescued lives was scudding swiftly through the spray back to the Lime
37 Rock again. One of the soldiers was able to stagger ashore but the other had to be carried
38 into the lighthouse from where they were safely conveyed to Fort Adams the next day.
