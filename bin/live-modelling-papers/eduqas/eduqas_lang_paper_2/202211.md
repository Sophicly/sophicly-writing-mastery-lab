2  Jumbo, the giant Victorian sensation.

4  Jumbo the elephant outshone the biggest names of music hall and

7  about the elephant.

8  Jumbo the elephant drew
9  millions of visitors, became a

16 Now Sir David Attenborough has
17 become the latest researcher
18 to try to uncover the truth about
19 Jumbo, the “gentle giant”. Jumbo stood four metres tall and weighed more than
20 seven tons. The skeleton of the elephant is kept at the American Museum of Natural
21 History in New York and Sir David was given unique access to it during the making
22 of a BBC documentary.

23 Using the latest techniques, Attenborough and a team of scientists painstakingly
24 examined Jumbo’s bones. And they have come to some fascinating conclusions
25 about Jumbo’s medical condition and diet.

26 The elephant arrived at London Zoo in 1865. “Hardly anyone in Europe had seen
27 a living African elephant 150 years ago,” Sir David points out. Jumbo became an
28 instant hit, attracting crowds of awe-struck Victorians who came to marvel at his
29 growing size, take rides on his back and take photos using early cameras. Among
30 those who are said to have adored Jumbo were Queen Victoria’s children and a
31 young Winston Churchill.

32 His keeper was Matthew Scott, who developed a strong bond with Jumbo. It was
33 even claimed he discovered a way of communicating with the animal. The young
34 elephant was disease-ridden when he arrived in London, but Scott nursed him back
35 to health and did his best to treat him well. He even slept in the elephant’s pen to
36 keep him company. Yet Scott knew little about elephant behaviour and away from
37 the crowds there was a darker side to Jumbo. He would have terrifyingly violent
38 rages, where he would smash the wooden frame of his enclosure. “He did it so often
39 that carpenters had to be called again and again to make repairs,” says Sir David.

40 He even broke his tusks in his frustration. Scientists now believe the reason for his
41 rages was bad toothache caused by his monotonous diet, largely of sticky buns
42 and hay. They found clear signs of damage to his teeth and indications of infection.
43 Matthew Scott was also said to feed Jumbo whisky and beer to keep him calm,
44 adding to his health problems. It is also known that 300 coins were discovered in
45 Jumbo’s stomach after his death – probably payment for rides which the elephant
46 scooped up and swallowed.

47 Photographs from the time show as many as a dozen people on his back and the
48 rides also caused some injury. Jumbo suffered joint problems. His bones were
49 shown to be like those of an animal in its 50s, rather than in his mid-20s as he was
50 when he died.

51 There’s no evidence that he ever
52 attacked anyone but zoo officials
53 became increasingly worried.
54 They feared that he would turn
55 on Scott, or even worse, a child,
56 causing a scandal, so when,
57 in 1882, the Barnum Circus
58 in America offered £2,000 for
59 Jumbo, an enormous sum at the
60 time, they found London Zoo in a
61 mood to accept.

62 “It was an opportunity for the
63 zoo to get rid of its problematic
64 elephant,” says Sir David. “A
65 shocking decision was made to
66 sell.”

67 There was an outcry – 100,000 children wrote to Queen Victoria begging her to
68 intervene, adults protested at the zoo – but there was no reprieve. Jumbo was put
69 in a crate and shipped across the Atlantic with his loyal keeper. Huge crowds were
70 waiting and Jumbo-mania erupted.

71 His popularity never faded but he died just three years later. While exercising at a
72 railway yard in Canada, during a circus tour, Jumbo was struck by a train and killed.
73 He was aged just 24, a tragically young age for an animal that can live to 70 in the
74 wild. Matthew Scott is said to have wept uncontrollably as his best friend took his
75 last breath.

## Source B
**Title:** Matthew Scott’s autobiography
**Author:** Matthew Scott
**Context:** When Jumbo the elephant was brought to England and to London Zoo in 1865, he was looked after by Matthew Scott, his keeper. The passage below is taken from Scott’s autobiography, published in 1885.

1  When I first saw Jumbo and he was given into my care, the poor thing was full of
2  disease, and his whole hide covered with sores. However, I undertook to be his doctor,
3  his nurse and general servant. I watched and nursed him night and day with all the care
4  and affection of a mother and I am now rewarded by having a fine-looking creature in a
5  perfectly sound state of mind and body. He is the largest, most intelligent and certainly
6  the most powerful living creature in creation. Jumbo’s daily diet is composed of hay,
7  oats and bread; his drink is chiefly water but as a treat he can cope with a big dram
8  of whisky. He weighs nearly eight tons and he stands today over twelve feet high. All
9  the experience I share with him is a pleasure to me and is a great reward, which I am
10 thankful for in these, my older days but so long as I am permitted the company of dear
11 old Jumbo, I shall be quite contented. I am happy in his company and do not wish to
12 leave it.

13 Jumbo has been a busy, industrious creature all his life, especially since I brought him
14 from his sick bed. He has no idle days for ‘loafing’ or otherwise wasting his time. He has
15 been engaged in carrying around children almost daily for twenty years, and I suppose
16 no animal has ever carried so many on his back as Jumbo. Certainly, I can claim
17 for him that no animal ever did his work more affectionately or tenderly, and without
18 accident. Once when I was riding him around in the Zoological Gardens, sitting on his
19 neck with about a dozen children on his broad back, we were proceeding down the
20 path when all at once Jumbo came to a standstill. I shouted to him to go along but for
21 once he did not obey the order. As I turned round to see what was the matter, there was
22 a lady running on to the path screaming and shouting, “Oh, my poor child! Oh, he will
23 be killed, he will be killed!” When I looked down, I saw Jumbo deliberately and coolly
24 putting his trunk around the body of an infant that had escaped its mother’s apron-
25 strings and had run and fallen in front of him. He just stopped right there, gently picked
26 up the child by the waist with his trunk and laid it beside its screaming mother, more
27 tenderly than the mother afterward took up the frightened child in her excitement.

28 Jumbo never gets excited when he is attending to children, who love to see him eat
29 the cakes they give him. He might get mad once in a while when provoked by some
30 drunken fool but I want you to think of Jumbo as a kind, affectionate creature and
31 remember, when you see anyone in trouble, you should go instantly to the rescue, and
32 remember that Jumbo did the same thing.
