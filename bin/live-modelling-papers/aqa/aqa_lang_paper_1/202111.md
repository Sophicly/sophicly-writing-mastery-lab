## Source A
**Title:** Half of a Yellow Sun
**Author:** Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie (2006)
**Context:** 21st Century prose fiction. An extract from the beginning of a novel published in 2006. This extract is taken from the opening of Chimamanda Adichie’s novel Half of a Yellow Sun, set in Nigeria in 1960. Ugwu, a thirteen-year-old boy, is starting work as a cleaner for a university professor in the city.

1  Master was a little crazy; he had spent too many years reading books overseas, talked to
2  himself in his office, did not always return greetings, and had too much hair. Ugwu’s aunty
3  said this in a low voice as they walked on the path. ‘But he is a good man,’ she added.
4  ‘And as long as you work well, you will eat well. You will even eat meat every day.’

5  Ugwu did not believe that anybody, not even this master he was going to live with, ate meat
6  every day. He did not disagree with his aunty, though, because he was too choked with
7  expectation, too busy imagining his new life away from the village. They had been walking
8  for a while now, since they got off the lorry at the motor park, and the afternoon sun burned
9  the back of his neck. But he did not mind. He was prepared to walk hours more in even
10 hotter sun. He had never seen anything like the streets that appeared after they went past
11 the university gates, streets so smooth and tarred that he itched to lay his cheek down on
12 them. He would never be able to describe to his sister Anulika how the bungalows here
13 were painted the colour of the sky and sat side by side like polite well-dressed men, how
14 the hedges separating them were trimmed so flat on top that they looked like tables
15 wrapped with leaves.

16 His aunty walked faster, her slippers making slap-slap sounds that echoed in the silent
17 street. Ugwu wondered if she, too, could feel the coal tar getting hotter underneath,
18 through her thin soles. They went past a sign, ODIM STREET, and Ugwu mouthed street,
19 as he did whenever he saw an English word that was not too long.

20 He smelt something sweet, heady, as they walked into a compound, and was sure it came
21 from the white flowers clustered on the bushes at the entrance. The bushes were shaped
22 like slender hills. The lawn glistened. Butterflies hovered overhead.

23 ‘I told Master you will learn everything very fast’ his aunty said. Ugwu nodded attentively
24 although she had already told him the story of how his good fortune came about: while she
25 was sweeping the corridor in the Mathematics Department a week ago, she heard Master
26 say that he needed a houseboy to do his cleaning, and she immediately said she could
27 help, speaking before his typist or office messenger could offer to bring someone.

28 ‘I will learn fast, Aunty,’ Ugwu said. He was staring at the car in the garage; a strip of metal
29 ran around its blue body like a necklace.

30 ‘Remember, what you will answer whenever he calls you is Yes, sah!’

31 ‘Yes, sah!’ Ugwu repeated.

32 They were standing before the glass door. Ugwu held back from reaching out to touch the
33 cement wall, to see how different it would feel from the mud walls of his mother’s hut that
34 still bore the faint patterns of moulding fingers. For a brief moment, he wished he were
35 back there now, in his mother’s hut, under the dim coolness of the thatch roof; or in his
36 aunty’s hut, the only one in the village with a corrugated-iron roof.

37 His aunty tapped on the glass. Ugwu could see the white curtains behind the door. A voice
38 said, in English, ‘Yes? Come in.’

39 They took off their slippers before walking in. Ugwu had never seen a room so wide.
40 Despite the brown sofas arranged in a semi-circle, the side tables between them, the
41 shelves crammed with books, and the centre table with a vase of red and white plastic
42 flowers, the room still seemed to have too much space. Master sat in an armchair, wearing
43 a vest and a pair of shorts. He was not sitting upright but slanted, a book covering his face,
44 as though oblivious that he had just asked people in.

45 ‘Good afternoon, sah! This is the child,’ Ugwu’s aunty said.

46 Master looked up. He pulled off his glasses. ‘The child?’

47 ‘The houseboy, sah. He will work hard,’ his aunty said. ‘He is a very good boy. Thank,
48 sah!’

49 Master grunted in response, watching Ugwu and his aunty with a faintly distracted
50 expression, as if their presence made it difficult for him to remember something important.
51 Ugwu’s aunty patted Ugwu’s shoulder, whispered that he should do well, and turned to the
52 door.

53 Ugwu stood by the door, waiting.

## Q2

He did not disagree with his aunty, though, because he was too choked with expectation, too busy imagining his new life away from the village. They had been walking for a while now, since they got off the lorry at the motor park, and the afternoon sun burned the back of his neck. But he did not mind.
