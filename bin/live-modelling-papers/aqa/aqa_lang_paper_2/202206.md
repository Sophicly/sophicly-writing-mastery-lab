## Source A
**Title:** The Tent, The Bucket and Me
**Author:** Emma Kennedy (2009)
**Context:** 21st Century literary non-fiction. An extract from an autobiography, published in 2009. Source A is an extract from The Tent, The Bucket and Me in which Emma Kennedy describes her camping holidays in France in the 1970s.

1  ‘You know,’ said my mother who, as far as I could tell, was the only person delighted to be back
2  in France, ‘we should treat this holiday as the occasion it is. There’s no point in being
3  miserable. Holidays are what you make them.’

4  Holidays were not what you made them. Holidays were in the hands of malevolent forces
5  hell-bent on wreaking chaos at every turn. Holidays were assault courses of the mind and
6  body, endurance tests designed to break spirits and shatter spleens. In my nine years on the
7  planet I had learnt one thing: going on holiday was awful. As we sat, chugging along through
8  the French countryside, sunflowers in the fields on either side of us, I thought, ‘Yes, it IS nice to
9  look at. But in the same way that cheese looks nice in a mousetrap.’

10 Eventually we arrived at the campsite where we had stayed the previous year. As is often the
11 way when you revisit somewhere you’ve been before, the allure was not quite as sparkling.
12 The table tennis hut, once such an astonishment of riches, was now a bit battered around the
13 edges, the pool a little more dull. Even my mother was forced to concede that the place had
14 lost its gloss. ‘This isn’t quite as nice as I remember it,’ she said, hands on hips. ‘Still, at least
15 it’s a bit cooler. What a relief!’

16 ‘Storm clouds gathering over there,’ said Dad, looking up to the west. ‘That’ll explain the drop
17 in temperature. Still, I’ll get the tent up.’

18 Our pitch backed on to a line of trees that acted as a windbreaker between us and the river. I
19 wandered off, tiptoeing through the branches to stand at the water’s edge. The low evening
20 sun was casting a pink tinge across the water and dragonflies were hovering. Picking up a
21 round, flat stone I skimmed it across the surface of the lake and watched with satisfaction as it
22 bounced away. Sometimes, it was the simplest things that provided the greatest pleasure and
23 as I stood, throwing stone after stone, I felt real contentment as if I were actually enjoying
24 myself.

25 I returned to our pitch, having been called to supper by my mother. Dad was staring skywards.
26 ‘Those clouds are shifting,’ he said, ‘we might get some rain after all.’

27 ‘I can’t remember the last time I saw rain,’ answered my mother, ‘must be well over a month.
28 It’ll be nice. Clear the air.’

29 Suddenly, there was a squall of activity all over the campsite as the sky darkened and the rain
30 began to fall in thick, steady drops. Caravan awnings were being winched in, windows
31 slammed shut, towels were being hastily gathered and everywhere, families were retreating to
32 the inside of their tents. Because the ground was so dry, the patter of rain on the hard earth
33 sounded almost metallic and each raindrop sparked up a plume of dust so fine it looked like
34 steam, making the soil look as if it were boiling. In the distance, a low rumble of thunder began
35 rolling towards us, the starter flag for any decent storm, and the rain which had an individual
36 and random quality became more pack-like, shifting shapes like a flock of starlings. The storm
37 was circling the area before clattering in to do its worst. Soon, the rain was slashing down, the
38 relentless battering against the tent canvas loud and frightening.

39 Despite all my father’s best efforts to waterproof the tent and lay the ground sheet properly,
40 water was starting to seep in. The ground, dry from so many weeks without moisture, couldn’t
41 cope with the sudden onslaught and the campsite was rapidly turned into a series of streaming
42 rivers. Not wanting to get our bedding wet, we bundled our sleeping bags together, placing
43 them on top of the camping table just outside the sleeping compartment. With nothing to sleep
44 in, and the water ever rising, Dad placed my air bed on top of their air bed and we sat, huddled
45 together, knees against our chests. As the storm fractured the skies, we clung together,
46 terrified.

47 Despite a small but intense gnawing in my chest, there was something deliciously spine-tingling
48 about being trapped inside the tent while hell rained itself down on me.

## Source B
**Title:** In the Wilderness
**Author:** Charles Dudley Warner (1878)
**Context:** 19th Century non-fiction. An extract from a book, published in 1878. Source B is an extract from In The Wilderness, written in 1878 by the American writer Charles Dudley Warner. At this time, some Americans were looking for adventure by camping in the wild.

1  The real enjoyment of camping in the woods lies in a return to primitive conditions of living,
2  dress and food and an escape from civilization. It is wonderful to see how easily the limits of
3  society fall off.

4  When our campers come to the bank of a lovely lake where they hope to enter the primitive life,
5  everything is beautiful and unspoilt. There is a point of land jutting into the lake, sloping down
6  to a sandy beach, on which the waters idly lap. The forest is untouched by the axe; ranks of
7  slender fir trees are marshalled by the shore. The discoverers of this paradise, which they have
8  entered to destroy, note the babbling of the stream that flows close at hand; they hear the
9  splash of the leaping fish. They listen to the sweet song of the evening birds, and the chatter of
10 the red squirrel, who angrily challenges their right to be there.

11 The site for a shelter is selected. The whole group is busy with the foundation of a new home.
12 The axes resound in the echoing spaces; great trunks fall with a crash; views are opened
13 towards the lake and the mountains. The spot for the shelter is cleared of underbrush; forked
14 stakes are driven into the ground, cross-pieces are laid on them, and poles sloping back to the
15 ground. In an incredible space of time there is the skeleton of a house, which is entirely open in
16 front. The roof and sides must be covered. For this purpose, the trunks of great spruce trees
17 are skinned. It needs but a few of these skins to cover the roof; and they make a perfectly
18 water-tight roof, except when it rains.

19 Later, whilst we eat supper, a drop or two of rain falls. The sky darkens; the wind rises; there is
20 a kind of shiver in the woods. We scud away into the shelter, taking the remains of our supper,
21 eating it as best we can. The rain increases. The fire sputters and fumes. All the trees are
22 dripping, dripping, and the ground is wet. We cannot step outdoors without getting a drenching.
23 Like sheep, we are penned in the little hut, where no one can stand upright. The rain swirls into
24 the open front and wets the bottom of the blankets. We curl up in our sleeping rows and try to
25 enjoy ourselves. How much better off we are than many a shelter-less wretch!

26 However, as we are dropping off to sleep, somebody unfortunately notes a drop of water on his
27 face. He moves his head to a dry place. Then he feels a dampness in his back and he finds a
28 puddle of water soaking through his blanket. By this time, somebody inquires if it is possible
29 that the roof leaks. One man has a stream of water under him; another says it is coming into
30 his ear. The roof appears to be a discriminating sieve. Those who are dry see no need of such
31 a fuss. The man in the corner spreads his umbrella, and the protective measure is resented by
32 his neighbour. In the darkness there is recrimination. The rain continues to soak down. The
33 fire is only half alive. The bedding is damp. Some sit up, if they can find a dry spot to sit on,
34 and smoke. A few sleep. And the night wears on.

35 The morning opens cheerless. The sky is still leaking and so is the shelter. The roof is patched
36 up. Even if the storm clears, the woods are soaked. There is no chance of going out. The
37 world is only ten feet square.

38 This life, without responsibility or clean clothes, may continue as long as the camper desires.
39 Some would be happy to live in this free fashion forever, in rain or sun, but there are others who
40 cannot exist more than three days without their worldly baggage. These campers will soon
41 leave and the abandoned camp is a melancholy sight.

42 The woods have been despoiled; the stumps are ugly; the bushes are scorched; the
43 pine-leaf-strewn earth is trodden into mud; the ground is littered with all the unsightly debris of a
44 hand-to-hand life. The dismantled shelter is a shabby object; the charred and blackened logs,
45 where the fire blazed, suggest the extinction of life. Man has wrought his usual wrong upon
46 Nature.
