## First — the one term you asked about

**SRSD = Self-Regulated Strategy Development** (Graham & Harris). It is the most strongly evidenced
method in adolescent writing instruction — for struggling 11–16 writers it roughly doubles the
effect of anything else we could add. In plain words: instead of giving a student help, you give
them a **short, memorisable strategy** — a handful of questions they ask themselves — practise it
until it's automatic, then step back. The point is that the strategy goes **with them**: into the
next beat, the next draft, and the exam hall. The exam hall is the ultimate no-AI environment, so
your "what if the servers are down?" question and "what do they do in the exam?" turn out to be the
same question, with the same answer.

## The recommendations

**1. One question per message.** Your own call, and the research is unambiguous for this age group
(working memory is still developing to ~15; the second half of a two-part question falls out of
their head while they answer the first — your own run proved it). The arc ask becomes three short
turns: confirm the opening → ask the closing → then the arc.

**2. The "two ends" recap appears once, ever.** Never four times. A return visit gets two lines and
a way to see the full text if wanted. Anything you wrote over ~30 words is quoted back to you at
most once — after that the chat points at the document instead of re-printing it.

**3. "Step 1 of 105" disappears.** The chat only ever says where you are in the *stage* ("beat 3 of
20"). The honest big total lives in the sidebar for anyone who wants it. Near a stage's end it
flips to "2 left in this stage" — the framing that measurably keeps people moving.

**4. First time a student meets a new kind of beat: a finish-the-sentence task, not a blank page.**
You liked this one. Stem + one requirement, they complete it. Later beats of the same kind open up.

**5. Examples: two short ones from different stories, then "what do these share?"** — NOT more
examples. ⭐ *Your ruling applied:* this is **not** because imitation is bad — you're right, Romeo
and Juliet is a retelling, and a student retelling a shape they love is doing what Shakespeare did.
The reason is narrower: one example sitting right beside the question gets its surface details
transcribed into that one answer, which teaches nothing. Two contrasted examples teach the *move*,
which is exactly what a student needs to retell **well**. Derivative story choices are never
penalised anywhere.

**6. Every kind of beat gets a pocket strategy — 3 or 4 self-questions.** Your practicality worry
is answered by one fact: there are ~100 beats but only **about seven kinds** of beat (opening
image, pressure/mask, turning point, trial, ally/enemy, revelation, final image). Seven small
question-sets, reused everywhere — not 110. The same four questions every "trial" beat, until
they're automatic. That's SRSD, and it's what they carry into the exam.

**7. Every writing ask ends with a self-check the student runs on their own sentence** — the same
criteria, restated as tick-questions ("does it name what she *does*?"). Free, instant, works with
the API down — and the evidence says 13–16s can genuinely do this given clear criteria. This is
the main answer to "how do they get feedback without AI": we stop being the only one who checks.

**8. Lean by default — nothing bogs them down.** ⭐ *Your ruling applied:* the standard beat is
criteria → example → question → self-check, and that's all. The richer exercises (spot-the-error
and so on) appear only the first time a beat type is met, or through the help buttons if a student
wants them. The walk's job is a finished outline over several sessions; the drafts are where depth
happens.

**9. If the AI is unavailable, nothing stops.** The walk already files, ticks and resumes without
it. The remaining ~6 AI moments per session degrade gracefully: your answer is accepted and filed,
the self-check stands in for the verdict, and "Ask Sophia" says honestly that she can't think it
through right now, re-offers the free help, and files your stuck-point for your tutor. A student
never loses work, never hits a dead end, and is never re-asked when the AI comes back. This
becomes a tested guarantee in the harnesses, not a hope.

**10. No random "inspiration" features, ever.** You don't believe in them; neither does the
evidence (a pre-registered study found random prompts do nothing). Every prompt is built from the
student's own earlier answers or the beat itself.

**11. The voice pass.** Machine phrases ("not written yet. This beat asks:") go. The rule: Sophia
talks about the story; the interface never talks about itself. A line is right if a teacher could
say it aloud to a 15-year-old.
