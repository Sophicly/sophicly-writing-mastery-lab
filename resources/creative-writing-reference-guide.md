# Creative Writing Reference Guide

A companion to the Creative Writing Masterclass. You do not need to read it all at once. Each step in the course points you to the section you need, exactly when you need it. Come back to it whenever you want to remind yourself *why* a step matters and *how* to get the most out of it.

---

## Step 1 — Your Writer's Profile and the Three Wells

### What this step is

Before you invent a single character or scene, you build a **Writer's Profile**: a short, honest map of what matters to you — your passions, your fears, the things that make you angry, the questions you can't stop thinking about, and the stories you already love.

It feels personal rather than "creative", and that is the point. You are not making anything up yet. You are gathering raw material.

### Why it matters (the part students always skip)

Every story you admire was written by a real person who cared about something. The great storytelling teacher **John Truby** puts it simply:

> *"Write something that may change your life. If a story is that important to you, it may be that important to a lot of people in the audience."*

Stories that move us almost always come from something the writer genuinely felt or feared or wondered about. If you start from your own profile, your story has a heartbeat from the very first line. If you start from nothing, you tend to write something that sounds like everyone else.

There is a second payoff, and it is just as important — see [Why your Writer's Profile makes you a better reader](#why-your-writers-profile-makes-you-a-better-reader) at the end.

### The Three Wells

The writer Matthew Kalil describes three "wells" that every writer draws story material from. A well is just a source you can return to again and again. Yours are already full — this step helps you look inside them.

#### The Memory Well

This is your richest well: your own life. Your experiences, the strong feelings you've had, and the values you hold. Powerful emotions — what you love, what frightens you, what you regret — are the fuel of great stories, because readers feel what writers genuinely felt.

In the course, Sophia will ask you about:

- **Your inner world** — what excites you, what you're passionate about, your greatest fear, what makes you happy, and a regret or setback that stayed with you.
- **Your moral compass** — what you value in people, what outrages you or feels unjust, a social problem you think about, an event that changed you, and who or what inspires you.
- **Your big question** — something about the world, life, or the future that you genuinely wonder about. The best stories often begin with a question the writer can't answer yet.

#### The Imagination Well

This is where you start to *play*. You take the real feelings from your Memory Well and stretch them with two words: **"What if…?"**

For example:

- *What if* you had to save the thing you're most passionate about from a powerful threat?
- *What if* a character had to face your exact greatest fear in order to get something they desperately wanted?

This is the bridge between a feeling and a story. A fear on its own is just a fear. A fear that a character must walk straight into is a plot.

#### The External Sources Well

This is your personal library — the books, films, shows, and games you already love. Your taste is not random. It quietly tells you what kinds of stories you find satisfying, which is a strong clue about what you'll enjoy writing. The course asks for one or two favourite stories of all time, and your favourite genres (fantasy, sci‑fi, mystery, thriller, horror, comedy, adventure, drama, and so on).

### How to answer well

- **Be specific, not impressive.** "I'm scared of being forgotten" is worth ten times more than "I'm scared of failure." Detail is where stories live.
- **Small things count.** You don't have to share anything painful or private. A minor regret — a friendship you let drift, a moment you stayed quiet when you should have spoken — is plenty of material.
- **Be honest rather than "writerly".** Nobody marks your profile. The truer your answers, the stronger every later step becomes.
- **One answer at a time.** Sophia asks a single question, waits, and gives you a quick word of encouragement before moving on. There's no rush.

### What you'll end up with

By the end of Step 1 you'll have two things saved into your document:

1. **Your Writer's Profile** — your passions and conflicts, your core "what if" scenarios, and the kinds of stories you're drawn to, all in one place.
2. **Three seed loglines** — three quick story ideas grown directly from your profile, just to show you how your own life turns into story fuel. A *logline* is a one‑sentence summary of a story. These are only starting points; you'll build your real logline properly in Step 3, and your story will keep growing and changing right through the course.

Everything you do later — your plot, your characters, your themes — reaches back to this profile. That's why it's worth doing slowly and honestly.

### Why your Writer's Profile makes you a better reader

Here is the connection that ties creative writing back to the analysis you do in your exams.

When you analyse a text, the hardest marks come from *evaluating the writer's intentions* — working out **why** the author wrote what they wrote, **what** they wanted you to think and feel, and often **what in their own life or times** moved them to write it at all. Examiners reward students who can step into the writer's shoes. There's a useful phrase for this skill: the passive reader reads the words; **the active reader reads what the writer is doing** — reconstructing the writer's purpose and the choices made to achieve it.

Building your own profile trains exactly that muscle. Once you've felt how *your* passions, fears, and questions shape *your* story, you start to see the same machinery in every text you read:

- Charles Dickens wrote *A Christmas Carol* partly out of real anger at how the poor were treated — his Memory Well.
- The myths and survival stories behind *The Hunger Games* and *The Lion King* are their writers' External Sources Wells at work.
- Every "what if" premise you meet — *what if* the dead could be brought back, *what if* a boy discovered he was a wizard — is an Imagination Well in action.

So this step is doing two jobs at once. It gives your own writing a beating heart, and it teaches you to read like a writer — which is precisely how you unlock the top bands in analysis. The next section goes deeper into exactly that.

## Step 2 — Finding Your Story (Story Sparks)

In Step 1 you looked inward and built your Writer's Profile. Step 2 is where you look *outward* for a spark — and then collide the two together.

### The collision: situation meets meaning

A story idea is rarely just one thing. The strongest ideas come from a **collision**:

> an **external spark** (a real event, an existing story, or a "what if") **×** a **theme from your own profile** (a passion, a fear, a question) **= a story idea that is both original and personal.**

The spark gives you the **situation**. Your profile gives it **meaning**. That is why two writers handed the same news story will write two completely different things — each pours in their own well.

This is your **External Sources Well** at work — the third of your three wells.

### Real-event sparks

Real life is stranger and more moving than anything we could invent. Here are three true stories — read each one in full, then ask: *which of my own themes does this collide with?*

#### When I Was 9 Years Old

When I was 9 years old I overslept and ran to school as quickly as I could. At the corner of the school was a man who had been murdered on the crosswalk. I had to step over him to get into the building. His body was still warm and he died with his eyes wide open. When I arrived at the front office for a pass the very first thing the receptionist said was "Y'all late again??? You're not going to be happy until we send you to court. Hurry up and get to class. I'm sick of seeing you up here. " Upon making it to class the teacher rolled her eyes with annoyance as she read the pass and handed me my papers. She berated me for putting my head down. " You walk in here an hour late and have the nerve to put your head down? You'd think you did enough of that last night!" As bizarre as it sounds their words traumatized me far more than the body that morning. It was the first time that I felt that I had no one. A different day I came home in a bad mood. I had been bullied that day for having a stain on my uniform and I couldn't wait to get home to escape them. The very first thing my mom said was "You always have a frown on your face. Don't come in here with no attitude. Fix your face before I fix it for you. " Now to be clear, this is not a diss post. This is not a bash the parent or the staff for xyz post. This is a reflection post. When I faced those situations the only thing I needed were kind words from the people who were in a position to help me overcome it. I shared this because I wanted you to understand just how much our children endure that we're not aware of and how much our words can impact them. The sticks and stones mantra is nonsense. Our words can hit like punches or can provide comfort like pillows.

We know this because as adults our greatest pain have come from words.

Yet we don't realize that they could be the thing that pushes a child over the edge or saves them from falling. I've noticed a trend during office hours that alot of my students will show up to be heard and not to be tutored. They share their heartbreak, their fears, their trauma and anxiety and while I'm honored to be a safe place I can't help but be concerned about what they will do when they exit the program. So much of the pain they experience come from the words that were said to them and every so often I'm put in a position where I have to reach out to their families to help. I find myself saying the same things to them that I've shared here with you. As a parent and an educator it's so important that you are careful what you say to kids because your words are the blocks they use to build their self image. Your words are powerful and while you don't think the joke you made about how much they eat is a big deal or how you ridiculed them for making a mistake 4 years ago is relevant, I promise you it is. Not only is it relevant but it's built the barrier between you that makes opening up feel unsafe. Because if they can't trust you to speak with kindness about the small things, how can they ever come to you when they struggling with bigger ones?

> *The spark:* the quiet power of words to harm or heal, and how much children carry unseen. *Collide it with* a profile theme of feeling unheard, of kindness, or of a small moment that changed you.

#### George Pickering

Doctors at a hospital in Texas informed George Pickering that his son was in a state of brain death.

They explained that his son showed "no brain activity" and the decision had been made to remove life support.   Pickering, however, was unconvinced by their assessment and resorted to extreme measures by drawing a firearm.

He demanded that the doctors continue their efforts to save his son's life, threatening to use the gun if they refused.

This situation escalated into a tense standoff lasting three hours, during which Pickering faced off against the police while the medical team performed additional tests on his son.

The hospital was secured under lockdown as police negotiators tried to reason with Pickering, who remained adamant.

In a pivotal moment, Pickering's son, previously declared braindead, responded to his father's request to squeeze his hand, indicating cognitive presence. Following this revelation, Pickering peacefully surrendered to the authorities.

George Pickering II subsequently served a prison sentence of 11 months. Remarkably, his son experienced a complete recovery.

> *The spark:* a parent's refusal to give up hope, and the collision between love and authority. *Collide it with* a theme of loyalty, faith, or standing alone against everyone.

#### Juliane Diller

**She Fell Nearly 2 Miles, and Walked Away**

At 17, biologist Juliane Diller was the sole survivor of a plane crash in the Amazon. Fifty years later she still runs Panguana, a research station founded by her parents in Peru.

On the morning after Juliane Diller fell to earth, she awoke in the deep jungle of the Peruvian rainforest dazed with incomprehension. Just before noon on the previous day — Christmas Eve, 1971 — Juliane, then 17, and her mother had boarded a flight in Lima bound for Pucallpa, a rough-and-tumble port city along the Ucayali River. Her final destination was Panguana, a biological research station in the belly of the Amazon, where for three years she had lived, on and off, with her mother, Maria, and her father, Hans-Wilhelm Koepcke, both zoologists.

The flight was supposed to last less than an hour. About 25 minutes after takeoff, the plane, an 86-passenger Lockheed L-188A Electra turboprop, flew into a thunderstorm and began to shake. Overhead storage bins popped open, showering passengers and crew with luggage and Christmas presents.

“My mother, who was sitting beside me, said, ‘Hopefully, this goes all right,” recalled Dr. Diller, who spoke by video from her home outside Munich, where she recently retired as deputy director of the Bavarian State Collection of Zoology. “Though I could sense her nervousness, I managed to stay calm.”

From a window seat in a back row, the teenager watched a bolt of lightning strike the plane’s right wing. She remembers the aircraft nose-diving and her mother saying, evenly, “Now it’s all over.” She remembers people weeping and screaming. And she remembers the thundering silence that followed. The aircraft had broken apart, separating her from everyone else onboard. “The next thing I knew, I was no longer inside the cabin,” Dr. Diller said. “I was outside, in the open air. I hadn’t left the plane; the plane had left me.”

As she plunged, the three-seat bench into which she was belted spun like the winged seed of a maple tree toward the jungle canopy. “From above, the treetops resembled heads of broccoli,” Dr. Diller recalled. She then blacked out, only to regain consciousness — alone, under the bench, in a torn minidress — on Christmas morning. She had fallen some 10,000 feet, nearly two miles. Her row of seats is thought to have landed in dense foliage, cushioning the impact. Juliane was the sole survivor of the crash.

Miraculously, her injuries were relatively minor: a broken collarbone, a sprained knee and gashes on her right shoulder and left calf, one eye swollen shut and her field of vision in the other narrowed to a slit. Most unbearable among the discomforts was the disappearance of her eyeglasses — she was nearsighted — and one of her open-back sandals. “I lay there, almost like an embryo for the rest of the day and a whole night, until the next morning,” she wrote in her memoir, “When I Fell From the Sky,” published in Germany in 2011. “I am completely soaked, covered with mud and dirt, for it must have been pouring rain for a day and a night.”
She listened to the calls of birds, the croaks of frogs and the buzzing of insects. “I recognized the sounds of wildlife from Panguana and realized I was in the same jungle and had survived the crash,” Dr. Diller said. “What I experienced was not fear but a boundless feeling of abandonment.” In shock, befogged by a concussion and with only a small bag of candy to sustain her, she soldiered on through the fearsome Amazon: eight-foot speckled caimans, poisonous snakes and spiders, stingless bees that clumped to her face, ever-present swarms of mosquitoes, riverbed stingrays that, when stepped on, instinctively lash out with their barbed, venomous tails.

It was the middle of the wet season, so there was no fruit within reach to pick and no dry kindling with which to make a fire. River water provided what little nourishment Juliane received. For 11 days, despite the staggering humidity and blast-furnace heat, she walked and waded and swam.

**A haven for ants and bats**

This year is the 50th anniversary of LANSA Flight 508, the deadliest lightning-strike disaster in aviation history. During the intervening years, Juliane moved to Germany, earned a Ph.D. in biology and became an eminent zoologist. In 1989, she married Erich Diller, an entomologist and an authority on parasitic wasps. Despite an understandable unease about air travel, she has been continually drawn back to Panguana, the remote conservation outpost established by her parents in 1968. “The jungle caught me and saved me,” said Dr. Diller, who hasn’t spoken publicly about the accident in many years. “It was not its fault that I landed there.”

In 1981, she spent 18 months in residence at the station while researching her graduate thesis on diurnal butterflies and her doctoral dissertation on bats. Nineteen years later, after the death of her father, Dr. Diller took over as director of Panguana and primary organizer of international expeditions to the refuge. “On my lonely 11-day hike back to civilization, I made myself a promise,” Dr. Diller said. “I vowed that if I stayed alive, I would devote my life to a meaningful cause that served nature and humanity.”

That cause would become Panguana, the oldest biological research station in Peru. Starting in the 1970s, Dr. Diller and her father lobbied the government to protect the area from clearing, hunting and colonization. Finally, in 2011, the newly minted Ministry of Environment declared Panguana a private conservation area. To help acquire adjacent plots of land, Dr. Diller enlisted sponsors from abroad. Largely through the largess of Hofpfisterei, a bakery chain based in Munich, the property has expanded from its original 445 acres to 4,000.

“Juliane is an outstanding ambassador for how much private philanthropy can achieve,” said Stefan Stolte, an executive board member of Stifterverband, a German nonprofit that promotes education, science and innovation.

Over the past half-century, Panguana has been an engine of scientific discovery. To date, the flora and fauna have provided the fodder for 315 published papers on such exotic topics as the biology of the Neotropical orchid genus Catasetum and the protrusile pheromone glands of the luring mantid.

Cleaved by the Yuyapichis River, the preserve is home to more than 500 species of trees (16 of them palms), 160 types of reptiles and amphibians, 100 different kinds of fish, seven varieties of monkey and 380 bird species. Panguana’s name comes from the local word for the undulated tinamou, a species of ground bird common to the Amazon basin. Dr. Diller’s favorite childhood pet was a panguana that she named Polsterchen — or Little Pillow — because of its soft plumage.

“Panguana offers outstanding conditions for biodiversity researchers, serving both as a home base with excellent infrastructure, and as a starting point into the primary rainforest just a few yards away,” said Andreas Segerer, deputy director of the Bavarian State Collection for Zoology, Munich. “Its extraordinary biodiversity is a ‘Garden of Eden’ for scientists, and a source of yielding successful research projects.”

Entomologists have cataloged a teeming array of insects on the ground and in the treetops of Panguana, including butterflies (more than 600 species), orchard bees (26 species) and moths (some 15,000). Manfred Verhaagh of the Natural History Museum in Karlsruhe, Germany, identified 520 species of ants. (So much for picnics at Panguana.)

While working on her dissertation, Dr. Diller documented 52 species of bats at the reserve. “We now know of 56,” she said. “By contrast, there are only 27 species in the entire continent of Europe.” The preserve has been colonized by all three species of vampires. Although they seldom attack humans, one dined on Dr. Diller’s big toe. “Vampire bats lap with their tongues, rather than suck,” she said. “After they make a small incision with their teeth, protein in their saliva called Draculin acts as an anticoagulant, which keeps the blood flowing while they feed.”

**Return to the crash site**

Dr. Diller described her youth in Peru with enthusiasm and affection. She was born in Lima, where her parents worked at the national history museum. Earthquakes were common.

“I grew up knowing that nothing is really safe, not even the solid ground I walked on,” Dr. Diller said. “The memories have helped me again and again to keep a cool head even in difficult situations.”

Dr. Diller said she was still haunted by the midair separation from her mother. Her voice lowered when she recounted certain moments of the experience. “Above all, of course, the moment when I had to accept that really only I had survived and that my mother had indeed died,” she said. “Then there was the moment when I realized that I no longer heard any search planes and was convinced that I would surely die, and the feeling of dying without ever having done anything of significance in my young life.”

She achieved a reluctant fame from the air disaster, thanks to a cheesy Italian biopic in 1974, “Miracles Still Happen,” in which the teenage Dr. Diller is portrayed as a hysterical dingbat. She avoided the news media for many years after, and is still stung by the early reportage, which was sometimes wildly inaccurate. According to an account in Life magazine in 1972, she made her getaway by building a raft of vines and branches. The German weekly Stern had her feasting on a cake she found in the wreckage and implied, from an interview conducted during her recovery, that she was arrogant and unfeeling.

Dr. Diller laid low until 1998, when she was approached by the movie director Werner Herzog, who hoped to turn her survivor’s story into a documentary for German TV. He had narrowly missed taking the same Christmas Eve flight while scouting locations for his historical drama “Aguirre, the Wrath of God.” He told her, “For all I know, we may have bumped elbows in the airport.”

Intrigued, Dr. Diller traveled to Peru and was flown by helicopter to the crash site, where she recounted the harrowing details to Mr. Herzog amid the plane’s still scattered remains. The most gruesome moment in the film was her recollection of the fourth day in the jungle, when she came upon a row of seats. Still strapped in were a woman and two men who had landed headfirst, with such force that they were buried three feet into the ground, legs jutting grotesquely upward.
“It was horrifying,” she told me. “I didn’t want to touch them, but I wanted to make sure that the woman wasn’t my mother. I grabbed a stick and turned one of her feet carefully so I could see the toenails. They were polished, and I took a deep breath. My mother never used polish on her nails.”

The result of Dr. Diller’s collaboration with Mr. Herzog was “Wings of Hope,” an unsettling film that, filtered through Mr. Herzog’s gruff humanism, demonstrated the strange and terrible beauty of nature. “Making the documentary was therapeutic,” Dr. Diller said. “At the time of the crash, no one offered me any formal counseling or psychological help. I had no idea that it was possible to even get help.”

**Lima or bust**

Dr. Diller attributes her tenacity to her father, Hans-Wilhelm Koepcke, a single-minded ecologist. He met his wife, Maria von Mikulicz-Radecki, in 1947 at the University of Kiel, where both were biology students. (Her Ph.D thesis dealt with the coloration of wild and domestic doves; his, woodlice). Late in 1948, Koepcke was offered a job at the natural history museum in Lima.

Getting there was not easy. Postwar travel in Europe was difficult enough, but particularly problematic for Germans. There were no passports, and visas were hard to come by.

To reach Peru, Dr. Koepcke had to first get to a port and inveigle his way onto a trans-Atlantic freighter. Setting off on foot, he trekked over several mountain ranges, was arrested and served time in an Italian prison camp, and finally stowed away in the hold of a cargo ship bound for Uruguay by burrowing into a pile of rock salt. When he showed up at the office of the museum director, two years after accepting the job offer, he was told the position had already been filled.

He persevered, and wound up managing the museum’s ichthyology collection. His fiancée followed him in a South Pacific steamer in 1950 and was hired at the museum, too, eventually running the ornithology department. An expert on Neotropical birds, she has since been memorialized in the scientific names of four Peruvian species.

He is remembered for a 1,684-page, two-volume opus, “Life Forms: The basis for a universally valid biological theory.” In 1956, a species of lava lizard endemic to Peru, Microlophus koepckeorum, was named in honor of the couple.

In 1968, the Koepckes moved from Lima to an abandoned patch of primary forest in the middle of the jungle. Their plan was to conduct field studies on its plants and animals for five years, exploring the rainforest without exploiting it. “I wasn’t exactly thrilled by the prospect of being there,” Dr. Diller said. “I was 14, and I didn’t want to leave my schoolmates to sit in what I imagined would be the gloom under tall trees, whose canopy of leaves didn’t permit even a glimmer of sunlight.”

To Juliane’s surprise, her new home wasn’t dreary at all. “It was gorgeous, an idyll on the river with trees that bloomed blazing red,” she recalled in her memoir. “There were mango, guava and citrus fruits, and over everything a glorious 150-foot-tall lupuna tree, also known as a kapok.”

The family lived in Panguana full-time with a German shepherd, Lobo, and a parakeet, Florian, in a wooden hut propped on stilts, with a roof of palm thatch. Juliane was home-schooled for two years, receiving her textbooks and homework by mail, until the educational authorities demanded that she return to Lima to finish high school.

**‘A place of peace and harmony’**

Dr. Diller’s parents instilled in their only child not only a love of the Amazon wilderness, but the knowledge of the inner workings of its volatile ecosystem. If you ever get lost in the rainforest, they counseled, find moving water and follow its course to a river, where human settlements are likely to be.

Their advice proved prescient. In 1971 Juliane, hiking away from the crash site, came upon a creek, which became a stream, which eventually became a river. On Day 11 of her ordeal she stumbled into the camp of a group of forest workers. They fed her cassava and poured gasoline into her open wounds to flush out the maggots that protruded “like asparagus tips,” she said. The next morning the workers took her to a village, from which she was flown to safety.
“For my parents, the rainforest station was a sanctuary, a place of peace and harmony, isolated and sublimely beautiful,” Dr. Diller said. “I feel the same way. The jungle was my real teacher. I learned to use old Indian trails as shortcuts and lay out a system of paths with a compass and folding ruler to orient myself in the thick bush. The jungle is as much a part of me as my love for my husband, the music of the people who live along the Amazon and its tributaries, and the scars that remain from the plane crash.”

Before 2020, when the coronavirus pandemic restricted international air travel, Dr. Diller made a point of visiting the nature preserve twice a year on monthlong expeditions. Much of her administrative work involves keeping industrial and agricultural development at bay. She estimates that as much as 17 percent of Amazonia has been deforested, and laments that vanishing ice, fluctuating rain patterns and global warming — the average temperature at Panguana has risen by 4 degrees Celsius in the past 30 years — are causing its wetlands to shrink. A recent study published in the journal Science Advances warned that the rainforest may be nearing a dangerous tipping point.

“After 20 percent, there is no possibility of recovery,” Dr. Diller said, grimly. “You could expect a major forest dieback and a rather sudden evolution to something else, probably a degraded savanna. That would lead to a dramatic increase in greenhouse gas emissions, which is why the preservation of the Peruvian rainforest is so urgent and necessary.”

Under Dr. Diller’s stewardship, Panguana has increased its outreach to neighboring Indigenous communities by providing jobs, bankrolling a new schoolhouse and raising awareness about the short- and long-term effects of human activity on the rainforest’s biodiversity and climate change.

“The key is getting the surrounding population to commit to preserving and protecting its environment,” she said. “Species and climate protection will only work if the locals are integrated into the projects, have a benefit for their already modest living conditions and the cooperation is transparent.” And so she plans to go back, and continue returning, once air travel allows.

Fifty years after Dr. Diller’s traumatic journey through the jungle, she is pleased to look back on her life and know that it has achieved purpose and meaning. “Just to have helped people and to have done something for nature means it was good that I was allowed to survive,” she said with a flicker of a smile. “And for that I am so grateful.”

> *The spark:* survival, isolation, and the will to keep going. *Collide it with* a fear of being alone, a love of the natural world, or a theme of resilience.

### Example story ideas

Here are six story ideas other writers have generated. Notice that each one is a **collision** — a situation carrying a deeper meaning. They are only examples, to get your own mind moving:

1. *Grief-stricken after the sudden loss of their daughter, a couple makes the controversial decision to bring her back as an exact-replica AI robot. As she navigates being both human and machine, the girl must confront the blurred line between humanity and technology, struggling to find her place in a world that no longer sees her as human.* **— spark: a "what if" about technology; meaning: what makes us human.**
2. *After a fiery explosion engulfs a high-rise, a seasoned firefighter is sent in to rescue survivors. When he fails, he is forced to confront his own mortality and the weight of his mistakes as he fights to redeem himself and save those still trapped.* **— spark: a disaster; meaning: guilt and redemption.**
3. *When a routine flight loses its engines, the pilots have only one choice: an emergency landing in the icy Hudson River. As they fight to keep the plane afloat and evacuate everyone, they must use all their skill and nerve to survive the chaos.* **— spark: a real event; meaning: grace under pressure.**
4. **Feathers of Fate** *— in a dystopian future where birds have gained intelligence and become the dominant species, a band of survivors navigates a world overrun by them, and stumbles on a hidden society of humans who can communicate with and control birds.* **— spark: a "what if" inversion of nature; meaning: power and survival.**
5. **Virtual Vengeance** *— a brilliant programmer builds a groundbreaking virtual-reality game, but as players grow obsessed, they discover their actions inside the game have real-world consequences. A group of gamers must uncover the truth behind its origin.* **— spark: technology; meaning: obsession and consequence.**
6. **Echoes of Extinction** *— after a cataclysm leaves most of Earth uninhabitable, a small group of survivors finds a hidden underground sanctuary, and uncovers a dark secret about the powers its remaining inhabitants possess.* **— spark: the post-apocalyptic; meaning: what we inherit and hide.**

### From spark to finished story

A spark is only the beginning — the rest of this course turns it into a crafted piece. Take a student story called *Elena*: it grew from a simple, personal spark — *what if a close friend came back changed, and wrong?* — into a taut piece of horror, all atmosphere, dread, and a final, unanswered question. Here it is in full. As you read, notice how much is achieved with restraint: short sentences for panic, the fog and shadows doing the threatening, the friend's face turned into the monster. That is what a Step-1 fear, collided with a "what if", can become.

#### Elena

*by Parashay Khan*

Somewhere behind her eyes, a whisper claws for attention—a warning simmering just under Elena’s skin, waiting to erupt.

She stands pressed against her locker, her friends gathered close in the whirl and spill of corridor noise. When someone tells a joke that falls flat, she laughs anyway—a little too quickly, her smile straining at the edges. No one notices her eyes flicking to the classroom door, then drifting to the ribbons of shadow pooling beneath the lockers, in places even the lights seem afraid to touch. Each time, she half-expects something to blink back from the blackness. Everything is fine, she tells herself. Just nerves.

The school bell chimes. The corridor empties. Elena trails behind Caroline. She watches Caroline’s hand drift, again and again, to her neck—fingers scratching the angry, red mark blooming below her jaw. Caroline moves stiffly. Eyes locked ahead. She flinches at each sudden noise from shadowed classrooms. The usual bustle fades to a distant hush as Caroline grabs her things and vanishes into the late afternoon, leaving Elena in the lingering silence.

That evening, Elena’s phone buzzes.

Caroline: Can you call me if you’re awake?

A pause. Messages hang between them, fragile.

Caroline: Never mind. Sorry.

Two grey ticks. No reply. Elena tries to call. Nothing. Morning comes but Caroline is gone. No texts answered. No posts. No sign of her at school. Days slip by, heavy and slow. Unease twists through Elena, gnawing at her resolve. The image of Caroline’s hurried retreat from school replays in her mind, unsettling every moment of quiet.

Night falls, two days later. The city suffocates under thick, uneasy dark. Elena heads home late, grocery bags biting red into her aching hands. Her footsteps echo down empty pavements. Streetlights stutter, blinking in and out. At the corner of her street, a sudden chill prickles her skin. She hesitates. Fog coils in the air—dense, impenetrable.

And then, through the swirling mist, a blonde-haired girl emerges—her face blurred and pale in the guttering lamp, looking so much like Caroline that Elena’s heart stutters with doubt.

Elena’s nerves snap with dread. She edges closer. The blonde-haired girl tilts her head—and as the harsh yellow light slides across her face, Elena sees it is, undeniably, Caroline—pale, inhuman.

Catching the sickly light, silver fangs gleam. Long. Razor-sharp. Too bright, like something forged from nightmares and moonlight. They jut from parted lips, a predator’s display.

Dark cracks spiderweb beneath her eyes, glinting like shattered ice. For a heartbeat, Elena can’t breathe. Her blood drums in her ears. The truth crashes in: the darkness she feared has found her—and it wears Caroline’s face.

Rooted. Frozen. Elena stares. Those fangs catch the meagre light, her gaze endless and empty. Cracks crawling over skin that once seemed familiar. Terror and hope snarl together in Elena’s chest. Then Caroline’s gaze sharpens—something savage, something lost. Panic detonates inside Elena. Her grip unravels. Groceries tumble, cans skittering across concrete, ringing in the night. She bolts into the fog. Heart battering her ribs. Breath torn and ragged. She doesn’t dare look back. Only escape matters.

The crossroads appear out of the mist. Elena bursts into the emptiness, lungs burning, footsteps ricocheting across black tarmac. Fog thickens, swirling, swallowing sound and light.

Then—with a blink—Caroline stands in front of her.

No warning. No sound. She just appears.

For a moment, Caroline flickers at the edge of reality—eyes glazed, hair damp, lips parted. Lamplight traces inhuman angles across her features. In the swirling dark, Caroline seems both real and not, as if Elena’s terror has conjured her from the shadows.

Elena’s world narrows to a single, quivering point of fear. She steps back, hands shaking. Caroline’s eyes snap to hers—predatory, hungry, familiar and utterly changed. Elena feels it now: there is no running. This darkness knows her name.

The crossroads, the city, the entire world, dissolve into fog and shadow.

Elena’s lips tremble. “Caroline... who did this to you?”

A flicker of something—regret? Grief?—but Caroline says nothing. Instead, she lunges. Movements twisted, animal, inhuman. Fangs flash.

Elena jerks aside just in time. Not quite fast enough. A sting flares as fangs rake her neck, tracing fire in her skin—a narrow escape, the wound burning hot.

The night holds its breath.

Then, in the next blink, Caroline is gone. Swallowed whole by mist. Elena stands frozen, hand pressed to her wounded neck. Nerves buzzing. Thoughts racing. The roar of warning in her mind drowns out even her hammering heart. Every shadow leans closer. Every breath tastes of dread. She tries to gather the pieces of herself. It will be fine. It has to be fine. Please, let it be fine.

In the shivering aftermath, she realises: she may never escape what waits for her in the shadows.

## Step 3 — Your Logline and the Building Blocks of Story

### What this step is

In Step 2 you chose one idea to develop. Now you turn it into a **logline** — a single, powerful sentence that captures the DNA of your whole story. Screenwriters use loglines to pitch a film in one breath; you'll use one to make sure your idea is strong *before* you spend hours building it. To write that sentence, you first break your idea into its essential parts. This guide explains each part — and the bigger truth about story they all serve.

### What a story actually is

Before the parts, the whole. At Sophicly we work from one definition that changes how you write *and* how you read:

> **A story is a series of events, deliberately arranged to teach a value.**

A story is never just "things that happen". A writer selects and orders events so that, by the end, you have *felt your way to an idea about how to live* — that loyalty costs something, that pride blinds us, that the powerless deserve dignity. That idea is the story's **meaning**, and every part below exists to deliver it.

This is why literature matters. **Literature is a creative exploration of what it means to be a human being** — a safe place to live through choices, losses, and temptations that aren't ours, and to come out understanding the world, and this life, a little more clearly. When you build a story, you are doing exactly what Dickens and Shakespeare did: arranging events to make a reader feel a truth.

### The protagonist *is* the story

Here is the single most important idea in this whole course:

> **The protagonist is the character who goes through the greatest change from the beginning of the story to the end — and that change is what reveals the story's meaning.**

Notice what this means. The protagonist is **not** simply the main character, the strongest, or the one who speaks most. They are the one who is *different at the end from how they began* — and the distance they travel is the message. Scrooge ends *A Christmas Carol* generous where he began cruel: the change *is* the meaning (we can choose to open our hearts). Macbeth ends a tyrant where he began a loyal soldier: the change *is* the meaning (unchecked ambition rots a person from the inside).

So before you reach for clever events, ask: **who changes most, and what does their change teach?** Build everything else to make that journey happen. (This is just as powerful in your *analysis*: find the character who changes most, and you've found where the writer hid the point.)

### The logline's building blocks

A strong logline is built from up to eight components. You won't cram all eight into one sentence — but understanding each one tells you what your story needs. As you work through the chat with Sophia in this step, she'll help you define them one at a time for your own idea; your answers are saved straight into your document.

#### 1. The protagonist

The character who will change the most — the heart of your story. In your logline, introduce them in a few words that hint at who they are *and* what's wrong: not "a girl", but "a guarded, self-reliant teenager".

#### 2. The flaw (the emotional shield)

This is the building block students find hardest — and the one that separates a flat story from a moving one. It is worth slowing right down here.

Strong protagonists are never perfect. They carry a **flaw**: a visible, repeated behaviour that gets them into trouble. But a flaw on its own is just a bad habit. What makes it powerful is what sits *underneath* it. The strongest flaws are an **emotional shield** — a behaviour the character built, often long ago, to protect a deeper, hidden **wound**. (The idea of the flaw as an emotional shield comes from Matt Bird's *The Secrets of Story*; at Sophicly we treat it as the heart of character.)

Picture it as two layers:

- **The wound (hidden, underneath):** a hurt, fear, or loss the character carries and would rather not feel — the fear of being abandoned, of not being good enough, of losing someone again.
- **The flaw, or shield (visible, on the surface):** the behaviour they use to keep anything from touching that wound. It *looks* like their problem, but it is really their armour.

Here is the crucial part: the shield **works**. That is why the character keeps using it. In ordinary life it holds the pain at a safe distance. The whole job of your story is to pile on enough pressure that the shield finally **stops working** and the character is forced to lower it. The moment they drop the shield and face the wound underneath — *that* is the change, and the change is where your story's meaning lives.

A few worked examples — notice the same two layers every time:

- **Ebenezer Scrooge** (the cold, miserly businessman in Charles Dickens's *A Christmas Carol*, 1843) — **Flaw:** greed. **Wound:** a fear of human connection, left by a lonely, loveless past. **The shield in action:** he hoards money and drives people away, because wealth cannot reject him the way people can. **The change:** the spirits strip the shield away until he can finally let others in.
- **Katniss Everdeen** (the teenage heroine of Suzanne Collins's *The Hunger Games*, 2008) — **Flaw:** cold detachment. **Wound:** the terror of losing the people she loves, having already lost her father. **The shield in action:** she keeps everyone at arm's length so their loss can never break her. **The change:** she learns to let herself love and be loved, even knowing what it might cost.
- **Macbeth** (the Scottish general in Shakespeare's tragedy, c.1606) — **Flaw:** ruthless ambition. **Wound:** a deep insecurity, a craving to be told he is enough. **The shield in action:** he seizes power to prove his own worth. **The change:** here the arc is a *fall* — he never lowers the shield, and it destroys him. (A flaw can lead to ruin as well as to growth; both reveal meaning.)

**How to find your protagonist's flaw.** Start with a visible behaviour, then dig beneath it. If your first answer is a surface trait — "he's clumsy", "she's shy", "he's arrogant" — ask the harder question: *what is that behaviour protecting them from?* Arrogance may be a shield against the fear of being thought worthless. Shyness may guard a wound of past humiliation. Keep asking "but why?" until you reach a *feeling*, not just a habit. A flaw with no wound beneath it is only a quirk; a flaw that shields a wound is a story.

Two traps to avoid. Don't give your character a flaw that never touches the plot — if the story would unfold exactly the same without it, the flaw isn't doing its job. And don't let them shed the flaw too easily — the shield should hold until real pressure forces it down. Earned change moves a reader; easy change doesn't.

#### 3. The inciting incident

The **stunning surprise** that shatters the protagonist's normal life and drags them into the story — the moment their shield stops working. Marley's ghost arrives and Scrooge can no longer hide behind his wealth. Will Byers vanishes and the safe, ordinary world breaks open. Without an inciting incident a character just *exists*; with one, a story *begins*.

#### 4. The life-changing event (the goal it opens up)

The inciting incident hands the protagonist a new **goal** — a single, physical, visible thing they are now trying to achieve. The richest goals carry a hidden second layer: a deep **internal need** the character doesn't yet understand. Luke Skywalker's external goal is to rescue the princess; his internal need is to discover who he is meant to become. The visible goal drives the plot; the invisible need drives the meaning.

#### 5. The opponent / antagonist

The formidable, seemingly **unbeatable** force standing in the protagonist's way. The most powerful opponents act as a **dark mirror** — they embody exactly the flaw the protagonist must overcome, so that beating them means changing. In *An Inspector Calls*, Inspector Goole forces a comfortable family to confront the wilful blindness they'd rather keep. An opponent need not be a villain; it is whoever or whatever attacks the protagonist's weakest point.

#### 6. The ally

The character who helps, challenges, or believes in the protagonist along the way — often the one who sees the wound the protagonist is hiding and gently refuses to let them hide. Allies make change *possible*: they offer the support, truth, or example the protagonist can't yet give themselves.

#### 7. The battle

The story's climax: the final, decisive confrontation where the protagonist faces the opponent — and, really, faces their own flaw. The battle isn't always a fight; it's the moment of greatest pressure, when the character must finally choose. This is where the change is won or lost.

#### 8. The stakes

What the protagonist stands to **lose** if they fail — the reason the reader cares. The strongest stakes are **personal and specific**. "If she fails, the world ends" is vague; "if she fails, her little sister goes into the arena alone" is felt instantly. Stakes can be survival, love, freedom, identity, or justice — but they land hardest when we can picture exactly what would be lost.

### Three ways to shape your logline

At Sophicly we offer three formulas. Each tells the *same* story but leans on a different building block — try all three and keep the one that best captures your idea's heart.

1. **Action-oriented** — *Inciting Incident + Protagonist + Action + Antagonist.*
   *"When a young boy disappears, his mother, a police chief, and his friends must confront terrifying forces to get him back."* — *Stranger Things*
2. **Goal-oriented** — *Protagonist + Action + Antagonist + Goal + Stake.*
   *"Luke Skywalker, a spirited farm boy, joins the rebels to fight Darth Vader and rescue Princess Leia from certain death."* — *Star Wars*
3. **Character-arc oriented** — *Protagonist has an opportunity to do something life-changing but must overcome their flaw to find a solution.*
   *"An old, greedy miser called Scrooge has the chance to improve the lives of those around him, but he must let go of his fear of human connection so he can become generous and find happiness."* — *A Christmas Carol*

The third formula is worth dwelling on: it puts the **change** at the centre — exactly where the meaning lives.

### What you'll carry forward

By the end of Step 3 you'll have one chosen logline and its components saved to your document. They become the foundation for everything that follows: your outline (Step 4), your plot structure (Steps 5–6), and every draft after. Get the change right here, and the rest of the story knows where it's going.

## Why Authors Write — Reading Like a Writer

This section is the bridge between your creative writing and your analysis. Once you understand *why* writers write, you read every text more powerfully — because you stop reading only the words and start reading what the writer is *doing*.

### Every text is a deliberate act

Nobody writes by accident. Behind every poem, play, and novel is a real person who wanted something from you — usually one or more of these:

- **To make you feel** — to move you to pity, fear, joy, anger, or hope.
- **To make you think** — to raise a question, change your mind, or show you the world differently.
- **To make you act** — to nudge you towards kindness, courage, or change.
- **To entertain** — to grip you, delight you, and keep you turning the page.

Most great texts do several of these at once. At Sophicly we call these the **Effects on the Reader**: what the writer makes you **Focus** on, **Feel**, **Think**, and be moved to **Act** on. Working out an author's purpose is simply working out which of these effects they were reaching for — and how their choices achieve it.

### Where a writer's purpose comes from

Purpose isn't random. It grows out of the same three wells you explored in your own profile:

- the writer's **own life and feelings** (their Memory Well),
- the **time and society** they lived in (its fears, injustices, and arguments),
- and the **stories and ideas** they grew up on (their External Sources Well).

That is why "context" matters so much in your exams. When you learn that Dickens worked in a factory as a child, or that *An Inspector Calls* was written just as Britain built its welfare state, you are really learning the contents of that writer's wells — and that tells you what they were trying to do.

### Five writers and what they were doing

A short tour of texts you may study. Notice that in every case the *purpose* explains the *choices*.

- **_Macbeth_ (William Shakespeare, 1606)** — a tragedy in which a Scottish general murders his king to seize the throne, then destroys himself. Shakespeare wrote it soon after the Gunpowder Plot of 1605, for a society terrified of treason, under a king (James I) who believed monarchs were chosen by God and had himself written a book on witchcraft. **Purpose:** to dramatise the horror of killing a rightful king and the way unchecked ambition rots a person from the inside — while flattering the anxieties of his royal audience. When Macbeth admits his only spur is *"vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself"* (Act 1, Scene 7), Shakespeare is naming the danger out loud. (Note: Macbeth's downfall comes from an *error of judgement* — choosing to act on ambition — not a simple "flaw" he was born with.)

- **_A Christmas Carol_ (Charles Dickens, 1843)** — a novella in which the cold-hearted miser Ebenezer Scrooge is visited by spirits and transformed into a generous man. Dickens knew real poverty: as a boy he was sent to work in a blacking factory while his father was in a debtors' prison — his Memory Well. **Purpose:** to make a comfortable middle-class readership *feel* for the poor and *act* with charity. The starving children he calls *Ignorance* and *Want* are a direct warning to a society that preferred to look away. The purpose (provoke conscience) explains the choice (a ghost story that ambushes you with emotion).

- **_An Inspector Calls_ (J. B. Priestley, 1945)** — a play set in 1912 in which a mysterious Inspector reveals how one prosperous family each played a part in a young woman's death. Priestley wrote it just as two world wars ended and Britain was building a fairer, more collective society. **Purpose:** to argue that *"we are members of one body"* — that we are responsible for each other, not just ourselves. Setting it in 1912 lets the audience, who know the wars are coming, feel the cost of ignoring that lesson.

- **_Romeo and Juliet_ (William Shakespeare, c.1595)** — a tragedy in which two young people from feuding families fall in love and die. **Purpose:** to grip an audience *and* to caution them: Shakespeare calls the deaths the work of *"a pair of star-crossed lovers"* and shows that the real killer is the families' inherited hatred. The purpose (a warning about destructive feuding) explains why the play punishes the older generation through the loss of their children.

- **_Jane Eyre_ (Charlotte Brontë, 1847)** — the life-story of an orphan who grows up to become a governess and insists on her own moral worth. Brontë poured her own experiences into it: a harsh charity school and years of governess work — her Memory Well. **Purpose:** to assert that a woman of no rank, money, or beauty still has a conscience and a soul equal to anyone's. *"I am no bird; and no net ensnares me; I am a free human being with an independent will,"* Jane declares — Brontë writing against the narrow social position women of her time were allowed.

### How to spot a writer's purpose

When you read for analysis, hunt for the same clues you'd leave in your own work:

1. **What does the text make you feel, and where?** Strong, deliberate feeling is the fingerprint of purpose.
2. **What does the writer praise, attack, or pity?** That reveals their values — and what they want you to value.
3. **What were their life and times like?** Context fills in the wells the writing flows from.
4. **What choices are repeated?** A pattern (a recurring image, a structure, a kind of character) is almost always purpose made visible.

Do this often enough and it becomes instinct — the same instinct you're building right now by mapping your *own* wells in Step 1.
