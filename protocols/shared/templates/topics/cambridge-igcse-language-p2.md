**Context:** The following passage is an article about teenagers taking part-time jobs while still at school.

The following passage is an article about teenagers taking part-time jobs while still at school.

Should school students work? Fifty years ago the question would have puzzled most families —
paper rounds and Saturday shifts were simply what teenagers did. Today, with exam pressure rising,
opinion is sharply divided.

The case for a part-time job is more than pocket money. Employers repeatedly say that young
people with work experience interview better: they look adults in the eye, they arrive on time,
and they can describe a problem they actually solved. Money handled is money understood — a
teenager who has earned sixty pounds thinks differently about spending it. Juggling shifts with
homework teaches the kind of time management no planner-writing lesson ever quite delivers, and
for many teenagers the workplace supplies something school cannot: colleagues of all ages,
responsibility that is real rather than rehearsed, and the particular confidence that comes from
being useful to strangers.

Teachers, though, see the other side of the ledger. One head of sixth form describes marking work
'visibly written at midnight after a six-hour shift'. Research on the question is awkwardly
balanced: students working a few hours a week often do as well as, or better than, their
classmates, but beyond roughly ten hours the picture darkens — grades slip, sleep shrinks and
absences creep upwards. There are also genuine concerns about the work itself. Young workers are
cheap, and some employers lean on that: late-notice rotas, shifts that quietly grow, minimum
duties on minimum pay. A sixteen-year-old rarely knows what a contract should look like, and
almost never complains.

Parents are caught in the middle, wanting their children to grow up capable but not exhausted.
Some notice something subtler, too: a teenager with somewhere to be on Saturday morning is a
teenager the family now schedules around.
Family financial pressure complicates the choice further: for some households a teenager's wage
is not character-building but necessary.

Perhaps the only honest conclusion is that the question is badly put. 'Should students work?'
matters less than 'how much, for whom, and at what cost?' A short, regular shift with a decent
employer can be the making of a teenager; the same teenager, over-rostered in exam season, can be
quietly sunk by it. The difference is not the job. It is the limits set around it.

**Saturday girl** — posted by Amara, 17

People at school think my weekend job is a tragedy. 'You work in a *bakery*? At *seven in the

no politely, how to keep smiling at hour six. When the queue is out of the door and we clear it,
the feeling is better than full marks.

It has changed school, too, in ways I did not expect. I plan my week now because I have to — and
my grades have gone *up* since I started, which nobody believes but is true. When my form tutor
asked for an example of 'working under pressure' for my personal statement, I had six. Even the
early starts have a strange upside: by nine on a Saturday I have already achieved something, and
the whole day feels longer and more mine.

My advice, if you are thinking about it: guard your hours like a dragon. I do one day, never
two; exam weeks are sacred, and Rosa respects that — a good boss will. If yours doesn't, that
tells you everything, and you should leave. A job should be the thing that makes the rest of
your life work better, not the thing that swallows it.

Would I recommend it? Completely — but on your terms, not theirs. The bakery does not need you
at the top of your class. You do.

**Context:** The following passage is an article about how habits are formed.

The following passage is an article about how habits are formed.

Why is it so hard to start running, and so easy to check a phone? The answer, according to
researchers who study behaviour, is that the brain is an efficiency machine. Anything repeated
often enough gets handed over to autopilot, which is wonderful when the repeated thing is
brushing your teeth and less wonderful when it is an hour of scrolling before breakfast.

The encouraging news is that autopilot can be programmed deliberately. The most reliable method
is not willpower but design. Start absurdly small: two minutes of an activity, not thirty, so
that beginning costs almost nothing. Attach the new habit to an existing one — after I pour my
morning drink, I read one page — because a settled routine is the strongest reminder there is.
And arrange the furniture of your life so that the good choice is the easy one: the book on the
pillow, the trainers by the door, the phone charging in another room. People who appear to have
iron discipline, the research suggests, mostly have well-arranged rooms.

Two popular beliefs deserve gentle demolition. The first is that a habit takes 21 days to form.
The number is folklore: in studies, people have needed anywhere from three weeks to the best part
of a year, depending on the habit and the person. The second is that missing a day is fatal. It
is not; in the data, a single lapse makes almost no difference to the eventual outcome. What
matters is never missing twice.

There are dissenting voices. Some psychologists worry that the habit industry — the apps, the
planners, the streak-counters — sells a mechanical picture of a human life, and that people can
end up serving their routines rather than being served by them. A habit, they point out, is a
tool for getting to something that matters. It is not the something.

Still, the central finding survives the argument: people do not usually fail at change because
they are weak, but because they ask too much of day one. Whatever you wish you did daily, the
advice of the research is unglamorous and unanimous. Make it small. Tie it to something you
already do. Make it easy to start and boring to skip. Then let repetition, quietly, do the rest.

**My year of the streak** — by Nadia Rahim

It began innocently: one app, to make me drink more water. Within three months I had six. There
was the one that grew a little digital tree while I studied, the one that pinged me to stretch,
the one that scored my sleep like an exam, and — I am not proud of this — one that tracked
whether I had used the other apps.

The strange thing is that it worked, at first. I have never drunk so much water, walked so many
steps or gone to bed so punctually as in those first weeks. There is a real pleasure in a chain
of green ticks, and anyone who says otherwise has never earned a 30-day streak. My concentration
improved. My friends noticed. I felt, for a while, like the CEO of my own life.

Then the streaks stopped serving me and I started serving them. I drank water I did not want at
23:50 to keep a chain alive. I chose the treadmill over my cousin's birthday dinner because the
app did not have a box for 'family'. When my phone died for a weekend and three streaks
perished, I felt a grief so genuine and so absurd that even I could hear the alarm bell. My sleep
score, meanwhile, had become the main thing keeping me awake.

So I deleted them — all but one, to which I'll return. What surprised me was how little
collapsed. The walking survived, because by then my legs simply expected it. The water drinking
survived. The 5 a.m. study sessions, which had only ever existed for the tree, vanished
instantly, and honestly they were doing less for my grades than sleep would have done.

Here is what my year taught me. The apps are training wheels: brilliant for starting, dangerous
to depend on. A habit that only exists because a phone is watching is not yet yours. And a
number can only measure a habit — it cannot tell you whether the habit deserves to exist.

The app I kept? The tree one. Not for the streaks. I just like the forest, all of it grown out
of afternoons when I was, quietly and without a witness, becoming someone who studies.

**Context:** The following passage is an article about the fashion industry and cheap clothing.

The following passage is an article about the fashion industry and cheap clothing.

There is a mountain in the Atacama Desert in Chile made almost entirely of clothes. Tens of
thousands of tonnes of unsold and unwanted garments arrive there every year — many with their
labels still attached. It has become the favourite photograph of everyone who believes that the
way we buy clothes has gone badly wrong.

The numbers behind the photograph are startling. The world produces roughly twice as many
garments as it did at the turn of the century, while the number of times each garment is actually
worn has fallen by more than a third. A t-shirt that once cost a day's wages now costs less than
a sandwich, and is treated accordingly: bought for one photograph, worn twice, abandoned. The
industry that makes this possible is thirsty and dirty — a single pair of jeans swallows
thousands of litres of water, and textile production emits more carbon than international flights
and shipping combined.

Yet the story is less one-sided than campaigners sometimes allow. Cheap clothing, its defenders
point out, is a democracy: fashion was once a private club for the wealthy, and low prices opened
it to everyone. The industry employs tens of millions of people, most of them women in countries
with few alternative employers, and a boycott organised from comfortable countries does not feed
their families. Nor is the expensive alternative automatically virtuous — a high price tag
guarantees higher profit, not better wages or cleaner rivers.

Even so, most researchers agree on where the real power lies: not in what shoppers buy so much
as in how long they keep it. Extending the life of a garment from one year to two roughly halves
its footprint. The greenest item of clothing, as the saying goes, is the one already hanging in
your wardrobe. Repairing, swapping, buying second-hand and — above all — buying less, but
better, achieve more than any recycling bin, since most 'recycled' clothing is in fact
downgraded into rags and stuffing, or shipped abroad to mountains like Atacama's.

The uncomfortable conclusion is that there is no virtuous purchase, only a less wasteful
wardrobe. The question the industry would prefer us not to ask is not 'is this t-shirt ethical?'
but 'do I need it at all?'

**Context:** The following passage is an article about machines that do everyday tasks for us.

The following passage is an article about machines that do everyday tasks for us.

Somewhere in your house, a machine is quietly deciding something for you. The navigation app has
chosen your route to school. The video service has chosen tonight's film. The spell-checker has
repaired this morning's messages, the camera has brightened your photographs, and a voice from a
small speaker in the kitchen has answered, without being thanked, four questions your family
once would have argued about enjoyably for an hour.

Psychologists call this cognitive offloading: handing a mental task to a device the way you
might hand a heavy bag to a friend. We have always done it — a shopping list is offloading, and
so is a knot in a handkerchief. What is new is the scale. Studies find that people who rely on
navigation apps develop weaker mental maps of their own neighbourhoods, and most of us can now
recall barely a handful of phone numbers, where our grandparents held dozens. The skill does not
vanish because the machine stole it; it fades because we stopped practising it, in the same way
that a language unspoken slips away.

The defenders of the machines have a strong reply: this is what tools are for. Nobody mourns the
mental arithmetic lost to calculators or demands we churn our own butter. Every technology from
writing onwards has been accused of rotting memory — Socrates said exactly that about the
written word — and yet each one freed human attention for something the machine could not do.
The pilot who lets the autopilot hold the course is not lazier; she is watching the weather.

The genuine worry, researchers suggest, is not laziness but choice. A list you wrote, you
chose. A route the app picks, a film the service recommends, a word the keyboard predicts — each
is a small decision made for you by a system whose reasons you cannot see, and which may not
share your interests. Convenience, in other words, is never free; it is paid for in decisions.

Their advice is not to abandon the machines but to stay their master: let them do what you could
do yourself if you had to — and check, every so often, by doing the thing unaided, that you
genuinely still can.

The machines are excellent servants. I just no longer let them apply for the manager's job.

**Context:** The following passage is an article about watching performances in person.

The following passage is an article about watching performances in person.

Almost everything can now be watched from a sofa. Concerts are filmed in ultra-high definition,
national theatres stream their productions into cinemas and living rooms, and the best seat in the
house — the one the cameras occupy — costs a fraction of the worst seat in the actual building.
Given that, the interesting question is not why audiences stay at home. It is why so many of them
still go out.

The case for staying in is strong and worth stating honestly. Streaming is cheaper, closer, and
open to people for whom a night out is difficult or impossible: those far from any city, those
with limited mobility, those who cannot afford tickets, travel and a meal. Subtitles help; pausing
helps; a bad view never happens. A generation that would once have been priced out of opera has
seen more of it than their grandparents ever did, and the recording lasts for ever.

Yet the evidence on live attendance is stubborn. Ticket sales for concerts and theatre have grown,
not shrunk, alongside the streaming boom, and the audiences are getting younger. Researchers who
study this describe two effects that a screen cannot reproduce. The first is physical: sound and
light experienced in a room, at a volume and scale a device cannot deliver, produce measurable
changes in heart rate and attention. The second is social: an audience laughing, gasping or
falling silent together creates what psychologists call collective effervescence — the sense of
being part of something larger, briefly, with strangers.

Venues themselves add a third argument, and it is a practical one: an audience physically present
in a building spends money in the streets around it, sustains the restaurants and late buses that
make a town feel alive at night, and keeps the technical trades employed. A streamed performance
supports the company that made it; a full house supports a neighbourhood.

There is also the matter of risk. A recording is finished; a live performance might fail. The
singer might crack the note, the actor might forget the line, the trapeze artist might miss. That
possibility is not a flaw in the experience — it is the source of its charge, and the reason a
recovery is applauded so loudly.

None of which makes streaming the enemy. Venues report that filmed performances often function as
a doorway: people watch at home, then buy a ticket for the next one. The two are not really
competitors. But if a young person has never once sat in a dark room with a thousand others and
felt the floor move, they have not yet been offered the choice.

## Text A
**Context:** The following passage is an article about homework.

The following passage is an article about homework.

Few school policies are argued about as often, or with as little evidence, as homework. Parents
suspect there is either too much of it or too little. Teachers set it partly because they are
expected to. And a surprising number of schools have never examined what their own homework
actually achieves.

The research, where it exists, is less exciting than either side would like. For older students,
homework shows a modest but real benefit — but the effect depends almost entirely on the type of
task. Practice that requires retrieving something from memory (self-testing, past questions,
explaining a concept without notes) improves learning measurably. Tasks that merely occupy time —
copying, colouring, decorating a poster — show close to no effect at all, and consume the same
evening. For younger children, the measurable academic benefit is very small indeed, which is why
several countries have reduced or abolished it before secondary school.

The quantity findings are more consistent. Benefit rises with time spent, then flattens, then
reverses: beyond roughly two hours a night for a sixteen-year-old, additional homework is
associated with worse outcomes, not better, and with reduced sleep — which independently damages
memory. In other words, a student who works until midnight is often undoing the work they did at
nine.

There is also a fairness problem that averages conceal. Homework is completed in conditions the
school does not control. One student has a desk, a quiet room, reliable internet and a parent who
studied the subject; another shares a kitchen table, minds a younger sibling, and works a
Saturday shift. Setting the same task to both is not equal treatment, and researchers consistently
find that homework widens the gap between them rather than narrowing it.

Nor is the picture improved by simply setting more marking. Teachers who mark every piece in detail
report spending evenings of their own on work that students often glance at and file, and the
research on written feedback suggests that a mark a student never acts on changes very little.

What emerges is not an argument for abolition, but for design. The schools that get the best out
of homework tend to set less of it, make it retrieval-based, mark it quickly or not at all, and
provide supervised time in school for those who need it. The question worth asking is not 'how
much homework should we set?' but 'what is this particular task for, and could the same benefit be
had in twenty minutes instead of ninety?'

I am not one of the students who wants homework abolished. I want to say that first, because the

engineer, so his physics is checked before it is handed in. We all get the same sheet and are
compared on the results. Everybody knows this, and nobody says it.
