### Protagonist
- **Note:** Santiago begins as an ageing Cuban fisherman branded "salao" — the unluckiest of all — pitied by his village after eighty-four fishless days; through his own choice to row far out and endure the great marlin he reclaims his dignity, so that though he returns destroyed in body he is undefeated in spirit, proving "a man can be destroyed but not defeated".

### Plot
- **Note:** Driven by pride to row far beyond the other boats, Santiago hooks a marlin too great to land alone and is towed for days farther out to sea; he kills it and lashes it alongside, but its blood draws the sharks, who devour it on the long voyage home, so he lands only its skeleton — a triumph reduced to bone, yet his spirit intact, with Manolin vowing to fish beside him again.

### Themes
- **Note:** The central themes are endurance and dignity ("a man can be destroyed but not defeated"), the pride and ambition that drive Santiago "too far out", the kinship and mutual respect between man and nature (he calls the marlin "brother"), and the redeeming human love of his bond with Manolin.

### Effects
- **Note:** The Old Man and the Sea is a tale of heroic endurance, and its emotional effect is admiration edged with poignant sorrow: we admire Santiago's undefeated dignity — courage and skill that survive crushing loss — and we grieve, tenderly rather than despairingly, at the cost of his struggle, so the stripped skeleton and the Christ-like climb leave us moved and uplifted rather than triumphant or in despair.

### Message
- **Note:** The novella affirms that a person's worth lies not in what they win but in how they endure — meeting suffering with courage, skill and grace under pressure so that dignity outlasts any material defeat — and it lends that endurance an almost sacred, redemptive nobility.
