### Protagonist
- **Note:** Scout Finch is the novel's evolving centre — a bright, quick-tempered child of Depression-era Maycomb who begins full of innocent certainties, imagining Boo Radley a monster and judging people fast; through witnessing Tom Robinson's unjust trial and finally meeting Boo, and through her own choice to apply Atticus's lesson in empathy, she grows into a girl with genuine moral sight, her childhood innocence traded for understanding.

### Plot
- **Note:** In 1930s Maycomb, Atticus Finch defends Tom Robinson, a Black man falsely accused by Mayella Ewell; he dismantles the Ewells' story in court yet the prejudiced all-white jury convicts Tom anyway, and the humiliated Bob Ewell takes revenge by attacking Jem and Scout — until the reclusive Boo Radley emerges to save them, Ewell dying in the struggle, and Scout at last understands the neighbour she once feared.

### Themes
- **Note:** The controlling ideas are empathy — that you never understand a person "until you climb into his skin and walk around in it" — set against the racial prejudice that blinds Maycomb's justice; the innocence of the "mockingbird" (Tom and Boo) destroyed by others' cruelty; and moral courage, the will to do right even when, as Atticus is, you are "licked before you begin".

### Effects
- **Note:** *To Kill a Mockingbird* is a bildungsroman set against social-realist injustice, and its emotional effect is empathy, poignancy and a tempered hope: we ache at Scout's loss of childhood innocence, bought by the moral understanding she gains, and feel sharp moral discomfort and indignation as an evident innocent is destroyed by prejudice — yet the empathy she learns leaves us moved and quietly hopeful rather than triumphant or amused.

### Message
- **Note:** The novel affirms that prejudice blinds a community and can destroy the innocent — it is a sin to harm the harmless "mockingbird" — yet insists that empathy and the moral courage to do right remain the true response even when they cannot win; you never truly understand another until you see the world from their point of view, and that seeing is the beginning of justice.
