### Quiz: Jane Eyre

1. **Type: MCQ [Tests Context]**
   * **Question:** Jane works as a governess. Why was this role so difficult in the 1840s?
   * **Options:** A) Governesses were paid more than anyone else, B) A governess was educated yet poor — neither a servant nor an equal of the family, leaving her socially trapped, C) Governesses were forbidden by law, D) Only men could be governesses
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The governess occupied an uneasy middle place — refined but dependent and powerless — which drives much of Jane's struggle for dignity.
   * **Why A:** Governesses were poorly paid and insecure, not wealthy.
   * **Why C:** The role was common, not illegal — it was one of few jobs open to educated women.
   * **Why D:** Governesses were women; that is central to Jane's situation.

2. **Type: MCQ [Tests Context]**
   * **Question:** Under Victorian law before reform, what usually happened to a woman's property when she married?
   * **Options:** A) It stayed entirely her own, B) It passed to her husband, leaving married women dependent, C) It was given to the church, D) It was shared equally by law
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. A married woman's property became her husband's — a dependence that makes Jane's insistence on her own worth and inheritance so significant.
   * **Why A:** A wife could not keep her property independent under the old law.
   * **Why C:** Property passed to the husband, not the church.
   * **Why D:** There was no equal split; the law favoured the husband.

3. **Type: True-False [Tests Context]**
   * **Question:** Brontë draws on the Gothic tradition, using Thornfield Hall's mysteries and the madwoman to create fear and suspense.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Strange laughter, a hidden wife and a brooding house give the novel a Gothic atmosphere alongside its realism.
   * **WhyWrong:** The novel does use Gothic elements — the secrets of Thornfield and Bertha in the attic create the mystery and dread of the form.

4. **Type: MCQ [Tests Context]**
   * **Question:** Bertha Mason, Rochester's first wife, comes from the Caribbean. What context does she connect the novel to?
   * **Options:** A) The Victorian railway boom, B) Britain's colonial empire and the wealth drawn from it, C) The French Revolution, D) The invention of photography
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Bertha's Creole origins tie the novel to Britain's colonial economy and the fortunes made overseas — a shadow behind Rochester's wealth.
   * **Why A:** Her role connects to empire and colonial wealth, not railways.
   * **Why C:** She links to the colonies, not the French Revolution.
   * **Why D:** Her significance is colonial, not technological.

   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "I am no bird; and no net ensnares me" declares Jane's refusal to be owned or confined — a keynote of her independence.

6. **Type: Select All [Tests Context]**
   * **Question:** Which Victorian realities shape Jane's world?
   * **Options:** A) Few respectable jobs open to educated women beyond governessing, B) A married woman's dependence on her husband, C) Wealth drawn from Britain's colonies, D) Equal voting rights for women
   * **Correct:** A, B, C
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A,B,C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Limited work for women, marital dependence and colonial wealth all press on Jane's choices.
   * **Why D:** Women had no vote in this period — Jane's independence is personal and moral, won against the law's grain.

7. **Type: MCQ [Tests Themes]**
   * **Question:** What does Jane most fight to hold onto throughout the novel?
   * **Options:** A) Wealth and status, B) Her independence, dignity and moral integrity, C) Revenge on the Reeds, D) A quiet life without love
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Jane will not sacrifice her self-respect or conscience — not to Rochester's wealth nor to St John's duty — which is the novel's moral centre.
   * **Why A:** Jane values integrity over status; she leaves Rochester rather than live compromised.
   * **Why C:** She rises above the wish for revenge as she matures.
   * **Why D:** Jane wants love, but only on terms that keep her dignity intact.

8. **Type: True-False [Tests Themes]**
   * **Question:** Jane insists she is Rochester's equal in soul and feeling, despite the gap in wealth and rank between them.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "I have as much soul as you, — and full as much heart!" asserts a spiritual equality that defies Victorian class and gender hierarchy.
   * **WhyWrong:** Jane does claim equality — she declares she has as much soul and heart as Rochester, whatever the difference in their station.

9. **Type: MCQ [Tests Themes]**
   * **Question:** Why does Jane refuse to marry St John Rivers?
   * **Options:** A) He is too poor, B) He offers duty and a loveless marriage of missionary service, not love — and she will not deny her heart, C) He is already married, D) Her family forbids it
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. St John offers usefulness without love; Jane refuses to betray herself, holding out for a union of equals and true feeling.
   * **Why A:** It is the absence of love, not money, that makes her refuse.
   * **Why C:** St John is free to marry; the problem is that he does not love her.
   * **Why D:** The choice is Jane's own conscience, not a family ban.

   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "I care for myself" is Jane's vow of self-respect — she will keep her own dignity even when unloved and unsupported.
   * **WhyWrong:** The word is "myself" — Jane insists she will value and keep faith with herself, no matter her circumstances.

11. **Type: MCQ [Tests Techniques]**
   * **Question:** The novel is told in the first person, by Jane herself. What is the main effect?
   * **Options:** A) It hides Jane's feelings, B) It draws us close to Jane's inner life, so we share her thoughts and moral struggles, C) It makes the story feel like a newspaper report, D) It removes all emotion
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Jane's first-person voice, often addressing the reader directly, makes us intimate with her conscience and growth.
   * **Why A:** The first-person voice reveals her feelings rather than hiding them.
   * **Why C:** Its intimacy is the opposite of a detached report.
   * **Why D:** It intensifies emotion by placing us inside Jane's mind.

12. **Type: MCQ [Tests Techniques]**
   * **Question:** After Rochester's proposal, a great chestnut tree is split by lightning. What is this an example of?
   * **Options:** A) Pathetic fallacy and symbolism, foreshadowing the coming division between the lovers, B) Onomatopoeia, C) A rhyming couplet, D) A comic aside
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The storm-split tree mirrors and foreshadows the rupture ahead — nature reflecting the human drama.
   * **Why B:** There is no sound-imitation; the image works through symbolism.
   * **Why C:** The novel is prose, not verse.
   * **Why D:** The moment is ominous, not comic.

13. **Type: True-False [Tests Techniques]**
   * **Question:** Brontë uses fire and ice imagery across the novel to contrast passion with cold restraint.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Rochester's warmth and passion are set against St John's icy self-denial, a fire-and-ice pattern that shapes Jane's choices.
   * **WhyWrong:** This contrast does run through the novel — fiery passion against cold restraint helps define the men and choices Jane faces.

14. **Type: Fill [Tests Techniques]**
   * **Question:** A novel that traces a character's growth from childhood to maturity, as Jane Eyre does, is called a [BLANK] novel (a novel of formation).
   * **Answer:** bildungsroman
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Jane Eyre is a bildungsroman — it follows Jane's moral and personal growth from a mistreated child to an independent woman.
   * **WhyWrong:** The term is "bildungsroman" — a coming-of-age novel tracing a character's development, exactly Jane's journey.

15. **Type: MCQ [Tests Characters & Plot]**
   * **Question:** Who is the man Jane falls in love with while working as a governess?
   * **Options:** A) St John Rivers, B) Mr Rochester, the master of Thornfield, C) Mr Brocklehurst, D) John Reed
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Jane loves Mr Rochester, the brooding master of Thornfield Hall where she is governess to his ward.
   * **Why A:** St John Rivers proposes later, but Jane does not love him.
   * **Why C:** Brocklehurst is the harsh master of Lowood School.
   * **Why D:** John Reed is her cruel cousin from childhood.

16. **Type: MCQ [Tests Characters & Plot]**
   * **Question:** What secret prevents Jane from marrying Rochester when she first agrees to?
   * **Options:** A) He is penniless, B) He already has a living wife, Bertha, kept hidden at Thornfield, C) He is her long-lost brother, D) He is already engaged to St John's sister
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Rochester's first wife, Bertha Mason, is alive and hidden in the attic — the revelation that halts the wedding and sends Jane away.
   * **Why A:** Rochester is wealthy; money is not the barrier.
   * **Why C:** They are not related; the obstacle is his existing marriage.
   * **Why D:** The barrier is Bertha, not any engagement to the Rivers family.
