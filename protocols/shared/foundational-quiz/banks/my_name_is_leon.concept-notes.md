### Protagonist
- **Note:** Leon begins as a bewildered, grieving child torn from his mother and his baby brother; his growth is not something done to him but driven by his own fierce love and his slow reaching for belonging — protecting his brother, working the allotment, insisting on who he is — so that by the close he has begun, tentatively, to find a hard-won stability and hope.

### Plot
- **Note:** When Leon's mother becomes unable to cope, he and his baby brother are taken into care; the system's preferences — a readily-adopted white baby against an older, mixed-race boy — cause the brothers' separation, and that loss drives Leon's grief and anger, his reaching for belonging on the allotment, and finally a fragile, hard-won hope.

### Themes
- **Note:** The central ideas are family and separation felt as deeply by a child as by any adult, race and its power to shape a child's treatment in the care and adoption system, grief that surfaces as anger and difficult behaviour, and growth and belonging — the allotment's slowly growing plants mirroring Leon's own healing.

### Effects
- **Note:** *My Name Is Leon* is a coming-of-age novel rooted in social realism, and its emotional effect is empathy and poignant hope: because the adult world's failures reach us through Leon's innocent, half-understanding eyes, his confusion and the injustice done to him cut all the deeper, moving us to a tender ache for what he has lost and a fragile, hard-won hope for the belonging he begins to find.

### Message
- **Note:** The novel insists that every child deserves to be seen and loved as an individual — named, not processed as a case — and quietly indicts a care system whose inequities, including race, can fail the children it should protect, while affirming that patience, love and belonging can nurture a wounded child towards hope.
