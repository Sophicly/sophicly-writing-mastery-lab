### Protagonist
- **Note:** Stanhope, the young company commander, was once a fit, hero-worshipped schoolboy; three years at the front have worn him into an exhausted, whisky-dependent officer with shattered nerves — yet his defining act is a choice, to master his terror and keep leading his men rather than break like Hibbert, and it is that endurance that makes his suffering move us to pity.

### Plot
- **Note:** In a cramped dugout near St Quentin the officers wait powerlessly for the German Spring Offensive of March 1918; high command orders a costly daylight raid to seize a prisoner, in which the steady, fatherly Osborne is killed, and when the long-dreaded attack finally breaks the hopeful young Raleigh is fatally wounded and dies on Stanhope's bed as the dugout is shelled into darkness — each loss following by necessity from the machinery of war the men cannot escape.

### Themes
- **Note:** The central ideas are that true courage is the endurance of fear rather than its absence, that comradeship and mutual care are the one shield against the terror of the front, that duty pulls against the instinct for self-preservation (Hibbert), and that war destroys the youth, nerve and promise of a whole generation.

### Effects
- **Note:** Journey's End is an anti-war tragedy, and its emotional effect is pity and pathos: we grieve at the futile waste of decent, ordinary young lives — gentle Osborne and hopeful Raleigh — and we share the men's helpless dread as they wait for an offensive they cannot stop. The play strips war of glory so that the closing descent into darkness leaves us mourning and desolate rather than triumphant or reassured.

### Message
- **Note:** Drawing on Sherriff's own service, the play insists that war is not glory but the futile waste of ordinary, decent lives, that its true heroism lies in quiet endurance and loyalty to one's comrades, and that the fear and suffering of those who fought deserve honest, unromantic witness.
