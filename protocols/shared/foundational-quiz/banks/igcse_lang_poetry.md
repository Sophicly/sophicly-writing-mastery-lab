# Foundational Quiz Bank — Edexcel IGCSE Language A Poetry (Poems)

Deterministic, code-scored foundational bank (parsed by `SWML_Quiz_Bank`).
15 questions on the actual ANTHOLOGY POEMS — three per poem across the three understanding
dimensions: Recognising the Poem · Form & Features · Meaning & Effects. The picker draws a random 5
per round, stratified across categories. Keys + feedback live server-side and are stripped before
questions reach the client. The AI is never the scorekeeper.

**Concept-based (governed by `FQ-QUESTION-STANDARD.md` § POETRY).** Every item tests the CENTRAL
CONCEPT of its dimension, not surface trivia. **Recognising** keys on the poem's *controlling idea /
argument* (anchored by a signature line), not image-matching; **Form & Features** tests how the form
*shapes meaning*, not its label; **Meaning & Effects** tests the controlling idea + the reader's
response. Distractors are plausible CONCEPTUAL MISREADINGS — for Recognising, other anthology poems
whose *argument differs*.

**Single reading stage — no `@set` tokens.** This anthology has only five poems, read as one
block, so (unlike the AQA Power and Conflict bank) there is no staged rollout by course order —
all five poems are live from the start and every question is available to the picker from day one.

Covers the five anthology poems: "Disabled" (Wilfred Owen), "Out, Out—" (Robert Frost),
"An Unknown Girl" (Moniza Alvi), "The Bright Lights of Sarajevo" (Tony Harrison), "Still I Rise"
(Maya Angelou).

### Quiz: IGCSE Language A Poetry (Poems)

1. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem argues that war's cruellest cost is a young man's irreversible ruin — the very town that once admired him now offering only pity, as he sits "waiting for dark" in his "ghastly suit of grey"?
   * **Options:** A) Disabled, B) Out, Out—, C) The Bright Lights of Sarajevo, D) Still I Rise
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Owen's "Disabled" sets the maimed soldier's glittering past against a bleak present — "waiting for dark" in his "ghastly suit of grey", pitied by the same society that once made him a local hero. The controlling idea is the waste of a life the war has broken cleanly in two.
   * **Why B:** Out, Out— argues how abruptly a single life can end in an accident, not the slow after-cost of war on a survivor.
   * **Why C:** The Bright Lights of Sarajevo argues that ordinary tenderness endures under siege — a whole city's daily survival, not one wounded veteran's isolation.
   * **Why D:** Still I Rise argues for defiant, triumphant resilience, the opposite of this soldier's diminished, pitied stillness.

2. **Type: True-False [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** In "Disabled", the irregular stanza lengths and the constant swing between the soldier's glamorous past and his maimed present are not accidental — the broken form itself enacts a life split cleanly in two by the war.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The uneven stanzas and the restless past/present shifts make the form carry the meaning — the shape on the page is as broken as the life it describes.
   * **WhyWrong:** This is true — the irregular form and the swings between past and present are exactly how Owen makes the structure mirror a shattered life.

3. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What does "Disabled" make us feel about why the soldier enlisted — and what is the effect of that revelation?
   * **Options:** A) He was conscripted against his will, so we feel only political anger, B) He signed up on a vain, thoughtless impulse — to look good in a uniform and impress girls — and the recruiters "wrote his lie; aged nineteen years", so his ruin feels all the more pitiful and wasteful, C) He always longed to die a hero, so we feel his wish was fulfilled, D) His family forced him, so the blame lies with them
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. He joins to "please the giddy jilts" and look "a god in kilts", and the recruiters cheerfully "wrote his lie; aged nineteen years" — the gap between that giddy vanity and the maimed man it produced is what makes the poem so pitiful.
   * **Why A:** No conscription appears — he enlists eagerly and voluntarily, which is exactly what makes his fate ironic rather than merely unjust.
   * **Why C:** The poem's irony is the reverse — a naive craving to look heroic curdles into isolation and regret, not a hero's death.
   * **Why D:** There is no family pressure; the impulse is his own vanity, which deepens the sense of waste.

4. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem argues how suddenly and arbitrarily a life can be cut short — a boy's hand taken by a "buzz saw" that "snarled and rattled in the yard", after which the living simply "turned to their affairs"?
   * **Options:** A) Out, Out—, B) Disabled, C) An Unknown Girl, D) Still I Rise
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Frost's "Out, Out—" turns an ordinary farmyard accident into an argument about how abruptly and meaninglessly a life can end — and how quickly the survivors, "not the one dead", return to work.
   * **Why B:** Disabled mourns a soldier's slow ruin and the long after-cost of war, not a single sudden accident.
   * **Why C:** An Unknown Girl explores belonging and cultural longing in a bazaar, not a fatal accident.
   * **Why D:** Still I Rise asserts defiant survival against oppression, not the arbitrariness of death.

5. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** "Out, Out—" is written in blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter with a plain, conversational rhythm. What does that unshowy form achieve?
   * **Options:** A) Its everyday, speech-like rhythm lulls us into an ordinary farmyard scene, so the sudden accident and the boy's death strike with far greater force, B) Its regular rhyme turns the poem into a cheerful, song-like ballad, C) It signals that the poet could not manage a rhyme scheme, D) It has no bearing on the poem's meaning
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The unrhymed, near-prose rhythm makes the opening feel utterly everyday, so when the saw "leaped out at the boy's hand" the horror lands with maximum shock — the plain form sets the trap.
   * **Why B:** Blank verse is unrhymed, so it is not a rhymed, song-like ballad — and the tone is grave, not cheerful.
   * **Why C:** Blank verse is a deliberate, skilled choice, not a failure to rhyme.
   * **Why D:** The lulling ordinariness of the form is precisely what makes the sudden death so devastating.

6. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What does the abrupt ending of "Out, Out—" — "Little—less—nothing!—and that ended it" — make us feel about the boy's death?
   * **Options:** A) Prolonged grief, as the family mourn for the rest of their lives, B) An unsettling chill at how quickly the living move on — the survivors "turned to their affairs" almost at once, exposing how casually life absorbs a death that is not its own, C) Relief, because the doctor arrives and saves him, D) Ceremony, as the poem closes on a long funeral scene
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The clipped final lines cut off as suddenly as the boy's life, and the survivors' immediate return to work leaves us disturbed by how little a death interrupts the ongoing business of living.
   * **Why A:** The poem stages the opposite of prolonged mourning — a fast, almost cold return to ordinary affairs.
   * **Why C:** "Little—less—nothing!—and that ended it" makes clear the boy dies; no rescue comes.
   * **Why D:** There is no funeral; the poem ends abruptly at the moment of loss, which is the source of its chill.

7. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem explores a speaker's longing to hold on to a cultural heritage that feels both intimate and slightly unfamiliar — dramatised as "an unknown girl" is "hennaing my hand" in an evening bazaar?
   * **Options:** A) An Unknown Girl, B) Still I Rise, C) Disabled, D) Out, Out—
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Alvi's "An Unknown Girl" uses the temporary henna design — applied by a stranger in a bright bazaar — to explore a yearning for a heritage the speaker can touch but not fully claim.
   * **Why B:** Still I Rise asserts defiant resilience against oppression, not a tender, uncertain reaching toward cultural belonging.
   * **Why C:** Disabled centres a wounded soldier's isolation, not a bazaar or a question of cultural identity.
   * **Why D:** Out, Out— depicts a fatal farmyard accident, not an intimate scene of cultural longing.

8. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** How does the loose free-verse form of "An Unknown Girl" — thin, drifting lines and a returning refrain — shape its meaning?
   * **Options:** A) The unrhymed, trailing lines mirror the flowing henna pattern — "a peacock spreads its lines / across my palm" — while the repeated "an unknown girl / is hennaing my hand" enacts the speaker's unfixed sense of belonging between two cultures, B) A strict sonnet locks the poem into a single, fixed argument, C) Marching rhyming couplets drive the poem forward in orderly pairs, D) A pounding refrain builds mounting anger toward the girl
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The drifting, unrhymed lines trace the henna's curving design and the speaker's own in-between identity, and the returning refrain keeps circling back to the fleeting connection she longs to hold.
   * **Why B:** The poem has no fourteen-line shape or single rhymed argument — it is open free verse.
   * **Why C:** Orderly rhyming couplets belong to The Bright Lights of Sarajevo, not this loosely flowing poem.
   * **Why D:** The tone is tender and wistful, not angry — the refrain deepens longing, not rage.

9. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What tension does "An Unknown Girl" make us feel through the henna placed on the speaker's hand?
   * **Options:** A) A quarrel between rival stallholders over money, B) A wistful pull between closeness and distance — she gains "new brown veins" and clings to "these firm peacock lines", yet knows the design "will fade in a week", so her bond with the heritage feels both precious and slipping away, C) A dispute over the price of cloth in the bazaar, D) A soldier's dread on the eve of battle
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The henna gives her "new brown veins" and she clings to "these firm peacock lines", but it "will fade in a week" — the poem leaves us feeling her yearning to hold a heritage that is hers and yet, in London, always partly out of reach.
   * **Why A:** No quarrel between stallholders appears; the poem's concern is inward and emotional.
   * **Why C:** The poem is not about trade or bargaining, but about identity and longing.
   * **Why D:** There is no soldier or battle here — that belongs to Disabled and Out, Out—.

10. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem argues that ordinary human tenderness endures even under siege — young couples courting in blacked-out streets, among the "shell scars" of a bombarded city?
    * **Options:** A) The Bright Lights of Sarajevo, B) An Unknown Girl, C) Disabled, D) Still I Rise
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Harrison's poem follows Sarajevo's people queuing, dodging snipers and courting through the blackout — insisting that everyday love and routine persist inside extraordinary danger, even on ground marked by "shell scars".
    * **Why B:** An Unknown Girl is a peaceful bazaar scene about cultural belonging, not a besieged city.
    * **Why C:** Disabled centres one soldier's isolation after war, not a whole community's daily survival.
    * **Why D:** Still I Rise is a personal declaration of resilience, not a portrait of communal life under bombardment.

11. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** "The Bright Lights of Sarajevo" is built from tight rhyming couplets — pairs such as "pass"/"gas" and "prams"/"grams". What does that insistent, paired rhyme achieve against the poem's subject?
    * **Options:** A) The neat, ordered couplets press a stubborn sense of order onto the chaos and danger of the siege, so the form itself dramatises life's refusal to fall apart, B) The rhyme makes the poem sound light and comic, mocking the citizens' fear, C) The rhymes prove the poem has no real structure or control, D) The couplets have no effect on how we read the danger
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Harrison imposes disciplined couplets on scenes of shelling and blackout, and the tension between that orderly rhyme and the chaos it describes enacts the citizens' determination to hold ordinary life together.
    * **Why B:** The controlled rhyme heightens the poignancy; it does not mock the citizens or turn the poem comic.
    * **Why C:** The consistent couplets are a tight, deliberate structure — the opposite of formlessness.
    * **Why D:** The clash between orderly form and dangerous content is central to the poem's meaning.

12. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What feeling does Harrison create by setting ordinary tenderness — queuing, courtship — against the danger of shelling?
    * **Options:** A) He trivialises the citizens' suffering, B) The fragile normality of everyday life and love feels all the more precious and precarious, because it persists in the shadow of "shell scars" and snipers, C) He turns the siege into comedy, D) He removes any sense of danger from the poem
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Set against blackout, snipers and "shell scars", the small human moments of courtship and routine feel both cherished and endangered — that is the ache the poem leaves us with.
    * **Why A:** The tender ordinariness heightens the suffering rather than making light of it.
    * **Why C:** The tone stays sombre and gentle; the danger is never played for laughs.
    * **Why D:** Snipers, shelling and "shell scars" run through the poem; the tenderness is precious precisely because the danger is real.

13. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem answers oppression with defiant, joyful resilience — refusing those who "write me down in history / With your bitter, twisted lies" and returning again to the refrain "I rise"?
    * **Options:** A) Still I Rise, B) Disabled, C) Out, Out—, D) The Bright Lights of Sarajevo
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Angelou's "Still I Rise" confronts slander and injustice head-on — "But still, like dust, I'll rise" — building through its repeated "I rise" into an argument for unbreakable dignity.
    * **Why B:** Disabled mourns a wounded soldier's isolation and diminishment — it carries no note of defiance.
    * **Why C:** Out, Out— ends in abrupt, indifferent silence, the opposite of a rising refrain.
    * **Why D:** The Bright Lights of Sarajevo quietly affirms endurance through a whole city, not one voice's personal defiance.

14. **Type: Select All [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** Which of these formal features does Angelou use to build the mounting defiance of "Still I Rise"?
    * **Options:** A) A repeated refrain, "I rise", that gathers force as the poem progresses, B) Rhetorical questions flung at an oppressor, such as "Does my sassiness upset you?", C) Extended imagery of natural, unstoppable forces — dust, tides, "a black ocean" — to embody resilience that cannot be held down, D) A tightly rhymed sonnet built around a single turn (volta)
    * **Correct:** A, B, C
    * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A,B,C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The rising refrain, the taunting rhetorical questions and the imagery of natural forces — dust, "the certainty of tides", "a black ocean" — combine so the form itself swells into unstoppable, cumulative defiance.
    * **Why D:** The poem is built from short stanzas and a recurring refrain, not a single fourteen-line sonnet with one turn.

15. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What does "Still I Rise" ultimately make us feel it is asserting?
    * **Options:** A) That suffering has permanently defeated the speaker, B) That she will rise above oppression and historical injustice with joyful, unstoppable defiance — "the dream and the hope of the slave", carrying forward the hopes of those who came before her, C) That she wants only revenge on those who wronged her, D) That she wishes to forget her history entirely
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Angelou closes on "I am the dream and the hope of the slave. / I rise / I rise / I rise" — asserting that she rises not for herself alone but bearing her ancestors' hopes, so the feeling is triumphant rather than bitter.
    * **Why A:** The poem insists on the opposite at every turn — repeated rising, never defeat.
    * **Why C:** The tone is self-assertion and joy, not a craving for revenge.
    * **Why D:** She draws strength from her ancestry and history; the poem embraces it rather than erasing it.
