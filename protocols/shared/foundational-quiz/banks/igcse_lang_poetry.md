# Foundational Quiz Bank — Edexcel IGCSE Language A Poetry (Poems)

Deterministic, code-scored foundational recall bank (parsed by `SWML_Quiz_Bank`).
15 questions on the actual ANTHOLOGY POEMS (Tier B) — three per poem across Neil's three
understanding dimensions: Recognising the Poem · Form & Features · Meaning & Effects. Unlike
the universal forms bank (`poetic_forms.md`, Tier A), these are poem-specific, testing what the
student has actually read. The picker draws a random 5 per round, stratified across categories.
Keys + feedback live server-side and are stripped before questions reach the client. The AI is
never the scorekeeper.

**Single reading stage — no `@set` tokens.** This anthology has only five poems, read as one
block, so (unlike the AQA Power and Conflict bank) there is no staged rollout by course order —
all five poems are live from the start and every question is available to the picker from day one.

Covers the five anthology poems: "Disabled" (Wilfred Owen), "Out, Out—" (Robert Frost),
"An Unknown Girl" (Moniza Alvi), "The Bright Lights of Sarajevo" (Tony Harrison), "Still I Rise"
(Maya Angelou).

### Quiz: IGCSE Language A Poetry (Poems)

1. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem depicts a young soldier "waiting for dark" in a wheelchair, in his "ghastly suit of grey", pitied by the same town that once admired him?
   * **Options:** A) Disabled, B) Out, Out—, C) The Bright Lights of Sarajevo, D) Still I Rise
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Owen's "Disabled" opens with the maimed soldier "waiting for dark" in his "ghastly suit of grey", his legs lost to the war that once made him a local hero.
   * **Why B:** Out, Out— follows a boy's death in a sawmill accident, not a soldier's long return from war.
   * **Why C:** The Bright Lights of Sarajevo is set among a besieged city's ordinary citizens, not a single wheelchair-bound veteran.
   * **Why D:** Still I Rise is a triumphant assertion of identity, not a study of a wounded, isolated man.

2. **Type: True-False [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** In "Disabled", the irregular stanza lengths and the shifts between the soldier's vivid past and his bleak present mirror his fractured, divided life.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Owen's uneven stanzas and the swing between the athletic, admired young man and the maimed present enact a life broken cleanly in two by the war.
   * **WhyWrong:** This is true — the irregular form and the past/present shifts are exactly how Owen structures the soldier's shattered life.

3. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What does "Disabled" suggest about why the soldier enlisted in the first place?
   * **Options:** A) He was legally conscripted against his will, B) He joined on a thoughtless, glamorous impulse — to look smart in a uniform and impress girls — and lied about his age to be accepted, C) His family forced him to enlist, D) He had always dreamed of dying a hero's death
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The recruiters smiling as they "wrote his lie: aged nineteen years" exposes how casually — and how young — he was waved into a war whose true cost he could not yet imagine.
   * **Why A:** No conscription is described; he enlists voluntarily, and eagerly.
   * **Why C:** There is no suggestion of family pressure in the poem.
   * **Why D:** The poem's irony is the reverse — a naive, vain reason for joining curdles into isolation and regret, not fulfilled glory.

4. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem describes a boy's hand cut by a "buzz-saw" in a woodyard, after which he dies while the others "turned to their affairs"?
   * **Options:** A) Out, Out—, B) Disabled, C) An Unknown Girl, D) Still I Rise
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Frost's "Out, Out—" opens with the "buzz-saw" that "snarled and rattled in the yard", and closes on the survivors who, "since they / Were not the one dead, turned to their affairs".
   * **Why B:** Disabled mourns a soldier maimed by war, not a boy killed in a farmyard accident.
   * **Why C:** An Unknown Girl takes place in a bazaar with a henna pattern, not a woodyard.
   * **Why D:** Still I Rise voices defiant survival, not a sudden, fatal accident.

5. **Type: Fill [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** "Out, Out—" is written in [BLANK] verse — unrhymed lines of iambic pentameter — whose plain, speech-like rhythm suits its deceptively ordinary opening.
   * **Answer:** blank
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The unrhymed, conversational rhythm of blank verse lulls the reader into an everyday farmyard scene, so the sudden accident strikes with far greater force.
   * **WhyWrong:** The word is "blank" — blank verse, the unrhymed iambic pentameter Frost uses throughout the poem.

6. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What does the ending of "Out, Out—" suggest about the reaction to the boy's death?
   * **Options:** A) The others are shown grieving for the rest of their lives, B) The survivors' abrupt return to their own business — "turned to their affairs" — reveals an unsettling, almost indifferent acceptance that life simply carries on, C) The poem closes with a lengthy funeral scene, D) The doctor arrives in time and saves the boy
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The clipped, unceremonious close cuts the poem off just as the boy's life is cut off, exposing how quickly the living move past a death that was not their own.
   * **Why A:** The text reveals the opposite — a fast, almost cold return to ordinary business, not prolonged mourning.
   * **Why C:** No funeral appears; the poem ends abruptly at the moment of loss.
   * **Why D:** "Little—less—nothing!—and that ended it" makes clear the boy dies; the doctor cannot save him.

7. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem is set in an evening bazaar, where the speaker has an intricate pattern worked onto her hand by a girl she has never met?
   * **Options:** A) An Unknown Girl, B) Still I Rise, C) Disabled, D) Out, Out—
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Alvi's "An Unknown Girl" is set in a bustling evening bazaar, where a stranger is "hennaing my hand" with a delicate, temporary design.
   * **Why B:** Still I Rise is a public declaration of resilience, not a quiet, intimate bazaar scene.
   * **Why C:** Disabled is set around a soldier's wheelchair and his memories of home, not a market stall.
   * **Why D:** Out, Out— is set in a farmyard woodlot, not a bazaar.

8. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** How does the free-verse form of "An Unknown Girl" support its meaning?
   * **Options:** A) Its loose, unrhymed lines and the returning refrain "In the evening bazaar ... an unknown girl / is hennaing my hand" echo the flowing henna design and the speaker's uncertain sense of belonging between two cultures, B) Its strict sonnet form argues a single fixed idea, C) Its rhyming couplets march the poem forward in orderly pairs, D) A repeating refrain builds mounting anger
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The thin, drifting, unrhymed lines trace the henna pattern's flowing lines — "a peacock spreads its lines / across my palm" — and the speaker's own unfixed identity, caught between her British life and a heritage she can touch but not fully claim.
   * **Why B:** The poem has neither a fixed fourteen-line shape nor a single rhymed argument; it is open free verse.
   * **Why C:** Rhyming couplets belong to The Bright Lights of Sarajevo, not this loosely flowing poem.
   * **Why D:** The tone is tender and reflective, not driven by an angry refrain.

9. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What tension does "An Unknown Girl" explore through the henna design placed on the speaker's hand?
   * **Options:** A) A quarrel between two rival stallholders, B) The speaker's longing to hold on to a fleeting, intimate connection with a cultural heritage that feels both close to her and slightly unfamiliar — she gains "new brown veins" yet knows the pattern "will fade in a week", C) A dispute over the price of goods in the bazaar, D) A soldier's fear on the eve of battle
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The henna gives her "new brown veins" but she knows it "will fade in a week", and the poem's closing image — reaching out "with my hands outstretched longing for the unknown girl / in the neon bazaar" — captures her yearning to hold on to a heritage she experiences as both hers and, in some ways, distant from her London life.
   * **Why A:** No quarrel between stallholders appears in the poem.
   * **Why C:** The poem is not concerned with trade or bargaining.
   * **Why D:** There is no soldier or battle in this poem; that belongs to Disabled and Out, Out—.

10. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem depicts ordinary life and courtship continuing between citizens of a besieged city, even as it is shelled around them?
    * **Options:** A) The Bright Lights of Sarajevo, B) An Unknown Girl, C) Disabled, D) Still I Rise
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Harrison's "The Bright Lights of Sarajevo" follows the city's people queuing, bartering and courting through the shelling and blackouts of the siege, insisting on ordinary tenderness inside extraordinary danger.
    * **Why B:** An Unknown Girl is set in a peaceful bazaar scene, not a besieged city under fire.
    * **Why C:** Disabled centres on one soldier's isolation after the war, not a whole city's daily survival.
    * **Why D:** Still I Rise is a personal declaration of resilience, not a wartime city scene.

11. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** How is "The Bright Lights of Sarajevo" structured?
    * **Options:** A) In rhyming couplets — pairs of lines that rhyme, such as "pass"/"gas" and "prams"/"grams" — running through the poem, B) In free verse, with no fixed rhyme, C) As a single fourteen-line sonnet, D) In blank verse, unrhymed throughout
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Harrison builds the poem from rhyming couplets, pairing lines such as "pass"/"gas" and "prams"/"grams", imposing a tight, orderly rhyme on the chaos of the siege.
    * **Why B:** The poem rhymes consistently in paired lines, so it is not free verse.
    * **Why C:** It runs as a longer sequence of rhymed couplets, not one fourteen-line sonnet.
    * **Why D:** Blank verse is unrhymed; this poem's lines rhyme throughout in couplets.

12. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What effect does Harrison create by setting scenes of ordinary tenderness — queuing, courtship — inside the strict order of rhyming couplets, against the danger of shelling?
    * **Options:** A) It trivialises the suffering of the besieged city, B) The neat, controlled rhyme contrasts with the chaos and danger of war, so the fragile normality of everyday life and love feels all the more precious and precarious, C) It turns the poem into comedy, D) It removes any sense of danger from the poem
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The disciplined couplets sit against scenes of shelling and blackout, and that contrast between order and chaos makes the small, human moments of courtship and routine feel both cherished and fragile.
    * **Why A:** The ordered rhyme heightens the poignancy of the citizens' suffering rather than making light of it.
    * **Why C:** The tone remains sombre and tender throughout, despite the orderly rhyme.
    * **Why D:** Shelling and danger are present throughout the poem; the couplets frame the danger, they do not erase it.

13. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem repeats the defiant refrain "I rise", answering those who would "write me down in history / With your bitter, twisted lies"?
    * **Options:** A) Still I Rise, B) Disabled, C) Out, Out—, D) The Bright Lights of Sarajevo
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Angelou's "Still I Rise" answers oppression and slander directly — "You may write me down in history / With your bitter, twisted lies... But still, like dust, I'll rise" — building to the recurring refrain "I rise".
    * **Why B:** Disabled mourns a wounded soldier's isolation; it carries no defiant refrain.
    * **Why C:** Out, Out— ends in an abrupt, indifferent silence, not a rising refrain.
    * **Why D:** The Bright Lights of Sarajevo is built on rhyming couplets, not a repeated refrain of defiance.

14. **Type: Select All [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** Which of the following are features Angelou uses to build "Still I Rise"?
    * **Options:** A) A repeated refrain, "I rise", that gathers force as the poem progresses, B) Rhetorical questions aimed at an oppressor, such as "Does my sassiness upset you?", C) Extended imagery of natural forces — dust, tides, an ocean — to embody unstoppable resilience, D) A tightly rhymed sonnet form built around a single turn (volta)
    * **Correct:** A, B, C
    * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A,B,C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Angelou combines a rising refrain, pointed rhetorical questions, and natural imagery — dust, "moons and suns", "the certainty of tides" — to build cumulative, unstoppable defiance.
    * **Why D:** The poem is built from short stanzas and a recurring refrain rather than a single fourteen-line sonnet with one turn.

15. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What does "Still I Rise" ultimately assert?
    * **Options:** A) That suffering has permanently defeated the speaker, B) That the speaker will rise above oppression and historical injustice with unstoppable, joyful defiance, carrying forward the hopes of those who came before her, C) That she seeks revenge on those who have wronged her, D) That she wishes to forget her history entirely
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Angelou closes on "I am the dream and the hope of the slave. / I rise / I rise / I rise" — asserting that she rises not only for herself but carrying the hopes of her ancestors with her.
    * **Why A:** The poem insists on the opposite at every turn — repeated rising, never defeat.
    * **Why C:** The tone is self-assertion and joy, not a desire for revenge on her oppressors.
    * **Why D:** She draws strength from her history and ancestry; the poem embraces it rather than erasing it.
