## UNIT 1

### Ballad
- **Definition:** A narrative poem (one that tells a story) rooted in folk songs that ordinary people passed on by memory, so it is built to be remembered and to be spoken or sung aloud.
- **Features:** Usually quatrains (four-line stanzas) in ballad metre: a four-beat line, then a three-beat line, again and again (iambic tetrameter and iambic trimeter; an iamb is a soft beat then a strong one, da-DUM). Usually rhymed ABCB or ABAB, often with a refrain (a line or chorus that repeats), in simple, everyday language. Poets often change the beat: “The Charge of the Light Brigade” gallops in two-beat lines of strong-soft-soft (dactylic dimeter: “Half a league, half a league”).
- **Effects:** Its steady, regular rhythm can feel like a heartbeat and carry the reader towards the end of the story, while the repetition (the refrain) fixes the story in the memory.
- **Form & Meaning:** The form’s simplicity often contrasts with the complexity or tragedy of the story it tells, and its regular beat can make the ending feel inevitable, as if the characters cannot escape it.

### Epic
- **Definition:** A very long poem in a grand, formal style about heroic deeds, often beginning in medias res (in the middle of the action).
- **Features:** English epics have no single metre: Milton’s “Paradise Lost” is in blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter: ten-syllable lines with five da-DUM beats), while Spenser and Pope used rhyme. What epics share is an opening in medias res (in the middle of the action), elevated diction (grand, formal word choices) and vast settings across time or place.
- **Effects:** Its huge scale and grand language create a sense of awe, making the reader a witness to something monumental.
- **Form & Meaning:** Poets sometimes borrow the epic’s usual features only to turn them upside down: making an ordinary, personal experience feel epic, or collapsing epic grandeur to show it failing.

### Lyric
- **Definition:** A usually short poem in the speaker’s own voice that puts feeling and the music of the words before telling a story.
- **Features:** Usually short, with rich figurative language (images and comparisons, such as metaphors and similes), patterns of rhythm and sound, and a focus on personal experience.
- **Effects:** Because it centres one person’s feeling, a lyric can hold a reader’s own grief or joy and make them feel understood.
- **Form & Meaning:** A simple, musical lyric can carry intense or even political content; because it is easy to take in, its serious message reaches more people.

## UNIT 2

### Elegy
- **Definition:** A poem of mourning about a loss (a death, a relationship, or a way of life) that traditionally moves towards some comfort.
- **Features:** A reflective, thoughtful tone; often a formal structure, such as iambic pentameter (ten-syllable lines with five da-DUM beats), rhyming couplets (pairs of lines that rhyme) or a set stanza pattern; and themes of death and memory.
- **Effects:** An elegy makes grief shared, inviting the reader to mourn and to think about loss and death alongside the speaker.
- **Form & Meaning:** A shift or break in the poem’s structure can act out the finality of the loss itself, so the form performs the ending the words describe.

### Ode
- **Definition:** A formal poem of praise for its subject (an object, a season or an idea), raising it to something worth thinking deeply about.
- **Features:** Elevated diction (grand, formal word choices), often complex rhyme schemes, rich sensory imagery (details of sight, sound, touch, taste and smell) and apostrophe (speaking directly to the subject, as if it could hear).
- **Effects:** Its rich, formal language slows the reader down into a calm, focused state of thinking about the subject.
- **Form & Meaning:** An ode’s pattern of praise can be turned upside down, so a poem that opens like praise ends up exposing irony, mockery or loss.

### Sonnet
- **Definition:** A fourteen-line poem, usually in iambic pentameter (ten-syllable lines with five da-DUM beats), built around a turn, or volta, where the argument or feeling changes direction.
- **Features:** Two classic types: the Petrarchan (an octave, eight lines, setting out a problem, then a sestet, six lines, answering it) and the Shakespearean (three quatrains, four-line groups, then a closing rhyming couplet, two lines that rhyme). Each has a fixed rhyme scheme.
- **Effects:** A sonnet builds tension as strong feeling pushes against its tight structure, then releases it at the volta (the turn); its neat logic can make an argument feel proven.
- **Form & Meaning:** The sonnet’s tight, traditional structure has sometimes been broken or turned upside down to voice serious political protest instead of romantic praise.

### Dramatic Monologue
- **Definition:** A poem in which one speaker talks to a silent listener we never hear, in a particular dramatic situation, and gives away their true character without meaning to.
- **Features:** One speaker; a silent, implied listener (someone the speaker is clearly talking to, who never answers); a defined dramatic situation; and depth that comes from the gap between what the speaker says and the truth.
- **Effects:** The reader becomes a judge or a detective, working out the truth the speaker gives away through their own words.
- **Form & Meaning:** Because the speaker is holding something back from the listener, the form can reveal a disturbing mind all the more chillingly through calm, controlled language.

## UNIT 3

### Free Verse
- **Definition:** Poetry with no fixed metre (no regular pattern of beats) and no set rhyme scheme, though it may still use some rhyme or other poetic devices.
- **Features:** Lines of different lengths and rhythms, enjambment (a sentence running on past the end of a line) for flow, and the way the lines are set out on the page, which can itself carry meaning.
- **Effects:** Breaking the fixed rules can make a poem feel honest and immediate, and can give a voice to people who are usually ignored, or challenge accepted ideas.
- **Form & Meaning:** Abandoning the traditional rules is itself a meaningful choice, often signalling freedom, resistance, or a deliberate break from structures linked with power or tradition.

### Narrative
- **Definition:** A poem that tells a story through characters, plot and setting, and can borrow features of other forms (such as ballad or dramatic elements).
- **Features:** A focus on telling a story, characterisation (how the characters are shown), events told in time order or out of order, and often dialogue (characters speaking).
- **Effects:** As the plot builds towards its ending, readers feel for the characters, feel tension and suspense, and finally catharsis (a release of strong feeling).
- **Form & Meaning:** A narrative poem’s structure can itself break down to act out a breakdown in the story: a clear beginning dissolving into disorder as events overwhelm a character.

### Hybrid Forms
- **Definition:** When a poet deliberately blends two or more forms in one poem, creating complex, sometimes contradictory effects.
- **Features:** A poem may borrow the structure or tone of one form and combine it with the content or closeness of another: for example, an epic’s grand scale used to make a private, inner experience feel huge.
- **Effects:** Blending forms can layer one form’s effects over another’s, producing a richer, sometimes conflicting response that neither form could create alone.
- **Form & Meaning:** Spotting when and why a poet combines forms, and what the mix lets the poem do, is what separates competent analysis from exceptional analysis.

### Interior Monologue
- **Definition:** A form that shows a character’s private thoughts as they really are, with no listener and no performance for an audience.
- **Features:** Direct access to a character’s mind, often as stream of consciousness (thoughts written down as they flow, unedited), without the holding back a listener would cause.
- **Effects:** Because it drops every filter, interior monologue can create a close connection with the character, or a feeling of uncomfortably looking in on their privacy.
- **Form & Meaning:** Interior monologue can treat a person’s inner life as worth serious attention, as important as anything that happens on the outside.
