## UNIT 1

### Ballad
- **Definition:** A narrative poem rooted in oral folk culture, built for memorability and designed to be spoken or sung aloud.
- **Features:** Quatrains alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter (ballad metre), an ABCB or ABAB rhyme scheme, refrains, and simple, everyday language.
- **Effects:** Its driving, regular rhythm can synchronise with the reader's own pulse, pulling them bodily towards the story's conclusion, while repetition lodges the narrative in memory.
- **Form & Meaning:** The form's simplicity often contrasts with the complexity or tragedy of the events it narrates, and its rigid metre can create a sense of inevitability the characters cannot escape.

### Epic
- **Definition:** A poem of great length and elevated style recounting heroic deeds, often beginning in medias res (in the middle of the action).
- **Features:** Blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter) in the English tradition, an in medias res opening, elevated formal diction, and vast settings across time or place.
- **Effects:** Its scale and elevated diction create a sense of awe, positioning the reader as a witness to something monumental.
- **Form & Meaning:** Poets sometimes borrow epic conventions only to subvert them, turning a personal or ordinary experience into something worthy of epic treatment — or collapsing epic grandeur to expose its failure.

### Lyric
- **Definition:** A typically short, subjective poem that prioritises the poet's own musicality and inner feeling over telling a story.
- **Features:** Shorter length, rich figurative language, rhythmic and sound patterning, and a focus on subjective, personal experience.
- **Effects:** Because it centres personal feeling, the lyric can act as a container for a reader's own grief or joy, validating what they feel.
- **Form & Meaning:** A simple, musical lyric form can carry intense or even political content, its accessibility making a serious message more widely felt.

## UNIT 2

### Elegy
- **Definition:** A poem of mourning that explores loss — for a death, a relationship, or a way of life — and traditionally moves towards some form of consolation.
- **Features:** A reflective, introspective tone, often a formal structure (iambic pentameter, couplets, or a set stanza pattern), and themes of mortality and memory.
- **Effects:** The elegy makes grief communal, inviting the reader to share in mourning and reflect on loss and mortality alongside the speaker.
- **Form & Meaning:** A shift or break in the poem's structure can enact the finality of loss itself, so that form performs the emotional "close" the content describes.

### Ode
- **Definition:** A formal, elevated poem of praise for its subject — an object, a season, or an idea — raising it to something worthy of contemplation.
- **Features:** Elevated diction, complex rhyme schemes, rich sensory imagery, and apostrophe (direct address to the subject).
- **Effects:** Its dense, elevated language slows the reader's pace, inducing a calm, focused state of contemplation on the subject.
- **Form & Meaning:** The ode's structure of praise can be inverted or subverted, so that a poem which opens like praise instead exposes irony, mockery, or loss.

### Sonnet
- **Definition:** A fourteen-line poem, typically in iambic pentameter, organised around a turn, or volta, in its argument or feeling.
- **Features:** Two classic types — the Petrarchan (an octave posing a problem, then a sestet offering resolution) and the Shakespearean (three quatrains then a closing couplet) — each built around a fixed rhyme scheme.
- **Effects:** The sonnet creates tension as feeling strains against its tight structure, releasing at the volta, and its resolved logic can train a reader to accept an argument as though proven.
- **Form & Meaning:** The sonnet's tightly organised, traditional structure has, in some cases, been broken or inverted to voice serious political dissent rather than romantic praise.

### Dramatic Monologue
- **Definition:** A poem in which a single speaker addresses a silent, implied listener within a specific dramatic situation, unintentionally revealing their true character through what they say.
- **Features:** One speaker addressing an implied audience, a defined dramatic situation, and psychological depth accessed through the gap between the speaker's words and the truth.
- **Effects:** The reader is placed in the role of judge or detective, decoding the truth a speaker unintentionally reveals through their own words.
- **Form & Meaning:** Because the speaker maintains some reservation before their silent listener, the form can expose disturbing psychology all the more chillingly through calm, controlled language.

## UNIT 3

### Free Verse
- **Definition:** Poetry with no fixed metrical pattern and no set rhyme scheme, though it may still use occasional rhyme or other poetic devices.
- **Features:** Varied line lengths and rhythms, enjambment for fluidity, and visual arrangement on the page that can itself carry meaning.
- **Effects:** Its rejection of fixed rules can create a sense of authenticity and immediacy, and can validate marginalised voices or challenge established norms.
- **Form & Meaning:** Abandoning traditional constraints is itself a meaningful choice — often signalling freedom, resistance, or a deliberate break from structures associated with power or tradition.

### Narrative
- **Definition:** A poem that tells a story through characters, plot and setting, and can incorporate features of other forms (such as ballad or dramatic elements).
- **Features:** Storytelling focus, characterisation, linear or non-linear progression, and frequent use of dialogue.
- **Effects:** As the plot builds towards resolution, readers feel identification with its characters, tension, suspense, and eventual catharsis.
- **Form & Meaning:** A narrative poem's structure can itself collapse or fragment to enact a breakdown in its story — a clear beginning dissolving into disordered form as events overwhelm a character.

### Hybrid Forms
- **Definition:** When a poet deliberately blends two or more forms together within a single poem, creating complex, sometimes contradictory effects.
- **Features:** A poem may borrow structural or tonal conventions from one form while combining them with the content or intimacy of another — for example, epic scale used to elevate a personal, interior experience.
- **Effects:** Hybridity can layer one form's effects over another's, producing a richer, sometimes contradictory response that neither form alone could create.
- **Form & Meaning:** Recognising when and why a poet combines forms — and what that combination allows the poem to do — separates competent analysis from genuinely exceptional analysis.

### Interior Monologue
- **Definition:** A form that presents a character's raw, unfiltered private thought, with no addressed listener and no performance for an audience.
- **Features:** Unmediated, stream-of-consciousness access to a character's mind, without the reservation an addressed listener would create.
- **Effects:** Because it drops all filters, interior monologue can create intimate connection with the character, or a sense of uncomfortable voyeurism.
- **Form & Meaning:** Interior monologue can validate the private, internal life as worthy of serious literary attention, treating psychological experience as significant as external action.
