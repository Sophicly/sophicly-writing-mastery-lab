### Protagonist
- **Note:** Brutus is the play's tragic hero: an honoured, principled Roman loved even by Caesar, whose hamartia is not envy but his idealism — persuaded by Cassius that killing his friend is a noble duty to save the republic, he chooses to act, then dooms his own cause by sparing Antony and letting him speak, until defeat at Philippi drives him to take his own life, mourned by Antony as "the noblest Roman of them all".

### Plot
- **Note:** Cassius flatters Brutus into a conspiracy against the ambitious Caesar; the senators assassinate him — "Et tu, Brute? Then fall, Caesar" — but Brutus's misjudgement in letting Antony deliver the funeral oration ("Friends, Romans, countrymen") turns the fickle crowd, unleashing the civil war that ends with the conspirators' defeat and the suicides of Cassius and Brutus at Philippi.

### Themes
- **Note:** The central themes are human responsibility over fate ("the fault … is not in our stars, / But in ourselves"), the power of persuasive rhetoric to sway a fickle crowd and reshape events, the danger of ambition and the fear of tyranny, and the tragedy of honour and idealism divorced from political realism, where noble motives cannot redeem a violent deed.

### Effects
- **Note:** *Julius Caesar* is a tragedy, and its emotional effect is pity and fear: we pity a genuinely honourable man who destroys himself through his own misjudgement — his ruin feels like a terrible waste of real virtue — and we fear because Brutus is recognisably "like us", so his fall warns that even honest ideals, badly judged, could undo anyone; the storms and omens deepen the dread, and the close leaves us moved rather than satisfied.

### Message
- **Note:** The play warns that political violence, even for a good cause, unleashes chaos rather than liberty — the conspirators destroy the very republic they meant to save — and that no nobility of motive can redeem the deed, affirming that human beings shape their own fate through their choices and must answer for them.
