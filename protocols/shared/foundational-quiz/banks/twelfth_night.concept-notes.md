### Protagonist
- **Note:** Viola begins a grief-stricken, shipwrecked stranger cast alone into Illyria, believing her twin drowned; her defining choice is to disguise herself as the page "Cesario" for safety and to serve Orsino faithfully, and by her wit, constancy and faith that "time" — not force — will "untangle this", she moves from concealed, isolated outsider to a woman revealed as herself and united in love.

### Plot
- **Note:** Viola's male disguise sets the comedy's engine running: sent by Orsino to woo Olivia, she makes Olivia fall for "Cesario" while she herself loves Orsino, tying a knot none can undo; the arrival of her identical twin Sebastian — mistaken for Cesario and married by Olivia — is the turning point that lets recognition of the twins loose every confusion into marriage, while Maria's forged letter gulls the vain Malvolio in a comic sub-plot.

### Themes
- **Note:** The controlling ideas are love in its many forms — Orsino's self-indulgent "food of love", Malvolio's vain self-love, and Viola's true constancy weighed against them — the gap between appearance and reality worked through disguise and mistaken identity ("I am not what I am"), wisdom wearing the fool's motley ("better a witty fool than a foolish wit"), and festive misrule that overturns order before restoring it.

### Effects
- **Note:** Twelfth Night is a romantic comedy, and its emotional effect is delight, warmth and relief: we take pleasure in watching a tangled world of disguise and mistaken love unravel into marriage — the joy of disorder resolved into harmony — and in knowing more than the self-deceived Malvolio, whose vanity in yellow stockings is comic, not fearful. The bittersweet undercurrent, caught in Feste's "the rain it raineth every day", deepens the warmth rather than souring it, sending us out moved and gladdened.

### Message
- **Note:** The play affirms that love, however foolish or misdirected it first appears, is a generous force that — given time and good humour — rights itself and knits the community together in marriage; that a season of playful disorder is fertile rather than destructive, resolving into renewed harmony; and that joyless self-importance, embodied in Malvolio against Sir Toby's "cakes and ale", is the true enemy of the festive generosity the comedy celebrates.
