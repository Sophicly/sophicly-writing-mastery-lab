# Foundational Quiz Bank — SQA Higher Scottish Poetry Collection (Poems)

Deterministic, code-scored foundational recall bank (parsed by `SWML_Quiz_Bank`).
18 questions on the actual ANTHOLOGY POEMS (Tier B) — three per poem across Neil's three
understanding dimensions: Recognising the Poem · Form & Features · Meaning & Effects. Unlike
the universal forms bank (`poetic_forms.md`, Tier A), these are poem-specific, testing what the
student has actually read. The picker draws a random 5 per round, stratified across categories.
Keys + feedback live server-side and are stripped before questions reach the client. The AI is
never the scorekeeper.

Categories: Recognising the Poem · Form & Features · Meaning & Effects
Types: MCQ · Fill · True-False · Select All

This is the SQA Higher English Scottish poetry collection — a single multi-author group of six
poems set for the 2025-26 session onward (first assessment 2026), so there is no set-staging
token here: all six poems are read together as one collection and every question is live from
the first round.

The six poems: **Thomas the Rhymer** (traditional ballad) · **Composed in August** (Robert Burns)
· **The Bonnie Broukit Bairn** (Hugh MacDiarmid) · **Summit of Corrie Etchachan** (Nan Shepherd)
· **Da Clearance** (Rhoda Bulter) · **33** (MacGillivray). Quotations are verified against the
Scottish Poetry Library and, for the traditional ballad, standard Child Ballad 37 texts.

### Quiz: SQA Higher Scottish Poetry Collection

<!-- ============================ POEM 1: THOMAS THE RHYMER ============================ -->

1. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem turns a mortal's crossing into the supernatural into a bargain — a man who follows a fairy queen beneath the Eildon Tree to Elfland for seven years, surrendering his own will there, and comes back granted "the tongue that can never lie"?
   * **Options:** A) Thomas the Rhymer, B) Composed in August, C) Da Clearance, D) 33
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The traditional ballad "Thomas the Rhymer" follows Thomas's encounter with the Queen of Elfland beneath the Eildon Tree, his seven-year sojourn, and the apple that gives him "the tongue that can never lie".
   * **Why B:** Composed in August is Burns's celebration of autumn birdlife and courtship, not a supernatural ballad.
   * **Why C:** Da Clearance recounts a Shetland eviction, not a fairy encounter.
   * **Why D:** 33 is a sonnet inspired by Mary Queen of Scots' execution, not a medieval ballad.

2. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** What form does "Thomas the Rhymer" take, and what is a key structural feature?
   * **Options:** A) A traditional ballad in four-line stanzas, built on dialogue and a choice of three roads, B) A Petrarchan sonnet with an octave and sestet, C) A single unrhymed free-verse stanza, D) A rhymed song addressed to a lover
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. As a traditional ballad, the poem unfolds in quatrains driven by dialogue between Thomas and the Queen, structured around her offer of three roads — righteousness, wickedness, and Elfland.
   * **Why B:** A Petrarchan sonnet's octave-sestet shape describes "33", not this ballad.
   * **Why C:** The ballad is patterned quatrains, not a single unrhymed free-verse stanza — that is Corrie Etchachan.
   * **Why D:** A rhymed song addressed to a lover describes Composed in August.

3. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What does the condition placed on Thomas — that he must stay silent in Elfland or never return — suggest about the fairy world?
   * **Options:** A) That it is a harmless holiday from ordinary life, B) That crossing into Elfland demands total obedience, and its gift of prophecy is bought at the cost of the traveller's own voice, C) That Thomas is free to speak as he pleases, D) That the Queen has no power over him
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The silence-or-exile rule reveals Elfland's power over Thomas as absolute — his gift of true speech on return is bought by surrendering his voice while he is there.
   * **Why A:** The strict rule and the seven-year binding show this is no casual holiday.
   * **Why C:** The ballad insists on his silence; breaking it would trap him in Elfland forever.
   * **Why D:** The Queen's commands entirely govern Thomas's time in Elfland.

<!-- ============================ POEM 2: COMPOSED IN AUGUST ============================ -->

4. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem sets autumn's natural harmony and a young man's love against the cruelty of the shooting season — opening "Now westlin winds, and slaught'ring guns / Bring autumn's pleasant weather," then turning from the birds to his "charmer"?
   * **Options:** A) Composed in August, B) 33, C) The Bonnie Broukit Bairn, D) Summit of Corrie Etchachan
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Burns's "Composed in August" opens with the westlin winds and "slaught'ring guns" of the shooting season before turning to the birds and the speaker's love for his "charmer".
   * **Why B:** 33 is a dream-vision sonnet about a funeral barque, not a harvest-season love song.
   * **Why C:** The Bonnie Broukit Bairn addresses the Earth among the planets, not autumn birds.
   * **Why D:** Summit of Corrie Etchachan describes a mountain summit, not a harvest landscape.

5. **Type: Fill [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** In "Composed in August", Burns's regular, rhymed song-stanzas move from birds and hunting to a closing address to his sweetheart, named [BLANK].
   * **Answer:** Peggy
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The song's final stanzas turn from nature to romance, addressing "Peggy dear" directly — fittingly, since Burns wrote the piece to be sung.
   * **WhyWrong:** The name is "Peggy" — Burns closes the song by addressing his sweetheart directly, turning nature description into courtship.

6. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What contrast does Burns draw when he condemns "Tyrannic man's dominion" and "the murd'ring cry" of the "sportsman's joy"?
   * **Options:** A) Between the natural harmony of the birds finding "their pleasure" and human violence that intrudes on it, B) Between two rival hunters, C) Between summer and winter weather, D) Between Peggy and another woman
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Burns sets the birds' natural, peaceable "pleasure" against the cruelty of the shooting season, briefly turning a season-and-courtship song into a rebuke of needless human violence.
   * **Why B:** The poem contrasts humanity with nature, not two hunters against each other.
   * **Why C:** The poem's season is fixed as autumn; there is no summer/winter contrast.
   * **Why D:** Peggy is the sole object of the speaker's affection; no rival appears.

<!-- ============================ POEM 3: THE BONNIE BROUKIT BAIRN ============================ -->

7. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem argues that the vain, self-absorbed heavens ignore a neglected Earth — imagining Mars "in crammasy" and Venus "in a green silk goun" gossiping among the stars, sparing not a thought for "thou bonnie broukit bairn"?
   * **Options:** A) The Bonnie Broukit Bairn, B) Da Clearance, C) Thomas the Rhymer, D) Composed in August
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. MacDiarmid's "The Bonnie Broukit Bairn" pictures Mars and Venus finely dressed and self-absorbed, ignoring the neglected Earth — the "bonnie broukit bairn" the poem addresses directly.
   * **Why B:** Da Clearance recounts a Shetland eviction, not the planets.
   * **Why C:** Thomas the Rhymer is a ballad about Elfland, with no planetary imagery.
   * **Why D:** Composed in August is set among autumn birds and a courting couple, not the night sky.

8. **Type: True-False [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** "The Bonnie Broukit Bairn" is an eight-line poem written in Scots that turns, after a dash, into a final, urgent couplet warning of Earth's tears.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. MacDiarmid compresses the whole poem into eight lines of Scots, with a dash marking a sharp turn into the final couplet's warning that the Earth's tears could drown "the haill clanjamfrie".
   * **WhyWrong:** This is true — the poem's dash marks exactly this turn, from the planets' vain chatter to Earth's own, more powerful, potential grief.

9. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What does the poem suggest by ending on the image of Earth's tears that could drown "the haill clanjamfrie" (the whole crowd of them)?
   * **Options:** A) That Earth is powerless and insignificant next to Mars and Venus, B) That the neglected Earth's grief holds a hidden power that could overwhelm the very bodies that ignore it, C) That the planets will comfort Earth if it cries, D) That crying is a sign of weakness
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The closing image reverses the poem's opening imbalance: the "broukit" (neglected) Earth's tears carry force enough to drown the vain, chattering "clanjamfrie" of Mars, Venus and the moon.
   * **Why A:** The final image gives Earth unexpected power, not confirmed insignificance.
   * **Why C:** The other bodies spare Earth not a single thought — "Nane for thee a thochtie sparin'" — indifferent, not comforting.
   * **Why D:** The tears are presented as a potential force, not a weakness.

<!-- ============================ POEM 4: SUMMIT OF CORRIE ETCHACHAN ============================ -->

10. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem describes reaching a mountain's summit to find "no peak, / No vision of the blue world," only a "grey plateau, rock-strewn, vast, silent"?
    * **Options:** A) Summit of Corrie Etchachan, B) The Bonnie Broukit Bairn, C) 33, D) Composed in August
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Nan Shepherd's "Summit of Corrie Etchachan" describes arriving at the corrie's summit to find not a clear vision but a silent, "rock-strewn" plateau and a dark loch.
    * **Why B:** The Bonnie Broukit Bairn addresses Earth among the planets, not a mountain summit.
    * **Why C:** 33 is a dream of a funeral barque, not a Cairngorms climb.
    * **Why D:** Composed in August is set among autumn birds and courtship, not a mountain ascent.

11. **Type: Fill [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** "Summit of Corrie Etchachan" is written in [BLANK] verse, with no regular rhyme or metre, its flowing lines tracing the mind's own "climbing ecstasy of thought".
    * **Answer:** free
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Shepherd's free verse has no fixed rhyme or metre; its long, unbroken lines mirror the continuous ascent from physical climb to reflection.
    * **WhyWrong:** The word is "free" — the poem's unrhymed, unmetred lines let thought move as continuously as the climb itself.

12. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What does Shepherd suggest by comparing the summit's "grey plateau" to how "the mind" achieves understanding?
    * **Options:** A) That real understanding, like the summit, offers no glorious final "vision of the infinite", but instead a confrontation with "its own terror, its own glory and power", B) That climbing mountains is a waste of effort, C) That the mind can only ever reach clear, untroubled conclusions, D) That the summit disappoints because it lacks any meaning at all
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Shepherd extends the anticlimax of the summit into a metaphor for thought itself: true achievement is not a triumphant "vision of the infinite" but an encounter with the mind's own daunting depth.
    * **Why B:** The poem values the "climbing ecstasy of thought" — effort is not dismissed as wasted.
    * **Why C:** The mind's discoveries are shown as "dark and inscrutable", not simply clear.
    * **Why D:** The plateau is charged with "immensity" and "power" — it is meaningful, not empty of meaning.

<!-- ============================ POEM 5: DA CLEARANCE ============================ -->

13. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem, written in Shetland dialect, recalls a peaceful crofting community before describing "fok aa hirded oot afore da laandloard's men"?
    * **Options:** A) Da Clearance, B) Thomas the Rhymer, C) 33, D) The Bonnie Broukit Bairn
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Rhoda Bulter's "Da Clearance" remembers a thriving Shetland community — thatched roofs, hens, children playing — before its people are "hirded oot" by the landlord's men.
    * **Why B:** Thomas the Rhymer is set in a ballad world of Elfland, not a historical eviction.
    * **Why C:** 33 is a dream-sonnet on Mary Queen of Scots' execution, not a Clearance.
    * **Why D:** The Bonnie Broukit Bairn addresses Earth among the planets, not a crofting community.

14. **Type: True-False [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** "Da Clearance" moves through four rhymed quatrains in strict chronological order — peaceful community, then eviction, then bare aftermath — building to a closing rhetorical question.
    * **Answer:** True
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Bulter's four quatrains move steadily from community life, through the "lood, lood knockin" of eviction, to the "bare waas staandin", ending on the sharp question about "twartree extry sheep".
    * **WhyWrong:** This is true — the poem's four stanzas follow exactly this before-during-after sequence, closing on that bitter question.

15. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What is the effect of the poem's closing question, "Tell me, wis it wirt it aa for twartree extry sheep?"
    * **Options:** A) It celebrates the profitable use of the cleared land, B) It exposes the enormity of human suffering caused by the Clearances as disproportionate to the small economic gain of "a few extra sheep", C) It suggests the sheep were more important than the people, D) It has no real force in the poem
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The direct, plain-spoken question lands with bitter force, measuring a community's uprooting against the pettiness of "twartree extry sheep" — exposing how little the human cost weighed against profit.
    * **Why A:** The question is accusatory, not celebratory, of the land's new use.
    * **Why C:** The comparison condemns valuing sheep over people; it does not endorse it.
    * **Why D:** The direct address to the reader gives the closing line real, pointed force.

<!-- ============================ POEM 6: 33 ============================ -->

16. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem describes a dream of a "sawdust chandelier" made from shipwreck driftwood, aboard a "great smouldering barque" that carries the dead?
    * **Options:** A) 33, B) Composed in August, C) Summit of Corrie Etchachan, D) Da Clearance
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. MacGillivray's "33" is a dream-vision of a funeral barque with its driftwood "chandelier", its figurehead singing "the death song of queens, of kings".
    * **Why B:** Composed in August is set among real autumn birds and a courting couple, not a dream barque.
    * **Why C:** Summit of Corrie Etchachan is a mountain-climbing reflection, not a dream of a ship.
    * **Why D:** Da Clearance recounts a historical Shetland eviction, not a dream vision.

17. **Type: Select All [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** Which statements correctly describe the form of "33"?
    * **Options:** A) It is a Petrarchan sonnet, built from an octave and a sestet, B) It is one of a sequence of poems written to commemorate Mary Queen of Scots, one for each of the 35 steps she descended to her execution, C) It uses free verse with no set structure at all, D) Its bare numeral title positions it as one part of that larger numbered sequence, not a standalone poem
    * **Correct:** A, B, D
    * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A,B,D. 1 mark if mostly correct.
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "33" is a Petrarchan sonnet from a sequence marking Mary Queen of Scots' 35 steps to execution; its bare numeral title signals it is one part of that larger sequence.
    * **Why C:** The poem keeps the Petrarchan sonnet's fixed octave-and-sestet shape; it is not free verse.

18. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What paradox does the poem capture in the image "you are moth and flame both... performs two killings"?
    * **Options:** A) That the barque is both drawn towards its own destruction and the source of that destruction, mirroring the deadly pull between light and death, B) That moths and flames have no connection to the poem's meaning, C) That the speaker literally owns a pet moth, D) That the barque is entirely safe from danger
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Casting the barque as "moth and flame both" fuses the thing drawn to destruction with the thing that destroys it — the funeral light is both fatally alluring and the cause of its own end, doubling the poem's sense of death.
    * **Why B:** The moth-and-flame image is central to the poem's meditation on dread and mortality.
    * **Why C:** The image is figurative — there is no literal pet moth in the poem.
    * **Why D:** The paradox depends on the barque itself courting its own destruction, not on safety.
