### Protagonist
- **Note:** Ralph is the everyman protagonist — an ordinary boy tested past his limits; he begins as the confident, elected chief who blows the conch and trusts that rules and rescue are natural, but through witnessing the island's savagery, and sharing in the frenzied dance that kills Simon, he becomes a hunted, weeping boy who has learned "the darkness of man's heart", so his evolution is a self-implicating loss of innocence, not mere misfortune.

### Plot
- **Note:** Stranded after a plane crash, the boys elect Ralph and build a fragile order (conch, signal fire, shelters), but fear of an imagined "beast" spreads and Jack exploits it, breaking away to lead a face-painted tribe of hunters whose loosened savagery escalates until the boys murder Simon in a frenzy, Roger kills Piggy and smashes the conch, and Jack hunts Ralph across the island — the descent halted only by the sudden arrival of a naval officer, each disaster caused by the last.

### Themes
- **Note:** The controlling ideas are the conflict between civilisation and savagery and the innate darkness of human nature (the "beast" is within, not without), the fragility of order and reason embodied in the conch and Piggy's glasses whose destruction marks civilisation's collapse, the loss of innocence, and how fear is exploited to seize power.

### Effects
- **Note:** Lord of the Flies is a dystopian, cautionary fable, and its emotional effect is fear-as-warning, dread and unease: the horror comes not from an external monster but from within ordinary, civilised children, so we feel a creeping dread that the same savagery lies in everyone, and a disquiet at our own complicity — the beast is us. Even the rescue brings no relief, since the adult world is fighting its own savage war, leaving us warned rather than comforted.

### Message
- **Note:** The novel warns that beneath the thin veneer of civilisation savagery lies within every human being — remove the rules and ordinary people descend into cruelty, for the true "beast" is our own capacity for evil, not an outside creature — and that civilisation is a fragile structure whose collapse, as the war overhead confirms, unleashes a barbarism that is universal, not rare or foreign.
