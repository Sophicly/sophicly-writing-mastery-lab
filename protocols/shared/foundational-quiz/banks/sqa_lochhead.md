# Foundational Quiz Bank — SQA Scottish Text: Liz Lochhead Poetry (Poems)

Deterministic, code-scored foundational recall bank (parsed by `SWML_Quiz_Bank`).

understanding dimensions: Recognising the Poem · Form & Features · Meaning & Effects. Unlike
the universal forms bank (`poetic_forms.md`, Tier A), these are poem-specific, testing what the
student has actually read. The picker draws a random 5 per round, stratified across categories.

never the scorekeeper.

text unit, so there is no set-staging token here (contrast the multi-set AQA Power and Conflict
bank, where poems are read across the course in stages).

### Quiz: Liz Lochhead Poetry

1. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** In which poem does the speaker visit a home "peopled with many surfaces" — polished tables "clear enough to see distortions in" — where "my rival serves us tea" with silver tongs?
   * **Options:** A) My Rival's House, B) Last Supper, C) Box Room, D) Sorting Through
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Lochhead's "My Rival's House" pictures a mother's over-polished house of "many surfaces", where the speaker and her partner "take our shoes off at her door" as the rival "serves us tea" with silver tongs.
   * **Why B:** Last Supper is set at the speaker's own table, preparing a final meal to end a relationship, not visiting a rival's polished house.
   * **Why C:** Box Room is set in a partner's childhood bedroom, not a formal tea-service sitting room.
   * **Why D:** Sorting Through concerns clearing a deceased mother's wardrobe, not a rival's house.

2. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** How does Lochhead structure "My Rival's House", and what tone does it create?
   * **Options:** A) A single fixed sonnet form throughout, B) Six stanzas of irregular length with shifting, often slant rhyme, moving from resentful observation to bitter acceptance, C) A regular ballad quatrain with a repeated refrain, D) Strict rhyming couplets throughout
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The poem's six stanzas vary from four to eleven lines, its rhymes sliding in and out of true chime, tracking the speaker's tone as it shifts from wary observation to resigned, bitter acceptance.
   * **Why A:** The poem is not a fourteen-line sonnet; its stanzas vary considerably in length.
   * **Why C:** There is no song-like refrain; each stanza develops the scene differently.
   * **Why D:** The rhyme is irregular and slant, not a set of strict, matching couplets.

3. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What does the closing line, "She has taken even this from me," reveal about the speaker in "My Rival's House"?
   * **Options:** A) That she has won the rivalry decisively, B) That the mother's influence over her son reaches so far that even the speaker's own feelings and identity feel claimed by her, C) That she no longer loves her partner, D) That the two women have become close friends
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Describing herself as "all edges, a surface, a shell", the speaker admits the rival's control extends even into her own sense of self — the mother has claimed not just the son, but the speaker's ability to feel secure in the relationship.
   * **Why A:** The line registers defeat and loss, not victory.
   * **Why C:** The poem is about rivalry for the partner's loyalty, not a loss of love for him.
   * **Why D:** The two women remain rivals; the tea-service is a hostile ritual, not friendship.

4. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** In which poem does a woman prepare an elaborate final meal for her partner, already imagining how she will retell the story, "cackling around the cauldron", with her friends?
   * **Options:** A) Last Supper, B) My Rival's House, C) For My Grandmother Knitting, D) The Spaces Between
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. In "Last Supper", the speaker prepares a lavish farewell feast, "getting good and ready to renounce / his sweet flesh", already picturing herself retelling the story to her friends, "cackling around the cauldron".
   * **Why B:** My Rival's House is set at a mother's tea table, not a farewell feast the speaker herself prepares.
   * **Why C:** For My Grandmother Knitting portrays an elderly woman's hands at work, not a staged final meal.
   * **Why D:** The Spaces Between contrasts cave handprints with a grandson's birthday, not a breakup dinner.

5. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** How does the form of "Last Supper" suit its subject?
   * **Options:** A) A tightly rhymed sonnet builds toward a calm resolution, B) Free verse with conversational, fragmented syntax and parenthetical asides mimics the speaker's shifting thoughts as she moves from present preparation to imagined future, C) A regular ballad refrain celebrates the relationship, D) Strict blank verse gives the scene formal detachment
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The loose, conversational free verse — broken by parenthetical asides — lets the poem slide naturally between the present ritual of cooking and the speaker's imagined future, retelling the story among friends.
   * **Why A:** There is no fixed sonnet form or calm resolution; the syntax is deliberately loose and fragmented.
   * **Why C:** The poem is not song-like, and it ends a relationship rather than celebrating one.
   * **Why D:** The voice is intimate and shifting, not formally detached blank verse.

6. **Type: True-False [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** In "Last Supper", preparing the final meal and imagining retelling the story to friends afterwards represents the speaker reclaiming control and agency over the end of her relationship.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. By staging the last meal herself and picturing herself "gorged on truth / and their own savage integrity" among her friends, the speaker turns a painful ending into an act she authors and controls.
   * **WhyWrong:** This is the poem's central movement — the speaker moves from passive hurt towards active authorship of the story's ending, sharing it on her own terms.

7. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**

   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Box Room" places the speaker in her partner's old bedroom at his mother's house, surrounded by boyhood relics including a preserved egg collection, while being reminded that the room "was always his".
   * **Why B:** Last Supper is set at the speaker's own table preparing a farewell meal, not a childhood bedroom.
   * **Why C:** My Rival's House takes place in the mother's formal sitting room over tea, not an overnight stay in a bedroom.
   * **Why D:** Sorting Through concerns clearing a mother's own wardrobe after her death, not visiting a partner's family home.

9. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What does the preserved boyhood bedroom, complete with its egg collection, suggest about the speaker's position in "Box Room"?
   * **Options:** A) That she is warmly welcomed as a permanent part of the family, B) That she is an outsider staying in a space still shaped by the son's past, her own place in his life feeling temporary and uncertain, C) That she owns the room jointly with her partner, D) That the mother has forgotten her son entirely
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Surrounded by relics of the son's boyhood that his mother has carefully kept, the speaker feels like a visitor in a space that still belongs to his past — her own claim on him feels displaced and provisional.

   * **Why C:** The room is presented as the son's alone, not shared with the speaker.
   * **Why D:** The meticulously kept egg collection is evidence the mother has preserved every detail of the son's boyhood, not forgotten it.

11. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** How is "For My Grandmother Knitting" structured, and how does it address its subject?
    * **Options:** A) A strict rhyming sonnet spoken by the grandmother herself, B) Six stanzas of free verse with no punctuation, addressing the grandmother directly as "you" and moving chronologically through her life from fisher-girl to old age, C) A ballad with a repeated musical refrain, D) Rhyming couplets throughout, spoken in the third person
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Lochhead uses six unpunctuated free-verse stanzas, speaking directly to the grandmother ("your hands") and tracing her life back to when she was "the fisher-girl", building a portrait of lifelong labour.
    * **Why A:** The poem is not a fourteen-line sonnet, and the grandmother is the addressee, not the speaker.
    * **Why C:** There is no song-like refrain; the structure develops as continuous reflection.
    * **Why D:** The poem lacks regular rhyme, and speaks to the grandmother directly rather than about her in the third person only.

12. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What does the poem suggest about the grandmother through the image of her hands still working, "as if your hands had forgotten / how to stop"?
    * **Options:** A) That she is confused and no longer capable of rational thought, B) That a lifetime of necessary labour has become so ingrained in her identity that it continues out of habit and dignity, long after the economic need has passed, C) That she secretly wants to stop knitting but is forced to continue, D) That the family values her knitting greatly
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The hands that move from habit rather than necessity show how deeply a lifetime of purposeful work has shaped the grandmother's identity — knitting sustains her sense of dignity and usefulness even after "there is no need".
    * **Why A:** The image conveys ingrained skill and habit, not confusion.
    * **Why C:** There is no suggestion of unwillingness; the movement comes from within her, not external force.
    * **Why D:** The family's "there is no need" confirms they dismiss the knitting as unnecessary, not that they value it.

15. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What central question does "The Spaces Between" raise through its image of "blown candles" and "blown kisses"?
    * **Options:** A) Whether the grandson will remember his birthday party, B) Whether love itself can leave any lasting mark on the world, in the way the ancient hand stencils have endured, C) Whether lockdown restrictions were necessary, D) Whether the cave paintings are genuinely prehistoric
    * **Correct:** B

    * **Why D:** The poem takes the ancient handprints as a given point of comparison, not a subject of doubt.

17. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** How is "Sorting Through" structured, and what effect does this create?
    * **Options:** A) Four short, tightly rhymed stanzas separate distinct memories, B) A single continuous block of free verse, unbroken by stanza divisions, mirroring the unbroken flow of the speaker's grief and memory, C) A dialogue between mother and daughter in alternating lines, D) A strict villanelle with a repeated refrain
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Lochhead lets the twenty-two lines run on as one continuous, unrhymed block, so the poem's grief and memory flow without pause, echoing the messy, non-stop nature of loss.
    * **Why A:** The poem is not divided into separate rhymed stanzas; it runs as one continuous piece.
    * **Why C:** There is a single speaking voice throughout — the mother does not speak.
    * **Why D:** There is no repeated refrain or the fixed pattern of a villanelle.
