# Foundational Quiz Bank — SQA Higher Imtiaz Dharker Poetry (Poems)

Deterministic, code-scored foundational recall bank (parsed by `SWML_Quiz_Bank`).
18 questions on the actual SQA Scottish Set Text poems (Tier B) — three per poem across Neil's
three understanding dimensions: Recognising the Poem · Form & Features · Meaning & Effects.
Unlike the universal forms bank (`poetic_forms.md`, Tier A), these are poem-specific, testing
what the student has actually read. The picker draws a random 5 per round, stratified across
categories. Keys + feedback live server-side and are stripped before questions reach the client.
The AI is never the scorekeeper.

Categories: Recognising the Poem · Form & Features · Meaning & Effects
Types: MCQ · Fill · True-False · Select All

This is a single-stage bank (SQA Higher, one poet, one set of six poems) — no `@set` staging
tokens are used; every question is live from the first round.

Poems covered (all from Imtiaz Dharker's *Luck Is the Hook*, Bloodaxe Books, 2018):
Bairn · Bloom · Letters to Glasgow · Send This · Stitch · The Knot

### Quiz: SQA Higher Imtiaz Dharker Poetry

<!-- ============================ BAIRN ============================ -->

1. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem describes light rising into a room from "the early morning bloom of snow", greeting the birth of a child ("bairn")?
   * **Options:** A) Bloom, B) Bairn, C) Stitch, D) The Knot
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Dharker's "Bairn" (Scots for baby) opens on light filling a room from "the early morning bloom of snow" as a newborn's first cry renews a tired world.
   * **Why A:** Bloom is addressed directly to an infant already held in the speaker's arms, not to a room filling with snow-light.
   * **Why C:** Stitch follows a mother knitting a cardigan, not a birth scene.
   * **Why D:** The Knot recalls a family picnic at Loch Lomond, not a birth.

2. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** What repeated phrase opens both main stanzas of "Bairn", underlining a wonder the speaker cannot put into words?
   * **Options:** A) "There are things that cleanse", B) "There is no way to explain", C) "Everything changes", D) "Do not send me"
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Both main stanzas of "Bairn" open with the anaphora "There is no way to explain", stressing that the child's effect on the world exceeds language.
   * **Why A:** That phrase is the poem's single closing line, not its repeated opening.
   * **Why C:** "Everything changes" opens a stanza in "Send This", not "Bairn".
   * **Why D:** "Do not send me" is the refusal refrain of "Send This".

3. **Type: True-False [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** "Bairn" suggests that a new baby can renew hope in a world that feels tired or "too old", even though the effect cannot fully be put into words.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The poem insists a "too spent, too old" world "can still be new, renewed" by the baby's arrival, framing birth as a source of hope beyond explanation.
   * **WhyWrong:** This is the poem's claim — despite the "no way to explain" refrain, the child's "first sound" renews a world that felt worn out.

<!-- ============================ BLOOM ============================ -->

4. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem addresses an infant directly, insisting "You are nothing more than yourself, / not a message sent to change the world"?
   * **Options:** A) Bairn, B) Bloom, C) Letters to Glasgow, D) Send This
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Bloom" speaks directly to a child held in the speaker's arms, insisting the infant exists simply as itself rather than as a symbol.
   * **Why A:** Bairn describes light filling a room at a birth, not a direct address insisting on the child's plain existence.
   * **Why C:** Letters to Glasgow follows train passengers arriving in the city, not a parent addressing an infant.
   * **Why D:** Send This refuses sentimental postcards of a lost home, not an address to a child.

5. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** "Bloom" builds its central comparisons through...
   * **Options:** A) A chain of similes drawn from nature — snail, leaf, sea — building to a closing couplet, B) A strict rhymed sonnet, C) Non-standard phonetic spelling, D) A refrain repeated in every stanza
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Dharker layers similes — "like a snail or mollusc", "like a leaf among thousands" — across free-verse tercets before closing on a two-line couplet.
   * **Why B:** The poem is unrhymed free verse, not a fourteen-line sonnet.
   * **Why C:** Non-standard phonetic spelling is not a feature of this poem.
   * **Why D:** There is no repeated refrain; the similes vary and accumulate instead.

6. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What tension does "Bloom" explore between the child and the speaker?
   * **Options:** A) The child is a burden the speaker resents, B) The child is simply and purely itself, yet this ordinary presence expands the speaker's whole sense of the world, C) The child is a symbol of a political cause, D) The speaker feels no connection to the child
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The poem insists the child is "nothing more than yourself", yet holding this ordinary, unsymbolic life makes the speaker feel she holds "the world".
   * **Why A:** The tone is tender and wondering, not resentful.
   * **Why C:** The poem explicitly rejects the child as "a message sent to change the world".
   * **Why D:** The speaker's close, attentive watching expresses deep connection, not detachment.

<!-- ==================== LETTERS TO GLASGOW ==================== -->

7. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which poem follows a train journey north, tracking a passenger's memories as "the hum of the train becomes the voice / of her mother", before arriving "over the Clyde where the great ships were born"?
   * **Options:** A) Letters to Glasgow, B) The Knot, C) Stitch, D) Send This
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Letters to Glasgow" follows the train's journey north, weaving a passenger's memories of her mother and lover into the rhythm of the wheels before it arrives "over the Clyde where the great ships were born".
   * **Why B:** The Knot recalls a family picnic at Loch Lomond, not a train journey.
   * **Why C:** Stitch follows a mother knitting a cardigan on Argyle Street, not a train arriving in the city.
   * **Why D:** Send This refuses postcards of a distant, changed home, not a train journey to Glasgow.

8. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** How does "Letters to Glasgow" bring other voices into its structure?
   * **Options:** A) It keeps a single unbroken first-person voice throughout, B) It breaks into italicised fragments of overheard speech and a repeated announcement, layered inside its three-line stanzas, C) It is written entirely as a formal letter, D) It uses only rhyming couplets
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Overheard, italicised fragments — passengers showing old photographs, and the repeated announcement "We are now approaching, we are now approaching" — interrupt the tercets, layering several journeys together.
   * **Why A:** Multiple voices and perspectives interrupt the poem, not a single unbroken voice.
   * **Why C:** The poem is verse in stanzas, not a literal letter, despite its title.
   * **Why D:** The stanzas are three-line and loosely rhymed, not rhyming couplets throughout.

9. **Type: True-False [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** "Letters to Glasgow" presents the train itself as a kind of "memory-keeper", carrying passengers' pasts alongside their physical journey into the city.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The poem names the train directly as "a memory-keeper", holding onto love and loss "like the words in a letter, / not gathering dust but held in trust" as it carries passengers toward Glasgow.
   * **WhyWrong:** This is the poem's own claim — the train is called "a memory-keeper", carrying the past alongside the present journey.

<!-- ============================ SEND THIS ============================ -->

10. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem refuses postcards and miniatures of a home city, insisting "nothing is ever fixed" and vowing to carry "the unfinished walls of my city / with me, in my pocket"?
    * **Options:** A) Send This, B) Bairn, C) Letters to Glasgow, D) The Knot
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Send This" rejects nostalgic images of a lost home, insisting the city is unfinished and always changing, and vowing to carry its "unfinished walls" instead.
    * **Why B:** Bairn concerns a newborn's first light and sound, not a distant remembered city.
    * **Why C:** Letters to Glasgow follows a train journey into Glasgow, not a refusal of postcards of a lost home.
    * **Why D:** The Knot recalls a family picnic, not correspondence about a home city.

11. **Type: Fill [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** "Send This" opens two of its stanzas with the repeated command "[BLANK] send me a postcard / of the city that once lived here", refusing sentimental images of home.
    * **Answer:** Do not
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The imperative "Do not" opens the poem's refrains, rejecting postcards and miniatures as false, static pictures of a home that has moved on.
    * **WhyWrong:** The phrase is "Do not" — repeated to refuse sentimental images of a home city that no longer exists as pictured.

12. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What does the speaker in "Send This" ask for instead of comforting, nostalgic images?
    * **Options:** A) A more detailed photograph of the old city, B) The honest, ongoing truth that the city is unfinished, still being built and rebuilt, imperfect and unfixed, C) A promise that nothing there will ever change, D) A map showing exactly where the old buildings stood
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The speaker rejects false, finished pictures of home, asking instead to be told of "girders laid / over half-drawn plans" — the honest, unfinished truth that "nothing is ever fixed".
    * **Why A:** The speaker rejects photographs and postcards altogether, not a better one.
    * **Why C:** The poem insists "Everything changes" — the opposite of permanence.
    * **Why D:** The interest is in present, ongoing change, not a record of what used to stand there.

<!-- ============================ STITCH ============================ -->

13. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem follows a mother who knits her daughter a red cardigan, "a perfect copy / of Cathy's, but red", and says at the end her "liver / has been torn apart"?
    * **Options:** A) Stitch, B) Bloom, C) Send This, D) The Knot
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Stitch" follows the speaker's mother knitting a cardigan after a trip "over the bridge / to Argyle Street for wool and needles", and grieving with the same bodily image when her daughter eventually leaves.
    * **Why B:** Bloom addresses an infant held in the speaker's arms, not a mother knitting.
    * **Why C:** Send This concerns a lost home city, not a knitted cardigan.
    * **Why D:** The Knot recalls a Loch Lomond picnic, not knitting.

14. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** What extended metaphor structures "Stitch"?
    * **Options:** A) Paper standing for fragile power, B) Knitting standing for the mother's love and the shaping of her daughter's identity, C) A storm standing for family conflict, D) A map standing for a lost homeland
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The mother's knitting — "needles clicking, clacking / in time to the pattern in her head" — becomes a metaphor for shaping and "knitting herself into" her daughter's identity.
    * **Why A:** The paper metaphor belongs to Dharker's separate poem "Tissue", not "Stitch".
    * **Why C:** There is no storm imagery in this poem; the setting is domestic and local (Argyle Street).
    * **Why D:** There is no map metaphor here; the geography named is a specific Glasgow street, not a remembered homeland.

15. **Type: Select All [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** Which statements correctly describe the meaning and effect of "Stitch"?
    * **Options:** A) The line "I am a piece of her liver" expresses an intense, bodily bond between mother and daughter, B) The contrast between Cathy's blue, machine-made cardigan and the daughter's red, hand-made one marks a quiet difference within a shared childhood world, C) The closing image of the mother's "liver... torn apart" echoes the opening bond in the pain of separation, D) The poem concludes that the mother felt nothing when her daughter eventually left home
    * **Correct:** A, B, C
    * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A,B,C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Dharker's bodily image of the "liver" opens and closes the poem, framing an intimate maternal bond; the blue/red, machine-made/hand-made contrast marks quiet difference within closeness, and the final "torn apart" echoes that bond in grief.
    * **Why D:** The final lines convey the mother in obvious pain, not indifference, when her daughter leaves.

<!-- ============================ THE KNOT ============================ -->

16. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
    * **Question:** Which poem recalls a family picnic at Loch Lomond where the parents are remembered as "king and queen", and the mother unties a cloth "to set the parathas free"?
    * **Options:** A) The Knot, B) Letters to Glasgow, C) Bairn, D) Send This
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "The Knot" remembers a childhood picnic at Loch Lomond, where "Her Majesty untied the cloth / to set the parathas free", an image that later becomes a metaphor for unresolved family hurt.
    * **Why B:** Letters to Glasgow describes a train arriving in the city, not a loch-side picnic.
    * **Why C:** Bairn describes light and sound at a birth, not a family outing.
    * **Why D:** Send This addresses a lost home city, not a Loch Lomond picnic.

17. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
    * **Question:** What form does Dharker use for "The Knot", and how does its rhyme behave?
    * **Options:** A) A fourteen-line sonnet, moving through three quatrains to a closing rhymed couplet, mixing full and slant rhyme, B) A ballad with a sung refrain, C) Entirely unrhymed free verse, D) A villanelle with two repeating refrain lines
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "The Knot" is a fourteen-line sonnet: three quatrains give way to a final rhymed couplet ("the mistake... / ...did not take"), with slant rhymes such as "mouth / cloth" alongside fuller ones such as "heart / apart".
    * **Why B:** There is no sung, repeating refrain; the poem is a sonnet, not a ballad.
    * **Why C:** The poem does rhyme, if loosely, rather than running entirely unrhymed.
    * **Why D:** A villanelle needs two alternating refrains repeated throughout; this fourteen-line sonnet has neither.

18. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
    * **Question:** What does the untying of the cloth around the parathas come to represent by the end of "The Knot"?
    * **Options:** A) A simple, happy memory with no further meaning, B) An extended metaphor for trying to undo a "knot" of family hurt, silence and the mother's decline, C) A protest against unhealthy food, D) A criticism of the mother's cooking
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The tender image of untying the parathas' cloth becomes an extended metaphor for the harder task of undoing "this deep red knot of hurt in the heart" as the mother's health and the family's silence unravel.
    * **Why A:** The image deepens into a metaphor for grief and unresolved hurt, not a closed, simple memory.
    * **Why C:** The poem is not concerned with food itself, but with what the untying comes to symbolise.
    * **Why D:** There is no criticism of the mother's cooking; the tone toward her is tender and grieving.
