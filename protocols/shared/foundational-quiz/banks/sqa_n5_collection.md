# Foundational Quiz Bank — SQA National 5 Scottish Poetry Collection (Poems)

Deterministic, code-scored foundational recall bank (parsed by `SWML_Quiz_Bank`).
18 questions on the actual COLLECTION POEMS (Tier B) — three per poem across Neil's three
understanding dimensions: Recognising the Poem · Form & Features · Meaning & Effects. Unlike
the universal forms bank (`poetic_forms.md`, Tier A), these are poem-specific, testing what the
student has actually read. The picker draws a random 5 per round, stratified across categories.
Keys + feedback live server-side and are stripped before questions reach the client. The AI is
never the scorekeeper.

Categories: Recognising the Poem · Form & Features · Meaning & Effects
Types: MCQ · Fill · True-False · Select All

This is a single-stage National 5 collection (six poems studied together, no staged reading
order) — questions carry no `@set` token.

Poems: The Bonnie Earl o' Moray (traditional Scots ballad) · The Twa Corbies (traditional Scots
ballad) · A Red, Red Rose (Robert Burns) · Lochinvar (Sir Walter Scott) · Auntie (Nadine Aisha
Jassat) · Little Girls (Len Pennie).

### Quiz: SQA National 5 Scottish Poetry Collection

1. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**
   * **Question:** Which ballad laments a murdered nobleman, asking "Ye Hielands and ye Lowlands, O, whaur hae ye been?" before revealing "They hae slain the Earl o' Moray"?
   * **Options:** A) The Twa Corbies, B) The Bonnie Earl o' Moray, C) Lochinvar, D) A Red, Red Rose
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "The Bonnie Earl o' Moray" opens with the haunting question "Ye Hielands and ye Lowlands, O, whaur hae ye been?" before lamenting that the Earl was slain and "laid him on the green".
   * **Why A:** The Twa Corbies describes two crows discussing a dead knight, not a lament asking where the Highlands and Lowlands have been.
   * **Why C:** Lochinvar is a galloping rescue ballad, not a lament for a murdered Earl.
   * **Why D:** A Red, Red Rose is a love song by Burns, not a ballad of murder and blame.

2. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** What ballad feature does "The Bonnie Earl o' Moray" use to frame its lament?
   * **Options:** A) A haunting opening question — "Ye Hielands and ye Lowlands..." — that frames the narrative like a refrain, B) An unbroken sonnet form, C) A single continuous stanza with no repetition, D) A strict villanelle refrain
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The ballad's opening question about the Highlands and Lowlands frames the lament, giving it a haunting, song-like shape typical of the traditional ballad.
   * **Why B:** The ballad is built from short narrative quatrains, not a fourteen-line sonnet.
   * **Why C:** The poem's opening question gives it a returning, refrain-like shape, so it is not without repetition.
   * **Why D:** A villanelle demands two alternating refrains across nineteen lines; this short ballad does not follow that pattern.

   * **WhyWrong:** This is true — the ballad names Huntly directly and states he was ordered only to bring the Earl, not to kill him.

4. **Type: MCQ [Tests Recognising the Poem]**

6. **Type: MCQ [Tests Meaning & Effects]**
   * **Question:** What makes the tone of "The Twa Corbies" so bleak compared with gentler versions of the same story?
   * **Options:** A) The knight is given a heroic funeral, B) His own hound, hawk and lady have already abandoned him, so nothing but the indifferent crows and wind remain, C) The crows decide to spare him, D) The poem ends with his lady's grief
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Unlike sentimental variants where loyal animals mourn the fallen knight, here his hound has gone hunting, his hawk fowling, and his lady has "ta'en another mate" — leaving only the crows and the wind that "sall blaw for evermair".
   * **Why A:** There is no funeral; his bones are left bare beneath the "auld fail dyke".
   * **Why C:** The crows plan to eat him, not to spare him.
   * **Why D:** His lady has already moved on to "another mate", not grieved him.

   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Burns's "A Red, Red Rose" opens with the famous simile comparing his love to a rose "newly sprung in June" and a melody "sweetly play'd in tune".

8. **Type: MCQ [Tests Form & Features]**
   * **Question:** How does Burns build his declarations of eternal love in "A Red, Red Rose"?
   * **Options:** A) Through a single extended metaphor with no other imagery, B) Through simile and hyperbole across simple rhyming quatrains, comparing his love to a rose and a melody, then vowing to love "till a' the seas gang dry", C) Through a strict, unrhymed sonnet, D) Through non-standard phonetic spelling
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Burns pairs simple, song-like quatrains with simile ("like a red, red rose") and hyperbole ("till a' the seas gang dry", "the rocks melt wi' the sun") to make his devotion feel boundless.
   * **Why A:** The poem uses more than one image — the rose, the melody, drying seas, melting rocks.
   * **Why C:** The poem is written as simple rhyming quatrains, not a fourteen-line sonnet.
   * **Why D:** Non-standard phonetic spelling belongs to poems like Checking Out Me History, not this Scots love song.
