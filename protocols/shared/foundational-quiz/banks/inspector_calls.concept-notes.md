### Protagonist
- **Note:** Sheila Birling is the character who truly evolves — from a sheltered, privileged young woman who has Eva sacked from Milwards out of jealous vanity to the moral conscience of the household; the engine of the change is her own choice to accept her guilt and learn from it, which is exactly what her unrepentant parents refuse to do.

### Plot
- **Note:** The Inspector unravels a chain of cause and effect: Birling sacks Eva for asking higher wages, Sheila has her dismissed from Milwards, Gerald keeps then abandons her, Eric exploits her, and Mrs Birling refuses her charity — each act pushing her lower until suicide, and because the older Birlings will not learn, a final phone call restarts the whole reckoning.

### Themes
- **Note:** The controlling ideas are collective social responsibility ("we are members of one body") against Birling's every-man-for-himself creed, the exploitation the rigid Edwardian class system permits, and a generational divide in which the young can change while their elders cling to self-interest.

### Effects
- **Note:** An Inspector Calls is a social-realism morality play, and its emotional effect is moral discomfort and shared guilt: we are made to recognise our own capacity for the Birlings' selfishness so the guilt cannot be kept safely at a distance, we feel pity for a powerless young woman and anger at the injustice done to her, and the cyclical ending deliberately denies us comfort — leaving us implicated and unsettled rather than reassured or triumphant.

### Message
- **Note:** The play warns that we are all responsible for one another and that a society built on selfishness and inequality will be taught its lesson "in fire and blood and anguish" — an argument, written in 1945 about 1912, for a more equal and socially responsible world in which the privileged answer for how they treat the vulnerable.
