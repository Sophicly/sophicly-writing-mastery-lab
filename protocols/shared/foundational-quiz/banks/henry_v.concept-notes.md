### Protagonist
- **Note:** Henry begins as the reformed "Prince Hal" of the earlier plays and grows into a disciplined, self-mastering warrior-king; his greatness is not inherited with the crown but earned — through his own choice to cast off his wild youth, master himself, and carry the weight of his men's lives on his conscience, until the victory at Agincourt proves the king he has made himself.

### Plot
- **Note:** Henry's claim to France is affirmed, and the Dauphin's insulting gift of tennis balls hardens his resolve into invasion; he takes Harfleur, and though heavily outnumbered at Agincourt his leadership — walking disguised among his men, then rallying them with the St Crispin's Day speech — wins a decisive victory, which forces France to terms and is sealed by peace and his betrothal to Princess Katherine.

### Themes
- **Note:** The controlling ideas are true kingship as an earned burden rather than a birthright, national unity forged as a "band of brothers" that crosses rank in shared danger, and war seen with double vision — its stirring heroic glory set inseparably beside its brutality and human cost.

### Effects
- **Note:** *Henry V* is a history play in the heroic-national mode, and its emotional effect is stirring admiration and national pride: we are roused by courageous leadership and the improbable triumph at Agincourt, drawn to feel we too could belong to the "band of brothers" — yet Shakespeare shadows that pride with a sober sense of war's cost, the brutality and the dead, so we are moved and clear-eyed rather than merely triumphant.

### Message
- **Note:** The play affirms that true kingship and greatness are proven, not merely inherited — earned through self-mastery, courage and chosen responsibility — that a nation's strength lies in fellowship that crosses rank, and that heroic glory, however genuine, is inseparable from the human cost of the war that wins it.
