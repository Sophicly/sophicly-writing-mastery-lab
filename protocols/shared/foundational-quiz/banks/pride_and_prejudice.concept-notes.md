### Protagonist
- **Note:** Elizabeth Bennet begins clever, spirited and confident in her own quick judgement — a confidence that hardens into prejudice against Darcy after his slight and Wickham's lies; her fault is not lack of sense but a vanity that trusts first impressions, and only when she faces her own error on reading Darcy's letter ("How despicably have I acted!"; "Till this moment, I never knew myself") does she grow into humbled, clear-sighted self-knowledge.

### Plot
- **Note:** Darcy's proud first-impression slight and Wickham's slanders breed Elizabeth's prejudice, so she refuses Darcy's insulting first proposal; the letter it provokes overturns her false picture and shames his pride, and his quiet rescue of the eloped Lydia proves his changed character — each event causing the next until both, reformed, can marry, while Jane and Bingley's union and Charlotte's and Lydia's lesser matches frame their own.

### Themes
- **Note:** The controlling ideas are the twin faults of the title — pride and prejudice, shared by both lovers and correctable only through self-knowledge — set beside the nature of marriage (love and mutual respect against money, security or infatuation), the unreliability of first impressions, and a gentle satire of class, rank and reputation in Regency society.

### Effects
- **Note:** Pride and Prejudice is a comedy of manners, and its emotional effect is delight and warm relief: we watch clever people trip over their own pride and prejudice with a buoyant amusement, confident the follies are survivable and will be mended, and we close in satisfied warmth as the misunderstandings clear, the snobs are exposed and forgiven, and the deserving couples are united — the pleasure of disorder resolved into harmony, never the pity and fear of tragedy.

### Message
- **Note:** The novel affirms that pride and prejudice blind us to the truth about others until honest self-knowledge corrects them, that people can genuinely reform (Darcy "properly humbled", Elizabeth learning to know herself), and that a true marriage — and real human worth — rests on understanding, respect and character rather than on rank or fortune.
