### Protagonist
- **Note:** Prospero begins as the wronged, deposed Duke of Milan, an exiled magus who bends the island and everyone on it to his will and plans a reckoning against the brother who betrayed him; his evolution is his own choice to answer wrong with mercy — deciding that "the rarer action is in virtue than in vengeance", he forgives his enemies, frees Ariel, and lays down his power, vowing to break his staff and "drown my book".

### Plot
- **Note:** Usurped by his brother Antonio and exiled with his daughter Miranda, Prospero masters magic and raises a storm to wreck his enemies' ship and bring them within his power; through Ariel he tests and torments them, engineers Miranda and Ferdinand's love, and — moved by Ariel to pity — chooses forgiveness over revenge, pardoning the men who wronged him, freeing Ariel, and relinquishing his magic to return to Milan as its rightful duke.

### Themes
- **Note:** The central themes are power and its necessary relinquishment (Prospero's near-total control finally laid down), freedom versus servitude (Ariel and Caliban both longing to be free), the choice of mercy and forgiveness over vengeance, and illusion versus reality — the conjured storm and vanishing masque making us question how solid reality itself is ("We are such stuff as dreams are made on").

### Effects
- **Note:** The Tempest is a late romance, or tragicomedy, and its emotional effect is wonder and the warm, relieved delight of reconciliation: the enchantments amaze rather than terrify — no one drowns, and loss is made "rich and strange" — while the danger and betrayal dissolve into forgiveness, the union of the young lovers and restored order, so that the ending moves us with a gladness touched by a wistful poignancy at Prospero's farewell to his art.

### Message
- **Note:** The play affirms that forgiveness is the "rarer", nobler and more human answer to being wronged — mercy chosen from a position of power heals a broken world where vengeance would only prolong its wounds — and that power over others, like life itself, is fragile and dream-like, something to be held lightly and finally surrendered with grace.
