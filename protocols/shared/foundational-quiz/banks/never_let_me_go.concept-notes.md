### Protagonist
- **Note:** Kathy H. begins as a sheltered Hailsham child who only half-understands the purpose she was made for; over her eleven years as a "carer" she comes to know it fully, yet her defining response is not rebellion but a chosen dignity — she preserves love, tends others, and holds her short life together through memory, and it is that quiet, self-authored acceptance in the face of an unchosen fate that makes her arc so moving.

### Plot
- **Note:** A sheltered Hailsham upbringing conditions Kathy, Tommy and Ruth to accept their purpose as donors; Ruth's pairing with Tommy delays the love between Kathy and Tommy for years, until Ruth's dying repentance reunites them and they seek a "deferral" — only to learn from Miss Emily that deferrals never existed, so the causal road to their "completion" runs on unbroken.

### Themes
- **Note:** The central ideas are what it means to be human (the Gallery gathered to prove the clones "had souls"), memory as the keeper of love and meaning in a shortened life, the power of soft euphemism ("donors", "completion") to normalise atrocity, and mortality itself — the clones' appointed deaths holding up a mirror to every finite human life.

### Effects
- **Note:** *Never Let Me Go* is a dystopia, and its emotional effect is fear-as-warning, unease and disquiet at complicity: because the horror is normalised and the world looks like our own, and because the students never rebel, we are made to fear our own capacity to accept the unacceptable — the calm, understated tone deepens the dread and leaves us unsettled and implicated rather than merely sad.

### Message
- **Note:** The novel warns that a society which dehumanises a group for others' benefit — hiding the cruelty behind gentle language and slow habit — commits a moral horror in which the comfortable, complicit majority shares the guilt; and, more universally, that because every life is finite, it is love and memory that give even a short, appointed life its meaning and worth.
