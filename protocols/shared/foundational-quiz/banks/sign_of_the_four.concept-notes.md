### Protagonist
- **Note:** Sherlock Holmes begins the novel numbed and restless in drug-induced boredom — "my mind rebels at stagnation" — and a real case reignites him into the fiercely engaged, masterful reasoner, a self-driven cycle rooted in his own craving for mental work that sinks back to the cocaine bottle once the hunt ends; beside him Watson travels a parallel human arc, from solitary companion to a man in love, engaged to Mary Morstan.

### Plot
- **Note:** A betrayal over the plundered Agra treasure in India sets Jonathan Small on a long course of revenge: imprisoned and vowing to reclaim it, he escapes with the islander Tonga, raids Pondicherry Lodge where Tonga's poisoned dart kills Bartholomew Sholto, and flees with the gold — until Holmes's steam-launch chase down the Thames captures Small, whose confession lays bare the buried chain of cause behind every crime.

### Themes
- **Note:** The central ideas are the power of disciplined reason and observation to bring order to chaos ("Detection is, or ought to be, an exact science"), the corrupting curse of greed and plundered imperial wealth that brings betrayal and death rather than happiness, and the tension between cold calculating reason and human feeling — resolved as the loss of the treasure clears the way for Watson and Mary's love.

### Effects
- **Note:** The Sign of the Four is a detective mystery with a Gothic strain, and its emotional effect is suspense and fascinated dread: the truth is withheld and released clue by clue so we are kept partly blind, gripped by the pull to see the hidden secret revealed, while the grotesque touches — Bartholomew's fixed death-grin, the poisoned thorn, Tonga in the dark, the river fog — charge the hunt with a shiver of horror, before the solved case restores order and leaves us satisfied rather than merely amused.

### Message
- **Note:** The novel affirms that disciplined reason can pierce even the darkest confusion and uncover a truth that seemed unknowable, restoring order and justice; yet the fate of the Agra treasure — greed-driven, colonial plunder that brings only ruin and is finally lost in the Thames — insists that ill-gotten wealth destroys those who chase it, so that only its clearing away frees human happiness to flourish.
