# **GCSE English Literature Mark Scheme Mastery Quiz: Edexcel Time and Place (per-anthology) v1.0**

Per-TEXT mark-scheme-quiz bank for the **Edexcel GCSE (1ET0) Time and Place** poetry anthology. Served by
`SWML_Quiz_Bank::pick_session()` via the canonical slug ladder (`edexcel_time_place_poetry.md`) — this
bank REPLACES the generic `poetry_anthology.md` for Time and Place lessons so every worked example comes
from a Time and Place anthology poem (never a poem from another cluster's or board's anthology).

**Mark-scheme facts follow the Edexcel (1ET0) Time and Place comparison grid** — marked out of 20, split by
section: Introduction = 2 (context-building sentence 1, comparative thesis 1), Body Paragraph 1 (Form) = 5,
Body Paragraph 2 (Structure) = 5, Body Paragraph 3 (Language) = 5, Conclusion = 3; placed on Edexcel's
five-level scale (Level 1–5). Only AO2 (methods — form, structure, language and their effects, argued
comparatively) and AO3 (context — comparative and causal) carry scored criteria; AO1 does not carry its
own criterion here and AO4 (SPaG) does not apply. This is the EASIER drill — the harder examiner-level
judging lives in the matching mark-scheme-ASSESSMENT bank. Only the poem EXAMPLES are scoped to the 15
Time and Place poems: To Autumn · Composed upon Westminster Bridge · Adlestrop · In Romney Marsh ·
Home Thoughts from Abroad · London · Where the Picnic Was · Absence · I started Early – Took my Dog ·
First Flight · Presents from my Aunts in Pakistan · Hurricane Hits England · Nothing's Changed ·
Postcard from a Travel Snob · Stewart Island.

Parsed deterministically (keys + feedback stripped before reaching the client). All questions worth 2
marks each.

### **SECTION A: EDEXCEL GCSE (1ET0 — Time and Place)**

1. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Grid Structure\]**
   * **Question:** How is the Edexcel GCSE (1ET0) Time and Place anthology comparison marked?
   * **Options:** A) Out of 20, split by section — Introduction 2, Body Paragraph 1 (Form) 5, Body Paragraph 2 (Structure) 5, Body Paragraph 3 (Language) 5, Conclusion 3, B) Out of 20 but marked holistically with no section split, C) Out of 20 with AO4 SPaG forming one of the scored criteria, D) Out of 15, split between two body paragraphs only.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The Time and Place comparison is marked out of 20, split by section: Introduction 2, three Body Paragraphs of 5 (Form, Structure, Language), and a Conclusion of 3. It is placed on Edexcel's five-level scale.
   * **AO:** Vocabulary
   * **Why B:** The section split is fixed and explicit, not a single holistic judgement — each section carries its own marks.
   * **Why C:** AO4 (SPaG) is not assessed on this comparison at all, so it forms no criterion.
   * **Why D:** The grid has three body paragraphs (Form, Structure, Language), not two, and totals 20 marks with an Introduction and Conclusion.
2. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO Focus\]**
   * **Question:** Which assessment objectives carry scored criteria on the Edexcel Time and Place comparison?
   * **Options:** A) AO2 (methods) and AO3 (context); AO1 carries no separate criterion and AO4 (SPaG) does not apply, B) All four assessment objectives, weighted equally, C) AO1 and AO4 only, D) AO3 alone, with methods uncredited.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The Time and Place comparison scores AO2 (methods — form, structure, language and their effects, argued comparatively) and AO3 (context — comparative and causal). AO1 does not carry its own scored criterion here, and AO4 (SPaG) does not apply.
   * **AO:** Vocabulary
   * **Why B:** The four AOs are not all scored here — this component is built on AO2 and AO3.
   * **Why C:** AO1 carries no separate criterion and AO4 does not apply, so these cannot be the scored strands.
   * **Why D:** AO3 (context) is scored, but so is AO2 (methods) — context is not the whole allocation.
3. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Level Scale\]**
   * **Question:** Edexcel places a Time and Place response on a five-level scale running from Level 1 up to Level \[BLANK\].
   * **Answer:** 5
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The scale runs Level 1 to Level 5; the top band (Level 4–5) demands analysis that is thoughtful, perceptive, critical and exploratory.
   * **AO:** Vocabulary
   * **WhyWrong:** The Edexcel Time and Place grid runs to five levels, so the top band is Level 5 — not Level 6, which belongs to a different board's pattern.
4. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Introduction Criteria\]**
   * **Question:** The Introduction to a Time and Place comparison is worth 2 marks. What two things must it do?
   * **Options:** A) A context-building sentence (1 mark) and a comparative thesis (1 mark), B) A plot summary of each poem, C) One quotation lifted from each poem, D) A dictionary definition of "place".
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The 2-mark Introduction splits into a context-building sentence and a comparative thesis, one mark each — the context sentence evaluating how each poem's context shapes theme or purpose differently, the thesis arguing the poets' approaches against one another.
   * **AO:** AO3
   * **Why B:** Retelling each poem's events is narrative, not the evaluated context and argued thesis the two criteria require.
   * **Why C:** A quotation is evidence for the body paragraphs; the Introduction is scored on context and a comparative thesis, not quotation.
   * **Why D:** Defining the anthology's theme is not the same as an argued comparative claim about the two poems.
5. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests Form Category\]**
   * **Question:** Comparing Composed upon Westminster Bridge and Postcard from a Travel Snob in Body Paragraph 1, which count as FORM? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) Free verse, B) Dramatic monologue, C) Sonnet, D) Enjambment.
   * **Correct:** A, B, C
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** Form is the TYPE or genre of poem — free verse, dramatic monologue, sonnet, elegy, ode, ballad, lyric, narrative poem. Enjambment is a structural technique, analysed in Body Paragraph 2, not Body Paragraph 1.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why D:** Enjambment concerns how lines break and run on — a structural device, not a genre — so it belongs to the Body Paragraph 2 (Structure) criteria.
6. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests Structure Category\]**
   * **Question:** Comparing London and In Romney Marsh in Body Paragraph 2, which count as STRUCTURE? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) Enjambment, B) Caesura, C) Volta, D) Diction.
   * **Correct:** A, B, C
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** Structure is HOW a poem is built internally — metre, rhyme scheme, enjambment, caesura, stanza arrangement, line length, volta, repetition and refrain. Diction is a word-level LANGUAGE choice, analysed in Body Paragraph 3.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why D:** Diction is word choice — a language technique for Body Paragraph 3, not an internal architectural pattern.
7. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests Language Category\]**
   * **Question:** For Body Paragraph 3 on Presents from my Aunts in Pakistan and Stewart Island, which count as LANGUAGE? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) Imagery, B) Diction, C) Symbolism, D) Metre.
   * **Correct:** A, B, C
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** Language is word-level technique — imagery, figurative language, sound devices, diction, symbolism. Metre is a structural technique, analysed in Body Paragraph 2, not Body Paragraph 3.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why D:** Metre is the poem's rhythmic architecture across lines — structure, not a word-level choice.
8. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Body Paragraph Order\]**
   * **Question:** In what order are the three 5-mark body paragraphs written for a Time and Place comparison?
   * **Options:** A) Form, then Structure, then Language, B) Language, then Form, then Structure, C) Context, then Effects, then Terminology, D) Any order the student prefers.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Body Paragraph 1 analyses Form, Body Paragraph 2 Structure, Body Paragraph 3 Language — each worth 5 marks, 15 across the three.
   * **AO:** Vocabulary
   * **Why B:** The fixed sequence is Form, Structure, Language — not Language first.
   * **Why C:** Context, effects and terminology are criteria WITHIN each paragraph, not the paragraph headings.
   * **Why D:** The paragraphs follow a fixed Form–Structure–Language sequence, not a free choice.
9. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Effects Criterion\]**
   * **Question:** In every body paragraph, comparative effects must be shown in \[BLANK\] sentences — one per poem — demonstrating how each poet's technique affects the reader differently.
   * **Answer:** two
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The effects criterion asks for TWO sentences, one per poem, each naming a distinct effect on the reader — the single largest criterion within a body paragraph.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **WhyWrong:** A single combined sentence, however well written, cannot show both poets' distinct effects; the criterion is explicit that two sentences are required.
10. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Effects Framework\]**
    * **Question:** The Effects framework moves through four stages in order. Which sequence is correct?
    * **Options:** A) Focus, then Emotions, then Thoughts, then Actions, B) Actions, then Focus, then Emotions, then Thoughts, C) Emotions, then Focus, then Actions, then Thoughts, D) Thoughts, then Actions, then Focus, then Emotions.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The framework climbs Focus (what the reader notices) → Emotions (a specific feeling) → Thoughts (what that feeling makes them judge) → Actions. A vague "makes the reader feel something" never leaves the Focus stage.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why B:** Actions is the final rung, not the first — the framework begins with Focus.
    * **Why C:** Focus precedes Emotions; a specific emotion is named only after the reader's attention is directed.
    * **Why D:** Thoughts and Actions sit at the top of the ladder, reached after Focus and Emotions, not before them.
11. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Weak Verb Penalty\]**
    * **Question:** One penalty targets a "weak analytical verb". Writing about To Autumn, which verb would trigger it?
    * **Options:** A) "reveals", B) "conveys", C) "shows", D) "constructs".
    * **Correct:** C
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Shows" is the weak, generic verb the mark scheme penalises — it names an effect without analysing HOW the method produces it. "Reveals", "conveys" and "constructs" all commit to a more precise analytical claim.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why A:** "Reveals" is an accepted precise analytical verb.
    * **Why B:** "Conveys" is an accepted precise analytical verb.
    * **Why D:** "Constructs" is an accepted precise analytical verb.
12. **Type: True/False \[Tests Causal Context\]**
    * **Question:** True or False: Context earns credit only when it is causal and comparative — showing how each poet's context drives a specific method — never as a bolt-on biographical fact.
    * **Answer:** True
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Context must be causal and comparative: it explains WHY a poet's technique choice was made and is linked to BOTH poets. A standalone fact about when or where a poet lived is bolt-on and earns nothing.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **WhyWrong:** Answering False assumes any mentioned fact earns the context mark, but a fact with no causal link to a method, and no equivalent point for the paired poet, is exactly what the context penalty targets.
13. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Context Principle\]**
    * **Question:** The guiding principle for context is: "context drives concepts which drive \[BLANK\]."
    * **Answer:** methods
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Context drives concepts which drive methods — a poet's historical, social or biographical context shapes the concept explored, which in turn shapes the formal, structural or linguistic methods chosen. Context sitting apart from this chain is bolt-on.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **WhyWrong:** Near guesses such as "meaning" or "themes" name adjacent ideas, but the exact chain is context, to concepts, to methods.
14. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Conclusion Criteria\]**
    * **Question:** The Conclusion of a Time and Place comparison is worth 3 marks across three criteria. Which trio is correct?
    * **Options:** A) A restated comparative thesis, a synthesis of Form/Structure/Language, and a universal message, B) A plot summary, a fresh quotation, and a definition, C) An apology, a prediction, and a rhetorical question, D) A restatement of the two poems' titles only.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The 3-mark Conclusion carries a restated thesis (in fresh phrasing), a synthesis drawing the Form, Structure and Language paragraphs into one comparative concept, and a universal message — a "so what" reaching beyond the two poems.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why B:** Summary, quotation and definition are not the three scored Conclusion criteria — restated thesis, synthesis and universal message are.
    * **Why C:** Apologies, predictions and rhetorical questions do no analytical work and meet none of the three criteria.
    * **Why D:** Repeating the titles restates nothing of the argument, synthesis or wider significance the Conclusion is scored on.
15. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Synthesis Criterion\]**
    * **Question:** The Conclusion's synthesis criterion must connect the Form, Structure and \[BLANK\] analyses into one central comparative concept.
    * **Answer:** Language
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The synthesis criterion draws the three body paragraphs — Form, Structure and Language — together into a single controlling comparative concept, rather than summarising each in turn.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **WhyWrong:** Naming only two of the three strands, or substituting a different word such as "context", misses the exact three-part synthesis the criterion requires.
16. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Universal Message\]**
    * **Question:** A Time and Place conclusion ends: "In conclusion, losing a place is bad." Why does this fail the universal-message criterion?
    * **Options:** A) It states a flat evaluative label with no significance beyond the two poems — the criterion needs a "so what" reaching past this comparison, B) It is factually incorrect, C) It is placed at the end rather than the start, D) Universal messages are not assessed in this component.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The universal-message criterion rewards a broader significance beyond the two poems compared — a "so what" for a reader who has met neither. "Losing a place is bad" offers a flat evaluative label, not an insight extending past the comparison.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **Why B:** The claim is not inaccurate — its fault is being unearned and unspecific, not wrong.
    * **Why C:** Sentence position is not the criterion; the content and precision are.
    * **Why D:** The universal message is explicitly one of the Conclusion's three criteria.
17. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Sustained Comparison\]**
    * **Question:** A body paragraph analyses Adlestrop in full for six sentences, then tacks Where the Picnic Was on in a final sentence. What does the sustained-comparison rule say?
    * **Options:** A) It should be rejected or redirected — every analytical point must address both poems comparatively, not one poem then the other, B) It is fine, provided both poems receive roughly equal space eventually, C) It is fine in a diagnostic but not a redraft, D) It is fine provided the final sentence uses precise terminology.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The sustained-comparison rule rejects sequential, block treatment — analysing one poem fully before turning to the other — even when both eventually appear. Every point must be built so the comparison itself produces the reading.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why B:** Equal space does not fix sequential treatment; the fault is structural (block, not interwoven), not proportional.
    * **Why C:** The rule applies to every essay type, not only redrafts.
    * **Why D:** Precise terminology in one sentence does not repair a paragraph built poem-by-poem rather than comparatively throughout.
18. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Top-Band Descriptors\]**
    * **Question:** Edexcel's Level 4–5 descriptors require analysis that is thoughtful, perceptive, \[BLANK\] and exploratory.
    * **Answer:** critical
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The top band requires analysis that is thoughtful, perceptive, critical and exploratory — four qualities shown together, not any single one alone.
    * **AO:** Vocabulary
    * **WhyWrong:** Words such as "detailed" or "developed" describe adjacent qualities; the exact fourth descriptor is "critical".
19. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Critical Vocabulary\]**
    * **Question:** A Level 5 Time and Place response is described as "convincing, critical, exploratory". In this mark-scheme sense, what does "critical" mean?
    * **Options:** A) Forming an evaluative, argued judgement rather than simply describing what each poem does, B) Pointing out faults in the poets' craft, C) Writing in a formal register throughout, D) Disagreeing with another critic's reading.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. In mark-scheme language, "critical" means evaluative and argued — the response commits to a judgement about how the comparison works, rather than describing each poem's features in turn.
    * **AO:** Vocabulary
    * **Why B:** This is the everyday, negative sense of "critical" (fault-finding), not the assessment sense.
    * **Why C:** Register alone does not make a response critical; the argued judgement does.
    * **Why D:** "Critical" concerns the poems being analysed, not disagreement with any other reading.
20. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests Top-Band Features\]**
    * **Question:** Which features push a Time and Place comparison toward the top band? (Select all that apply)
    * **Options:** A) A comparative concept argued in each paragraph, B) Integrated quotations from BOTH poems, C) Causal, comparative context, D) Block treatment of one poem then the other.
    * **Correct:** A, B, C
    * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
    * **Feedback:** A comparative concept, integrated evidence from both poems, and causal comparative context all define top-band work. Block treatment — one poem fully, then the other — breaks the sustained comparison the top band requires.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why D:** Block treatment feels organised, but handling one poem then the other prevents the interwoven comparison the top band demands.
21. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Comparative Thesis\]**
    * **Question:** Two Time and Place theses: (i) "Both Adlestrop and Absence explore remembered places." (ii) "While Thomas renders a chance stop as a still moment that widens outward into connection with a whole landscape, Jennings renders a revisited garden as an unchanged surface whose very ordinariness detonates private grief." Why does (ii) earn the thesis mark?
    * **Options:** A) It states a precise COMPARATIVE argument about the poets' distinct approaches, not just an idea both poems share, B) It is longer and denser, C) It uses the words "time and place", D) It avoids naming either poet.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The comparative thesis criterion needs an argued comparison of the poets' approaches — naming a shared topic ("remembered places") is not the same as arguing HOW the two treatments differ.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why B:** Length is not a criterion; the first thesis could be expanded without becoming comparative.
    * **Why C:** Naming the anthology's theme is not the same as an argued comparative claim.
    * **Why D:** Both poets are in fact named in the strong thesis; omitting names is not what earns the mark.
22. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Topic Sentence\]**
    * **Question:** A Body Paragraph 1 topic sentence must establish a comparative concept about how BOTH poets' FORM choices convey \[BLANK\].
    * **Answer:** meaning
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The topic-sentence criterion is a comparative concept about how both poets' form choices convey MEANING — it must argue what the form choice means, not simply that a form was chosen.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **WhyWrong:** Words like "emotion" or "effect" name adjacent ideas, but the exact criterion word is "meaning" — form must be argued as meaning-making, with effect analysed later in the paragraph.
23. **Type: True/False \[Tests Introduction Context\]**
    * **Question:** True or False: The Introduction's context sentence must evaluate how EACH poem's context shapes its theme or purpose differently, not merely note a shared timeframe.
    * **Answer:** True
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The context-building criterion asks the student to evaluate how each poem's context shapes theme or purpose DIFFERENTLY. A bare shared decade names a timeframe but evaluates no difference and drives no thesis.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **WhyWrong:** Answering False assumes a single shared context point suffices, but the criterion demands the DIFFERENCE between how each poem's context operates.
24. **Type: Ranking \[Tests Introduction\]**
    * **Question:** Rank these four openings to a Time and Place comparison of Hurricane Hits England and Nothing's Changed from WEAKEST to STRONGEST (type the letters in order, weakest first).
    * **Options:** A) "Nichols and Afrika both write about place.", B) "Nichols and Afrika both explore the theme of time and place in their poems.", C) "Nichols wrote Hurricane Hits England from the perspective of a Caribbean-born speaker in England, while Afrika wrote Nothing's Changed about apartheid-era District Six, so both poems consider how place shapes identity.", D) "While Nichols's Caribbean-born speaker reads an English storm as an ancestral force reconnecting her to her origins, Afrika's return to District Six reads a rebuilt place as proof that apartheid's divisions persist beneath an unchanged surface — so both poems argue that a place carries histories the present cannot erase."
    * **Correct:** A, B, C, D
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Each rung adds a required element. A bare shared subject is weakest. A named shared theme with no argument is still descriptive. A context point attached to each poem, though not yet argued as a difference, is stronger. The top rung states a precise comparative thesis in which each poet's DIFFERENT context-driven approach is argued — meeting both of the Introduction's criteria together.
    * **WhyWrong:** Weakest to strongest runs from a bare shared subject, to a named theme with no argument, to a context point per poem without a stated difference, to a precise comparative thesis arguing how the poets' different contexts drive different treatments of time and place.
