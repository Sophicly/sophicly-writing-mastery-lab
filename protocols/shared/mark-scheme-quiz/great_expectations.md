# **GCSE English Literature Mark Scheme Mastery Quiz: AQA Great Expectations (per-text) v1.0**

Per-TEXT mark-scheme-quiz bank for **AQA 8702 Great Expectations** (Charles Dickens — 19th-century
novel, Paper 1 Section B). Served by `SWML_Quiz_Bank::pick_session()` via the canonical slug ladder
(`great_expectations.md`) — this bank REPLACES the generic `19th_century.md` for Great Expectations
lessons so every worked example comes from Dickens's novel, never another 19th-century set text.

**Mark-scheme facts are the AQA 8702 novel grid** (30 marks: AO1 12 / AO2 12 / AO3 6; six levels of
five marks each; NO AO4 on the novel — technical accuracy is assessed only on the Shakespeare and
modern-text questions) — reused verbatim from the vetted `great_expectations` MSA bank. Only the
worked examples are scoped to Great Expectations: Pip · Magwitch · Miss Havisham · Estella · Joe ·
Satis House · the themes of gentility, social class, ambition, worth and guilt.

Parsed deterministically (keys + feedback stripped before reaching the client). All questions worth
2 marks each.

### **SECTION A: AQA (8702 — Great Expectations)**

1. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO Weighting\]**
   * **Question:** How is the AQA GCSE (8702) 19th-century novel question — the Great Expectations essay — marked?
   * **Options:** A) AO1=12, AO2=12, AO3=6 (total 30), with no AO4, B) AO2 only, on the printed extract, C) AO3 context carries the majority of the marks, D) AO4 SPaG carries the final marks, as on the Shakespeare question.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The Great Expectations response is marked out of 30 across six levels of five marks: 12 for argument (AO1), 12 for methods (AO2), 6 for context (AO3). Unlike the Shakespeare and modern-text questions, there is no AO4 (technical accuracy) on the novel.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why B:** Close analysis matters greatly, but believing AO2 is marked alone ignores the equal 12 marks for whole-text argument and the 6 for context.
   * **Why C:** Context feels central to a "novel of ideas", yet AO3 is the lightest strand at just 6 of the 30 marks — never the majority.
   * **Why D:** SPaG is assessed on the AQA Shakespeare and modern-text questions, which makes this tempting, but AO4 does not apply to the 19th-century novel.
2. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO1 Level 6\]**
   * **Question:** Complete the AQA Level 6 AO1 descriptor: a top-band Great Expectations response is "critical, exploratory and \[BLANK\] in its response to task and whole text".
   * **Answer:** Conceptualised
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The AQA Level 6 AO1 strand is "critical, exploratory, conceptualised response to task and whole text". "Conceptualised" separates the top band — the response is organised around an idea (ambition mistaking wealth for worth), not around Pip's actions or the sequence of events.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Guesses like "detailed" or "thoughtful" describe lower bands — "thoughtful" is the Level 5 keyword, whereas the Level 6 word is conceptualised: the novel treated as the vehicle for a concept.
3. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO1 Conceptualised\]**
   * **Question:** A student writes: "Dickens presents Pip as a poor boy who comes into a fortune, becomes a snob, and is finally humbled." For AO1, which AQA Level does this response sit at, and why?
   * **Options:** A) Level 6 — a critical, exploratory, conceptualised response to task and whole text, B) Level 5 — a thoughtful, developed response with apt references integrated into interpretation, C) Level 3 — some explained comment that stays at character and plot level rather than concept, D) Level 1 — an undeveloped, simple comment with no explanation.
   * **Correct:** C
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Tracing Pip's rise and fall is character-and-plot narration — AQA Level 3 "some explained response". To climb, the student must treat Pip as a vehicle for a concept (ambition mistaking wealth for worth), not narrate his career.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why A:** The conceptualised, critical argument the top band demands is absent — this narrates an arc, it does not argue an idea.
   * **Why B:** A thoughtful, developed response with references integrated into interpretation needs both development and quotation, and this has neither.
   * **Why D:** It is explained comment about the character, not a bare simple comment — it sits above the lowest band.
4. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO1 Judicious References\]**
   * **Question:** Two responses both cite Magwitch's "I've made a gentleman on you!" One lists it after a point; the other selects it to pin an argument that Pip's gentility is bought by the very convict he is ashamed of. For AO1, which AQA phrase distinguishes the second from the first?
   * **Options:** A) It embeds more quotations to demonstrate the breadth of its supporting evidence, B) It makes "judicious use of precise references to support interpretation" rather than general reference, C) It integrates the quotation with a clearly explained comment on its effect, D) It identifies the writer's method in the quotation using subject terminology.
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA Level 6 AO1 is "judicious use of precise references to support interpretation(s)". "Judicious" means the reference is chosen to do argumentative work, not listed. A precise reference tied to a concept outweighs many general ones.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why A:** AQA rewards judicious selection, not quantity — more quotations can dilute precision.
   * **Why C:** Explaining the effect of the quotation is AO2 work; the AO1 move is how the reference is selected to support interpretation.
   * **Why D:** Naming the method and terminology is AO2 surface behaviour, not the AO1 distinction of judicious selection.
5. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests AO1 Quality\]**
   * **Question:** Which features signal a top-band AQA AO1 response on Great Expectations? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) Judicious single-word quotations (Estella's "coarse and common"), B) Treating Pip and Estella as constructs used to explore ideas, C) Retelling the plot from the marshes to London in sequence, D) A conceptualised thesis that ambition mistakes wealth for worth.
   * **Correct:** A, B, D
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, D. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** Judicious micro-quotes, construct-level thinking, and a conceptual thesis all push you into Level 6. Retelling Pip's journey from the forge to London caps you at the lower bands — it shows knowledge of events, not argument.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why C:** Retelling the plot feels like proving you know the text, but it only shows knowledge of events — examiners reward argument and analysis, not narrative summary.
6. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO3 Integration\]**
   * **Question:** True or False: For AQA, context should be integrated into your analysis of Great Expectations rather than saved for a standalone history paragraph.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA Level 6 AO3 rewards context shown by "specific, detailed links between context/text/task" — Victorian faith in gentility as social rank driving Dickens's argument that true worth is moral, not monetary — never a bolt-on paragraph.
   * **AO:** AO3
   * **WhyWrong:** Choosing False usually comes from being taught a separate "context paragraph" — that bolt-on habit caps your AO3 marks because it never explains why Dickens made his choices.
7. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO2 Level\]**
   * **Question:** A paragraph reads: "Dickens uses the metaphor of Satis House being 'all in decay'. This is a technique. It tells us Miss Havisham is stuck in the past." For AO2, which AQA Level fits, and what is the ceiling?
   * **Options:** A) Level 6 — analysis with sustained exploration of the effects, B) Level 5 — examination of how the writer's methods work, C) Level 3 — identification of the method with only a general comment on effect, D) Level 4 — clear explanation of the method and its effect.
   * **Correct:** C
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Naming the metaphor and adding "it tells us she is stuck" is AQA Level 3 AO2: "identification of the writer's methods" with only a general comment. Level 4 needs "clear explanation of the effects"; Level 5 "examination"; Level 6 "analysis... exploration of effects of the writer's methods" with terminology used judiciously.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why A:** No analysis and no exploration of layered effects — the top band is out of reach here.
   * **Why B:** "Examination" at Level 5 requires sustained working-through of how the method makes meaning, absent here.
   * **Why D:** "Clear explanation" at Level 4 needs the effect explained, not merely asserted.
8. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO2 Level 6\]**
   * **Question:** Complete the AQA Level 6 AO2 descriptor: "Analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used \[BLANK\]".
   * **Answer:** Judiciously
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA Level 6 AO2 is "analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously". "Judiciously" warns against feature-spotting: terminology earns marks only when it is selected to serve the analysis of effect.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **WhyWrong:** Guesses like "accurately" or "frequently" borrow from lower bands — the AQA word is judiciously, meaning terminology used to serve analysis of effect, not scattered to impress.
9. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests AO2 Level 6\]**
   * **Question:** Which statements are true of an AQA Level 6 AO2 response on Great Expectations? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) It moves from identifying a method (the stopped clocks at Satis House) to analysing how it creates meaning, B) It explores several effects the writer's methods have on the reader, C) It names as many techniques as possible to demonstrate range, D) It uses subject terminology judiciously, only where it serves the analysis.
   * **Correct:** A, B, D
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, D. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** AQA Level 6 AO2 is "analysis of the writer's methods... exploration of effects of the writer's methods... subject terminology used judiciously". Analysis, exploration of effects and judicious terminology are all top-band. Maximising technique-count is feature-spotting, which the mark scheme penalises.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why C:** Naming many techniques is feature-spotting — AQA rewards judicious selection and exploration of effect, not quantity.
10. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO2 Effects\]**
    * **Question:** AQA AO2 climbs from "explanation of effects" (Level 4) to "exploration of effects" (Level 6). A response reads Pip's guilt as a sustained pattern — the stolen file, the pork pie, the leg-iron — that makes conscience feel inescapable, opening up several effects. Which does this demonstrate?
    * **Options:** A) Explanation — it states a single fixed effect, B) Exploration — it opens up several effects and how they shift the reader, C) Feature-spotting — it merely names more techniques, D) Identification — it only spots the method.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Explanation" states one effect; "exploration" opens up several possible meanings and how they work on the reader. Reading a sustained motif and exploring layered effects is the AQA Level 4 to Level 6 AO2 climb.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why A:** Explanation stops at one stated effect; exploring the motif's layered pressure on the reader is what lifts the band.
    * **Why C:** Naming more techniques is feature-spotting, not exploration.
    * **Why D:** Merely spotting the leg-iron or the file is Level 3 identification, the foot of the AO2 ladder.
11. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO3 Bolt-on\]**
    * **Question:** "Great Expectations was published in 1861, and Victorians admired gentlemen." A student adds this to the end of a paragraph. For AO3, why does it stay low in the AQA bands?
    * **Options:** A) It is factually inaccurate about the Victorian period, B) It is bolt-on context — AQA Level 6 AO3 needs "specific, detailed links between context, text and task" where context drives the interpretation, C) It is too brief — a contextual point must run to several sentences to score, D) Context is credited only on the poetry question, not on the novel.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. A free-standing fact is bolt-on. AQA Level 6 AO3 is "exploration of ideas/perspectives/contextual factors shown by specific, detailed links between context/text/task". Context must DRIVE the concept: Victorian faith in gentility as social rank drives Dickens's argument that true worth is moral, not monetary.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **Why A:** The fact is broadly accurate — accuracy is not the problem; integration is.
    * **Why C:** Length is not the issue; a short linked point can be top-band.
    * **Why D:** Context is assessed throughout the AQA novel question through AO3.
12. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO3 Level 6\]**
    * **Question:** Complete the AQA Level 6 AO3 descriptor: context should be shown by "specific, detailed \[BLANK\] between context, text and task".
    * **Answer:** Links
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA Level 6 AO3 is "specific, detailed links between context/text/task". Context earns top marks only when it is connected to the text and the question, never when it sits alone as a fact.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **WhyWrong:** Guesses like "facts" or "references" miss the wording — the AQA word is links: context, text and task must be linked, not listed separately.
13. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests AO3 Chain\]**
    * **Question:** A response argues: "Victorian belief that the gentleman was defined by rank and wealth (context) drives Dickens's idea that Pip mistakes gentility for goodness (concept), which drives his choice to have Pip grow ashamed of the loyal Joe (method)." Which AQA AO3 strengths does this show? (Select all that apply)
    * **Options:** A) Context is used to drive a concept rather than bolted on as a fact, B) There are specific, detailed links between context, text and task, C) It explores ideas and perspectives opened up by the context, D) It lists a standalone historical fact with no link to text or task.
    * **Correct:** A, B, C
    * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
    * **Feedback:** This is the AQA Level 6 AO3 chain: context drives concept drives method, with "specific, detailed links between context/text/task" and "exploration of ideas/perspectives/contextual factors". Listing a standalone fact is the opposite of what is happening here.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **Why D:** The response does the reverse of bolt-on — every element is linked to text and task.
14. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO Weighting\]**
    * **Question:** True or False: In the AQA 19th-century novel question, AO1 and AO2 each carry 12 marks and AO3 carries 6, for a 30-mark total.
    * **Answer:** True
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The novel response is marked out of 30 across six levels of five marks each, with AO1 and AO2 weighted equally at 12 marks each and AO3 (context) carrying 6 — half their weight.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **WhyWrong:** Answering False often comes from assuming context is worth as much as analysis — but AO3 carries 6 marks against 12 apiece for AO1 and AO2, so it is weighted at half.
15. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO4 Exclusion\]**
    * **Question:** Is AO4 (technical accuracy / SPaG) assessed on the AQA Great Expectations question?
    * **Options:** A) Yes — it carries the final marks, as on every Literature question, B) No — there is no AO4 on the AQA novel; technical accuracy is assessed on the Shakespeare and modern-text questions, C) Yes, but here AO4 means context rather than spelling, D) No — AO4 is simply relocated to AO2 on this paper.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The AQA novel is marked on AO1, AO2 and AO3 only. Unlike the Shakespeare and modern-prose questions, there is no AO4 (technical accuracy) on the 19th-century novel — so spelling and punctuation carry no separate marks here.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** SPaG is assessed on the AQA Shakespeare and modern-text questions, not on the novel — assuming it applies everywhere is the trap.
    * **Why C:** AO4 meaning context is the Edexcel IGCSE Spec A anomaly, not the AQA novel — AQA does not use AO4 on this question at all.
    * **Why D:** Technical accuracy is not relocated to AO2; it simply is not assessed on the AQA novel.
16. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Level 6 Label\]**
    * **Question:** AQA Level 6 (26–30) is labelled "Convincing, critical analysis and \[BLANK\]".
    * **Answer:** Exploration
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The top band (26–30) is "Convincing, critical analysis and exploration" — a critical, exploratory, conceptualised response to task and whole text, supported by judicious use of precise references.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **WhyWrong:** "Evaluation" or "analysis" name the skills, but AQA's exact Level 6 heading pairs "analysis" with "exploration".
17. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Level 4 Name\]**
    * **Question:** In the AQA mark scheme, what is the name of Level 4 (16–20 marks)?
    * **Options:** A) Clear understanding, B) Convincing, critical analysis and exploration, C) Thoughtful, developed response, D) Simple, explicit comments.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA names Level 4 "Clear understanding" — the response is clear, explained and sustained, using references effectively to support explanation. The bands above it are "Thoughtful, developed response" (Level 5) and "Convincing, critical analysis and exploration" (Level 6).
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why B:** This is the name of the very top band (Level 6, 26–30) — clear explanation lifts to critical exploration only two levels higher, so it overshoots.
    * **Why C:** "Thoughtful, developed response" is Level 5 (21–25); it sits one band above clear understanding.
    * **Why D:** "Simple, explicit comments" is the bottom band (Level 1, 1–5) — narrative and descriptive rather than clear and explained.
18. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Band Boundary\]**
    * **Question:** Which mark range is AQA Level 5 ("Thoughtful, developed response")?
    * **Options:** A) 16–20, B) 21–25, C) 26–30, D) 11–15.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA marks the novel across six levels of five marks each. Level 5 spans 21–25; Level 6 sits above at 26–30 and Level 4 below at 16–20.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** 16–20 is Level 4 ("Clear understanding"), one band below.
    * **Why C:** 26–30 is Level 6, the top band ("Convincing, critical analysis and exploration").
    * **Why D:** 11–15 is Level 3 ("Explained"), two bands below.
19. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Vocabulary\]**
    * **Question:** AQA asks for a "critical style". In a mark scheme, what does "critical" actually mean?
    * **Options:** A) Pointing out the novel's faults and the things Dickens did badly, B) Forming an evaluative, argued judgement and taking a position on interpretation, C) Writing in a consistently serious, formal and academic tone, D) Disagreeing with the interpretations other readers have offered.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. In a mark scheme "critical" does NOT mean fault-finding. It means evaluative and argued — you weigh interpretations and commit to a position. AQA pairs it as "critical, exploratory": an argued reading that opens up meaning.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** This is the everyday sense of "critical" (negative), not the assessment sense.
    * **Why C:** Tone is not what "critical" rewards — an argued judgement is.
    * **Why D:** Critical means evaluating the text, not other students' readings.
20. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Vocabulary\]**
    * **Question:** Two students argue Estella's contempt reshapes how Pip sees himself. One quotes ten lines of the Satis House scene; the other selects the single phrase "coarse and common" to pin the point. Whose reference is "judicious" — and what does "judicious" mean?
    * **Options:** A) The first — judicious means marshalling as much supporting evidence as possible, B) The second — judicious means a well-judged, selective reference chosen to do the most argumentative work, C) Both equally — judicious simply means quoting the text accurately and in full, D) Neither — judicious means choosing the longest, most detailed quotation available.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Judicious" means well-judged and selective — the reference is chosen because it does real work, not because it is long or abundant. A precise phrase outperforms ten general lines. This is why AQA Level 6 says "judicious use of precise references".
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Judicious is about wise selection, not quantity — more can mean less precise.
    * **Why C:** Accuracy alone is not judiciousness; the choice must serve the argument.
    * **Why D:** Length is irrelevant; aptness to the point is what counts.
21. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Extract Springboard\]**
    * **Question:** For the AQA 19th-century novel question you are given a printed extract from Great Expectations. What is the "springboard" way to use it?
    * **Options:** A) Spend the whole answer inside the extract and mention the rest of the novel in the conclusion, B) Ignore the extract and write from memory, C) Use the extract as your AO2 evidence bank for close analysis, then bounce out to the whole novel for AO1 argument and arc, D) Copy large chunks of the extract to show you have read it.
    * **Correct:** C
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The extract is your evidence bank for detailed language analysis (AO2). To reach the top bands you must move beyond it to argue about the whole novel (AO1) — Pip's arc from the forge to London and back to humility. This whole-text reach is a Grade 9 separator.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Staying in the extract feels safe because the material is in front of you, but never moving beyond it means you cannot prove whole-text understanding.
    * **Why B:** Ignoring the extract throws away your richest source of detailed language evidence — it is given to you precisely for close analysis.
    * **Why D:** Copying chunks proves nothing about your thinking; the extract is for analysing word choices, not for transcription.
22. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Vocabulary\]**
    * **Question:** AQA Level 6 rewards an "exploratory" response. An exploratory response is one that...
    * **Options:** A) considers multiple interpretations or ambiguities — for instance whether Magwitch's fortune redeems or corrupts Pip — weighing alternative readings, B) covers as much of the plot as possible in sequence, C) uses the most difficult vocabulary available to sound sophisticated, D) includes as much historical context as it can fit.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Exploratory" means weighing alternative readings of the same detail — whether Magwitch's secret fortune redeems or corrupts Pip — rather than settling for the obvious meaning. It is what the AQA top band pairs with "critical" and "conceptualised".
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why B:** Covering the plot is narration, the opposite of exploring alternative meanings.
    * **Why C:** Difficult vocabulary is diction, not the weighing of readings that "exploratory" names.
    * **Why D:** Context supports AO3; stacking it does not make a response exploratory.
23. **Type: Ranking \[Tests AO1\]**
    * **Question:** Rank these four Great Expectations openings from WEAKEST to STRONGEST by AQA AO1 Level (type the letters in order, weakest first).
    * **Options:** A) "Dickens constructs Pip's 'great expectations' as an interrogation of a society that mistakes wealth for worth, using his ascent to expose the hollowness of gentility itself.", B) "Pip is a boy who is given money by a secret benefactor and becomes a gentleman in London.", C) "Ambition is a bad thing in the novel, and it makes Pip unhappy and ashamed.", D) "Dickens presents ambition as corrupting through Pip's snobbery towards Joe, making the reader judge how far social climbing distorts him."
    * **Correct:** B, C, D, A
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Each rung is one clear AQA band higher. The plot-summary rung, narrating events only, is Level 1 (simple comment). The bare value-judgement about a bad thing causing unhappiness, with no method and no evidence, is Level 2 (supported comment). The clear, explained rung that names a method (Pip's snobbery towards Joe) and its effect on the reader is Level 4 (clear explained response). The critical, conceptualised rung, arguing an idea from the first line, is Level 6. The ladder is plot, to comment, to explained method-and-effect, to concept.
    * **WhyWrong:** Weakest to strongest runs from plot narration, to a value-judgement with no method, to an explained method plus reader effect, to a conceptualised argument. Each rung is one AQA Level higher; the jump to the top is the move from explaining events to arguing a concept.
