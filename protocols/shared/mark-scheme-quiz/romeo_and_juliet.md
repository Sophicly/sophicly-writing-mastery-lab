# **GCSE English Literature Mark Scheme Mastery Quiz: AQA Romeo and Juliet (per-text) v1.0**

Per-TEXT mark-scheme-quiz bank for the **AQA 8702 Shakespeare** question, scoped to **Romeo and
Juliet**. Served by `SWML_Quiz_Bank::pick_session()` via the canonical slug ladder
(`romeo_and_juliet.md`) — this bank REPLACES the generic `shakespeare.md` for R&J lessons so every
worked example comes from Romeo and Juliet (never Macbeth or any other set text).

**Mark-scheme facts match the AQA 8702 Shakespeare grid** as stated in the vetted generic
`shakespeare.md` AQA section and corroborated by the R&J mark-scheme-ASSESSMENT bank: the Levels 1–6
grid marks AO1, AO2 and AO3 out of 30, with a further 4 marks for AO4 (technical accuracy / SPaG) —
34 marks in total. Band boundaries: Level 6 (26–30), Level 5 (21–25), Level 4 (16–20), Level 3
(11–15). This drill is EASIER than the mark-scheme-ASSESSMENT bank — it tests recall and recognition
of the grid, not examiner-level judgement.

Every worked example comes from Romeo and Juliet — the feud between the Montagues and Capulets, the
star-crossed lovers, Mercutio, Tybalt, Friar Laurence, and the play's key lines ("These violent
delights have violent ends", "My only love sprung from my only hate", "a rose by any other name",
the oxymoron "loving hate", "I'll no longer be a Capulet").

Parsed deterministically (keys + feedback stripped before reaching the client). All questions worth
2 marks each.

### **SECTION A: AQA (8702 — Romeo and Juliet)**

1. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Mark Allocation\]**
   * **Question:** How are the marks allocated on the AQA Romeo and Juliet (Shakespeare) question?
   * **Options:** A) 30 marks across AO1, AO2 and AO3, with a further 4 marks for AO4 (SPaG) — 34 in total, B) 40 marks shared equally between four AOs, C) 30 marks for AO2 analysis alone, D) 20 marks with no AO4 assessed.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The Levels 1–6 grid marks AO1, AO2 and AO3 out of 30; AO4 (technical accuracy) adds 4 marks on top, for 34 in total. Proofreading your Romeo and Juliet essay earns real marks.
   * **AO:** AO4
   * **Why B:** The four objectives are not equally weighted — the level grid covers AO1–AO3 out of 30 and technical accuracy sits separately at 4 marks.
   * **Why C:** Analysis of methods is one strand inside the 30-mark grid, not the whole allocation; argument and context are marked alongside it.
   * **Why D:** Technical accuracy IS assessed on the AQA Shakespeare response — 4 marks for spelling, punctuation, vocabulary and sentence structures.
2. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO4 SPaG\]**
   * **Question:** True or False: For the AQA Romeo and Juliet response, AO4 (Technical Accuracy / SPaG) contributes 4 additional marks alongside AO1, AO2 and AO3.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA applies AO4 SPaG (4 marks) to the Shakespeare response on top of AO1 + AO2 + AO3. Proofread your essay on the feud and the lovers carefully — those marks are real.
   * **AO:** AO4
   * **WhyWrong:** It is easy to assume technical accuracy only matters on Language papers, but AQA attaches AO4 SPaG marks to the Shakespeare question, so accurate spelling and punctuation still earn credit here.
3. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Number of Levels\]**
   * **Question:** Across how many levels is the AQA Shakespeare (Romeo and Juliet) 30-mark grid marked?
   * **Options:** A) Four levels, B) Five levels, C) Six levels, D) Nine levels.
   * **Correct:** C
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The AQA grid runs across six levels — Level 1 (1–5) up to Level 6 (26–30) — each carrying banded AO1, AO2 and AO3 descriptors.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why A:** Four levels matches some shorter mark schemes, but the AQA Shakespeare grid is banded into six.
   * **Why B:** Five levels is the Edexcel pattern; AQA's 30-mark grid runs to six.
   * **Why D:** Nine is the GCSE grade scale, not the number of marking levels.
4. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO1 Conceptual\]**
   * **Question:** AQA Level 6 demands a \[BLANK\] response — one that treats Romeo and Juliet as a conscious construct exploring big ideas, not a story to retell.
   * **Answer:** Conceptualised
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Conceptualised" is the Level 6 AO1 descriptor. Treat Shakespeare as a thinker arguing a thesis — for example, that the feud dramatises how inherited hatred destroys the young — not as a storyteller.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Likely guesses such as "detailed" or "developed" describe lower bands; Level 6 specifically rewards a conceptualised response that argues a big idea about the play.
5. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO1 Conceptualised\]**
   * **Question:** Which opening best fits AQA's Level 6 "conceptualised" descriptor for Romeo and Juliet?
   * **Options:** A) "Romeo and Juliet meet at a party, marry in secret and then both die.", B) "There are lots of techniques in this extract.", C) "Shakespeare constructs the feud as an inherited order that values loyalty to a name above life itself, so the lovers' deaths read as the violence such a system was always going to produce.", D) "This extract is from Act 1.".
   * **Correct:** C
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Option C treats the play as a conscious construct exploring a big idea — the conceptualised, exploratory thesis Level 6 rewards. The lift comes from the argument, not the vocabulary.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why A:** This retells the plot; narrative sequence without argument caps the response at the lower bands.
   * **Why B:** Announcing that techniques exist is feature-spotting with no conceptual claim about what Shakespeare explores.
   * **Why D:** Locating the extract is orientation, not interpretation — it makes no argument about meaning.
6. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO1 Judicious\]**
   * **Question:** AQA Level 6 rewards "judicious use of precise references." Two students argue Juliet matures: one quotes six lines about love; the other selects the half-line "I'll no longer be a Capulet". Whose references are "judicious", and why?
   * **Options:** A) The first — judicious means marshalling as much supporting evidence as possible, B) The second — judicious means a well-judged, selective reference chosen to do the most argumentative work, C) Both equally — judicious just means quoting the text accurately, D) Neither — judicious means choosing the longest quotation available.
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Judicious" means well-judged and selective. The precise half-line "I'll no longer be a Capulet" pins Juliet's turn to self-determination and outperforms six general lines. Quality of choice, not quantity.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why A:** Piling up quotations shows coverage, not judgement — judicious is about wise selection.
   * **Why C:** Accuracy alone is not judiciousness; the CHOICE must serve the argument.
   * **Why D:** Length is irrelevant — a precise micro-quotation beats a long block.
7. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO1 References\]**
   * **Question:** AQA Level 6 AO1 rewards "judicious use of \[BLANK\] references to support interpretation(s)" — precisely chosen, not piled up.
   * **Answer:** precise
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The descriptor is "judicious use of precise references to support interpretation(s)". A single precise reference — the oxymoron "loving hate", say — tied to an argument beats many general ones.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Guesses like "detailed" or "relevant" circle the idea, but the AQA word paired with "judicious" is "precise".
8. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Evaluative Vocabulary\]**
   * **Question:** In a mark scheme, AQA asks for a "critical" style. What does "critical" actually mean here?
   * **Options:** A) Pointing out the play's faults and what Shakespeare did badly, B) Forming an evaluative, argued judgement and taking a position on interpretation, C) Writing in a consistently serious, formal tone throughout, D) Disagreeing with the readings other students offer.
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. In a mark scheme "critical" does NOT mean fault-finding. It means evaluative and argued — you weigh interpretations of the feud, the lovers or Friar Laurence's part and commit to a position. AQA pairs it as "critical, exploratory".
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why A:** This is the everyday, negative sense of "critical", not the assessment sense.
   * **Why C:** Tone is not what "critical" rewards — an argued judgement is.
   * **Why D:** Critical means evaluating the text, not correcting other students.
9. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests AO2 Methods\]**
   * **Question:** Which count as valid AO2 Writer's Methods to analyse in Romeo and Juliet? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) Specific word choices and their connotations (the oxymoron "loving hate"), B) Dramatic form (soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony), C) Structural shifts across a scene, D) The biography of the actor playing Romeo.
   * **Correct:** A, B, C
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** Word-level choice, dramatic form and structure are all AO2 methods — the deliberate choices Shakespeare made. But a method only counts once you pair it with its effect on the audience (Focus/Feel/Think/Act); naming "dramatic irony" alone is feature-spotting. An actor's biography sits outside the text.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why D:** It is tempting to count anything about performance as a method, but an actor's biography sits outside the text — writer's methods are the choices Shakespeare made on the page.
10. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO2 Verb Ladder\]**
    * **Question:** The AQA AO2 verb ladder climbs identify → explain → examine → \[BLANK\], where the final verb is the Level 6 move.
    * **Answer:** explore
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AO2 climbs from "identification" of methods to "exploration of effects of the writer's methods". "Exploration" is the Level 6 verb; "examination" tops out at Level 5.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **WhyWrong:** "Explain" or "examine" are middle rungs; the Level 6 AO2 verb is "explore".
11. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Level 5 vs Level 6 AO2\]**
    * **Question:** A student names the oxymoron "loving hate" and adds "this shows Romeo is confused." For AO2, which AQA Level does this reach, and why?
    * **Options:** A) Level 6 — analysis with exploration of layered effects, B) Level 5 — examination of how the method works, C) Level 3 — identification of the method with only a general comment on effect, D) Level 4 — clear explanation of the effect.
    * **Correct:** C
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Naming the oxymoron and asserting "he is confused" is Level 3 AO2: identification of the method with only a general comment on effect. Level 4 needs the effect clearly explained; Level 5 "examination"; Level 6 "analysis... exploration of effects".
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why A:** There is no analysis and no exploration of layered effects here — Level 6 is out of reach.
    * **Why B:** "Examination" (Level 5) requires sustained working-through of how the method makes meaning, absent here.
    * **Why D:** "Clear explanation" (Level 4) needs the effect explained, not just asserted.
12. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO2 Judicious\]**
    * **Question:** Complete the AQA Level 6 AO2 descriptor: "Analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used \[BLANK\]."
    * **Answer:** judiciously
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA Level 6 AO2 = "Analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously." Terminology earns marks only when it is chosen to serve the analysis of effect — not scattered to impress.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **WhyWrong:** The AQA word is "judiciously" — terminology used to serve analysis, not feature-spotted for show.
13. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO3 Integration\]**
    * **Question:** "Romeo and Juliet was written in Elizabethan times, when fathers arranged their daughters' marriages." A student bolts this sentence onto the end of a paragraph. For AO3, why does it stay low in the bands?
    * **Options:** A) It is factually inaccurate about Elizabethan marriage, B) It is bolt-on context — AQA rewards "specific, detailed links between context, text and task" where context drives the interpretation, C) It is too short — a contextual point must run several sentences to score, D) Context is credited only on the Language paper.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA AO3 credits "specific, detailed links between context/text/task" — never a free-standing fact. Here, inherited authority over marriage should DRIVE the reading of Juliet's struggle for autonomy, not sit beside it.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **Why A:** The fact is broadly accurate — accuracy is not the problem; integration is.
    * **Why C:** Length is not the issue; a short linked point can be top band.
    * **Why D:** AO3 (context) is assessed throughout the AQA Shakespeare question.
14. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO3 Links\]**

    * **Answer:** links
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The word is "links": the Elizabethan honour culture behind Tybalt's challenge earns marks only when it is linked to the text and the question, never bolted on as a standalone fact.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **WhyWrong:** The AQA word is "links" — context, text and task must be linked, not listed separately.
15. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO3 Integration\]**
    * **Question:** True or False: For AQA Romeo and Juliet, context (AO3) should be integrated to drive your interpretation, not dropped in as a standalone history paragraph.
    * **Answer:** True
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA rewards context as the driver of Shakespeare's concept — the honour code that makes Tybalt's challenge feel inevitable — woven through the argument, not a bolt-on.

16. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Exploration vs Explanation\]**
    * **Question:** AQA AO2 moves from "explanation of effects" (Level 4) to "exploration of effects" (Level 6). What does "exploration" add?
    * **Options:** A) It names a greater number of techniques, B) It opens up several possible effects or meanings and how they shift the audience, rather than stating a single fixed effect, C) It develops the same single effect at greater length, D) It works without using any quotations.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Explanation" states one effect; "exploration" opens up several possible meanings — reading Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, say, as playful, cynical AND self-deceiving at once. That shift from one stated effect to layered meaning is the Level 4 to Level 6 climb.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why A:** Naming more techniques is feature-spotting, not exploration.
    * **Why C:** Length is not the difference — depth of opened-up meaning is.
    * **Why D:** Quotations are still needed; exploration works through precise references.
17. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Level 6 Heading\]**
    * **Question:** The AQA Level 6 (26–30) column is headed "Convincing, critical analysis and \[BLANK\]".
    * **Answer:** Exploration
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The Level 6 heading is "Convincing, critical analysis and exploration". Level 5 is "Thoughtful, developed consideration"; Level 4 is "Clear understanding".
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **WhyWrong:** "Evaluation" or "analysis" name skills inside the band, but the exact Level 6 heading word is "exploration".
18. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Band Boundary\]**
    * **Question:** A Romeo and Juliet response shows clear understanding and explains the effects of Shakespeare's methods, but does not examine or explore them. Which band does it most likely reach?
    * **Options:** A) Level 6 (26–30), B) Level 5 (21–25), C) Level 4 (16–20), D) Level 3 (11–15).
    * **Correct:** C
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Clear explanation" of effects is Level 4 (16–20). Level 5 (21–25) needs "examination"; Level 6 (26–30) needs "exploration". The ladder is identify → explain → examine → explore.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why A:** Level 6 demands exploration of effects, not just clear explanation of them.
    * **Why B:** Level 5 requires examination — thoughtful, developed analysis — whereas this response only explains.
    * **Why D:** Level 3 sits below clear understanding; this response already shows clear understanding and explains effects, which lifts it above that band.
19. **Type: True/False \[Tests Whole-Text Focus\]**
    * **Question:** True or False: AQA's Level 6 AO1 descriptor asks for a "response to task and whole text", not analysis confined to the printed extract.
    * **Answer:** True
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The descriptor names "task and whole text". Use the extract for close AO2 work on lines like "These violent delights have violent ends", then range across the whole play to build the AO1 argument.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **WhyWrong:** It can feel safer to stay inside the printed extract, but the Level 6 descriptor explicitly demands engagement with the whole play, not the extract alone.
20. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests Level 6 AO1\]**
    * **Question:** Which features push an AQA Romeo and Juliet response to Level 6 AO1? (Select all that apply)
    * **Options:** A) Judicious micro-quotations, B) Treating characters as constructs (Juliet as a vehicle for autonomy against inherited authority), C) Retelling the plot in sequence, D) A conceptualised thesis in the introduction.
    * **Correct:** A, B, D

    * **AO:** AO1

23. **Type: Ranking \[Tests AO1\]**
    * **Question:** Rank these four openings to a Romeo and Juliet essay on the feud from WEAKEST to STRONGEST by AQA AO1 Level (type the letters in order, weakest first).
    * **Options:** A) "Shakespeare uses the lovers' deaths to explore how a rigid inherited order destroys the very lives it claims to protect.", B) "Romeo and Juliet is a play about two young people who fall in love and then die.", C) "The feud between the two families is a bad thing, and it leads to a lot of sadness in the play.", D) "Shakespeare presents the feud as destructive through the deaths of the lovers, making the audience feel how dangerous inherited hatred can be."
    * **Correct:** B, C, D, A
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Each rung climbs one AQA band. Retelling the plot only is Level 1 (narrative and/or descriptive). The value-judgement that the feud is "a bad thing" causing sadness, with no method and no evidence, is Level 2. The clear response naming a method (the deaths) and its effect on the audience is Level 4. The conceptualised opening, arguing an idea from the first line, is Level 6.
    * **WhyWrong:** Weakest to strongest runs from plot only, to a value-judgement with no method, to an explained method-and-effect, to a conceptualised argument. Each rung is exactly one AQA Level higher.
