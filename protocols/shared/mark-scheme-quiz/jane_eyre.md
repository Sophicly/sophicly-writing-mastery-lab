# **GCSE English Literature Mark Scheme Mastery Quiz: AQA Jane Eyre (per-text) v1.0**

Per-TEXT mark-scheme-quiz bank for the **AQA 8702** 19th-century novel *Jane Eyre* (Paper 1 Section B).
Served by `SWML_Quiz_Bank::pick_session()` via the canonical slug ladder (`jane_eyre.md`) — this bank
REPLACES the generic `19th_century.md` for Jane Eyre lessons so every worked example comes from *Jane
Eyre* (Jane, Rochester, Bertha Mason, St John Rivers, Thornfield), never another novel.

**Mark-scheme facts are identical to the shared AQA 19th-century-novel grid** (30 marks: AO1 12 /
AO2 12 / AO3 6; six levels of five marks each; NO AO4 on the novel — AO4 technical accuracy is
assessed only on the Shakespeare and modern-prose responses) — reused unchanged from the vetted AQA
assessment bank. Only the EXAMPLES are scoped to Jane Eyre.

Parsed deterministically (keys + feedback stripped before reaching the client). All questions worth
2 marks each.

### **SECTION A: AQA (8702 — Jane Eyre)**

1. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO Weighting\]**
   * **Question:** How is the AQA GCSE 19th-century novel question (Jane Eyre) marked?
   * **Options:** A) AO1=12, AO2=12, AO3=6 (total 30), no AO4, B) AO2 only, C) AO4 SPaG across the whole essay, D) AO1=10, AO2=10, AO3=10.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The AQA 19th-century novel response is marked out of 30 across six levels: 12 for argument (AO1), 12 for methods (AO2), 6 for context (AO3). There is no AO4 on the novel — AO4 (technical accuracy) is assessed only on the Shakespeare and modern-prose responses.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why B:** Method analysis matters greatly, but believing AO2 is marked alone ignores the equal 12 marks for argument and the 6 for context.
   * **Why C:** SPaG is assessed on some Literature questions, which makes this tempting, but AO4 is not assessed on the 19th-century novel at all.
   * **Why D:** An even split feels fair, yet context carries HALF the weight of argument and analysis (6 against 12 apiece), not a third.
2. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Level 6 AO1\]**
   * **Question:** AQA Level 6 AO1 rewards a "critical, exploratory, \[BLANK\] response to task and whole text" — one that treats Jane as a vehicle for an idea, not a life to narrate.
   * **Answer:** Conceptualised
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The exact wording is "critical, exploratory, conceptualised response to task and whole text." Treating the novel as the vehicle for a concept — an individual conscience refusing to surrender its integrity to either passion or social constraint — is what lifts AO1 into the top band.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Guesses like "detailed" or "thoughtful" name lower bands — "thoughtful" is the Level 5 keyword, whereas the decisive Level 6 word is conceptualised.
3. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO3 Integration\]**
   * **Question:** Which is a Level 6 "integrated" use of context on Jane Eyre?
   * **Options:** A) "Jane Eyre was published in 1847.", B) "The precarious status of the Victorian governess — genteel yet dependent, placed neither among the servants nor the family — makes Jane's insistence on moral self-ownership a response to a society that offers her no secure place.", C) "Victorian people cared a lot about class and money.", D) "Brontë lived in the 19th century."
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA Level 6 AO3 is "exploration of ideas/perspectives/contextual factors shown by specific, detailed links between context/text/task." A free-standing date is bolt-on; the governess's ambiguous social rank DRIVES Brontë's concept and Jane's refusals.
   * **AO:** AO3
   * **Why A:** A publication date feels like solid context, but a fact bolted onto nothing explains no authorial choice — the classic bottom-band add-on.
   * **Why C:** A general statement about Victorian class describes the era rather than the text; integration means context drives a specific idea or method.
   * **Why D:** Naming the century is vaguer still — it gestures at history without linking any contextual pressure to Brontë's choices about Jane.
4. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests Top-Band AO1\]**
   * **Question:** Which features push an AQA Jane Eyre response to Level 6 AO1? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) Judicious use of precise references (e.g. the single phrase "no net ensnares me"), B) Treating Jane as a construct Brontë builds to carry ideas about integrity, C) A conceptualised thesis argued from the introduction, D) Retelling the plot in sequence from Gateshead to the wedding.
   * **Correct:** A, B, C
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** Judicious micro-quotations, construct-level thinking and a conceptualised thesis all define Level 6 AO1. Retelling the plot shows knowledge of events only, capping the response at the lower bands.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why D:** Narrating events from Gateshead to the wedding feels like proving you know the novel, but examiners reward conceptualised argument, not narrative summary.
5. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO3 Chain\]**
   * **Question:** True or False: For AQA, context on Jane Eyre should be woven into the argument — via "specific, detailed links between context/text/task" — rather than saved for a standalone history paragraph.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA rewards context as a driver, not a bolt-on. Never write a separate history paragraph — link the governess's placelessness to why Jane insists on owning herself morally, so context, text and task connect.
   * **AO:** AO3
   * **WhyWrong:** Choosing False usually comes from a KS3 habit of a separate "context paragraph" — that bolt-on caps your AO3 because it never links context to the writer's choices.
6. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO2 Descriptor Ladder\]**
   * **Question:** A paragraph reads: "Brontë uses the caged-bird metaphor. This is a technique. It tells us Jane wants freedom." For AO2, which AQA Level fits, and what is the ceiling?
   * **Options:** A) Level 6 — analysis with exploration of the effects, B) Level 5 — examination of how the method works, C) Level 3 — identification of the method with only a general comment on effect, D) Level 4 — clear explanation of the effect.
   * **Correct:** C
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Naming the caged-bird metaphor and adding "it tells us she wants freedom" is AQA Level 3 AO2: "identification of the writer's methods" with only a general comment. Level 4 needs a clear explanation of the effect; Level 5 "examination"; Level 6 "analysis... exploration of effects of the writer's methods" with terminology used judiciously.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why A:** There is no analysis and no exploration of layered effects — the top band is out of reach here.
   * **Why B:** "Examination" (Level 5) requires sustained working-through of how the method makes meaning, which is absent.
   * **Why D:** "Clear explanation" (Level 4) needs the effect explained, not merely asserted with "it tells us".
7. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Level 6 AO2\]**
   * **Question:** Complete the AQA Level 6 AO2 descriptor: "Analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used \[BLANK\]."
   * **Answer:** Judiciously
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA Level 6 AO2 is "analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously." "Judiciously" warns against feature-spotting: terminology earns marks only when it is selected to serve the analysis of effect.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **WhyWrong:** The AQA word is "judiciously" — terminology used to serve analysis of effect, not scattered to impress.
8. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests Level 6 AO2\]**
   * **Question:** Which are true of an AQA Level 6 AO2 response on Jane Eyre? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) It moves from identifying the red-room imagery to analysing how it creates meaning, B) It explores several effects the method has on the reader, C) It uses subject terminology judiciously, only where it serves the analysis, D) It names as many techniques as possible to demonstrate range.
   * **Correct:** A, B, C
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** AQA Level 6 AO2 is "analysis of the writer's methods... exploration of effects of the writer's methods... subject terminology used judiciously." Analysis, exploration of effects and judicious terminology are all top-band. Maximising technique-count is feature-spotting, which the mark scheme penalises.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why D:** Naming many techniques is feature-spotting — AQA rewards judicious selection and exploration of effect, not quantity.
9. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO4 Anomaly\]**
   * **Question:** True or False: The AQA 19th-century novel question (Jane Eyre) awards AO4 marks for accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar.
   * **Answer:** False
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. There is no AO4 on the 19th-century novel. AO4 (technical accuracy) is assessed only on the Shakespeare and modern-prose responses. Jane Eyre is marked on AO1, AO2 and AO3 alone.
   * **AO:** AO3
   * **WhyWrong:** Answering True carries the SPaG habit across from other papers — but the novel question has no AO4 strand, so chasing phantom SPaG marks wastes effort better spent on context.
10. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Evaluative Vocabulary\]**
    * **Question:** Which verb signals Level 6 critical evaluation on Jane Eyre?
    * **Options:** A) "Shows", B) "Says", C) "Weaponises" (e.g. "Brontë weaponises the red-room's blood-tinted walls so the child's terror begins to manufacture the ghost she dreads"), D) "Writes about".
    * **Correct:** C
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Evaluative verbs ("weaponises", "drains", "inverts") convert description into critical judgement — the Level 6 voice. The word earns credit only when it fronts a judgement about HOW the method works on the reader; dropped in alone it is an empty label.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why A:** "Shows" points at the text without judging how the method works on the reader — description, not evaluation.
    * **Why B:** "Says" treats the novel as plain statement, reporting content rather than evaluating Brontë's crafted choices.
    * **Why D:** "Writes about" is topic-labelling — it names the subject with no judgement of method or effect.
11. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Number of Levels\]**
    * **Question:** Across how many levels is the AQA 19th-century novel question marked?
    * **Options:** A) Four levels, B) Five levels, C) Six levels, D) Nine levels.
    * **Correct:** C
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA marks this 30-mark question across six levels of five marks each — Level 1 (1–5) up to Level 6 (26–30) — each carrying banded AO1, AO2 and AO3 descriptors.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Four levels matches some shorter mark schemes, but the AQA novel question is banded into six.
    * **Why B:** Five levels is the Edexcel pattern; AQA's 30-mark grid runs to six.
    * **Why D:** Nine is the GCSE grade scale, not the number of marking levels.

13. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Band Boundary\]**
    * **Question:** Which name and mark range is AQA Level 4 on the novel?
    * **Options:** A) "Clear understanding" (16–20), B) "Thoughtful, developed response" (21–25), C) "Convincing, critical analysis and exploration" (26–30), D) "Supported, relevant comments" (6–10).
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Level 4 is "Clear understanding" (16–20) — a clear, explained, sustained response. Level 5 "Thoughtful, developed response" (21–25) sits above and Level 2 "Supported, relevant comments" (6–10) below.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why B:** "Thoughtful, developed response" is Level 5 (21–25), one band higher.
    * **Why C:** "Convincing, critical analysis and exploration" is the top band, Level 6 (26–30).

15. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO3 Wording\]**
    * **Question:** For AQA AO3 (6 marks) on Jane Eyre, what does the top band actually credit?
    * **Options:** A) "Exploration of ideas/perspectives/contextual factors shown by specific, detailed links between context/text/task", B) Accurate spelling and punctuation, C) A memorised biography of Brontë placed before the analysis, D) Naming the novel's publication date.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA AO3 rewards contextual understanding shown through "specific, detailed links between context/text/task" — never a bolt-on history paragraph or a free-standing fact.
    * **AO:** AO3
    * **Why B:** Spelling and punctuation is AO4, which is not assessed on the 19th-century novel.
    * **Why C:** A memorised biography is exactly the bolt-on background the band rejects; context must be tied to text and task.
    * **Why D:** A date is a free-standing fact that explains no authorial choice — the band demands linked context, not information.

18. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Judicious Vocabulary\]**
    * **Question:** Two students support the idea that Jane refuses to be possessed. One quotes six lines. The other selects the single phrase "no net ensnares me" to pin the point. Whose reference is "judicious" — and what does "judicious" mean?
    * **Options:** A) The first — judicious means marshalling as much supporting evidence as possible, B) The second — judicious means a well-judged, selective reference chosen to do the most argumentative work, C) Both equally — judicious simply means quoting the text accurately and in full, D) Neither — judicious means choosing the longest, most detailed quotation available.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Judicious" means well-judged and selective — the reference is chosen because it does real work, not because it is long or abundant. A precise half-line outperforms six general ones. This is why AQA Level 6 says "judicious use of precise references".
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Judicious is about wise selection, not quantity — more can mean less precise.
    * **Why C:** Accuracy alone is not judiciousness; the CHOICE must serve the argument.
    * **Why D:** Length is irrelevant; aptness to the point is what counts.

    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Level 5 is "Thoughtful, developed response" — examination of methods with apt references integrated into interpretation, sitting just below the conceptualised exploration of Level 6.

20. **Type: Ranking \[Tests AO1\]**
    * **Question:** Rank these four openings to a Jane Eyre essay from WEAKEST to STRONGEST by AQA AO1 Level (type the letters in order, weakest first).
    * **Options:** A) "Brontë constructs Jane's integrity as a moral will that refuses to be owned, using the whole novel to interrogate how a woman without rank preserves an inviolable self.", B) "Jane is an orphan who suffers at Gateshead and Lowood, becomes a governess, and marries Rochester at the end.", C) "Independence is a good thing in the novel, and it helps Jane in the end.", D) "Brontë presents Jane's independence as unbreakable through her refusal of Rochester, making the reader admire her self-command."
    * **Correct:** B, C, D, A
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Each rung is one clear AQA band higher. The plot-summary rung, narrating events only, is Level 1 (simple comment). The bare value-judgement about independence being good, with no method and no evidence, is Level 2 (supported comment). The clear, explained rung that names a method (the refusal) and its effect on the reader is Level 4 (clear understanding). The critical, conceptualised rung, arguing an idea across the whole novel, is Level 6. The ladder is plot → comment → explained method-and-effect → concept.
    * **WhyWrong:** Weakest to strongest runs from plot narration, to a value-judgement with no method, to an explained method plus reader effect, to a conceptualised argument. Each rung is exactly one AQA Level higher; the jump to Level 6 is the move from explaining events to arguing a concept.
21. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Extract vs Whole Text\]**
    * **Question:** On the AQA Jane Eyre question the printed extract is given alongside the whole-text task. What is the top-band way to use it?
    * **Options:** A) Spend the whole answer on the extract and mention the rest of the novel in the conclusion, B) Ignore the extract and write from memory, C) Use the extract as an evidence bank for close AO2 analysis, then move out to the whole novel for AO1 argument and arc, D) Copy long stretches of the extract to prove it has been read.
    * **Correct:** C
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The extract is the richest source of close language evidence (AO2), but the top bands demand a conceptualised whole-text argument (AO1). Moving fluently from the printed passage out to the arc of the whole novel is what secures the top Level.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Staying inside the extract feels safe, but never moving beyond it cannot prove whole-text understanding.
    * **Why B:** Ignoring the extract discards the best material for detailed language analysis.
    * **Why D:** Copying long stretches shows transcription, not thinking — the extract is for analysing choices, not reproducing.
22. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Conceptualised Response\]**
    * **Question:** AQA Level 6 AO1 rewards a "conceptualised" reading of Jane Eyre. A conceptualised response is one that...
    * **Options:** A) uses difficult, complicated vocabulary to signal sophistication, B) is organised around an idea — e.g. reading the novel as an exploration of a conscience that keeps its integrity against passion and social constraint — treating character and plot as evidence for that idea, C) packs in as much Victorian historical context as it can, D) is very long and dense with closely-detailed observation.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Conceptualised" means built around a concept — reading Jane's refusals of Rochester and of St John Rivers as evidence for a single idea about integrity, not a set of events to retell. It is about organising ideas, not complexity, length or context-stuffing.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Hard vocabulary is not a concept — conceptualised is about the idea, not the wording.
    * **Why C:** Context supports AO3; it does not make a response conceptualised.
    * **Why D:** Length is not conceptualisation — a short response can be conceptualised.
