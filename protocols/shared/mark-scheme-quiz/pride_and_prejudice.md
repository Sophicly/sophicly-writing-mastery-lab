# **GCSE English Literature Mark Scheme Mastery Quiz: AQA Pride and Prejudice (per-text) v1.0**

Per-TEXT mark-scheme-quiz bank for *Pride and Prejudice* on the **AQA 8702**
19th-century novel question (Paper 1 Section B). Served by `SWML_Quiz_Bank::pick_session()` via the
canonical slug ladder (`pride_and_prejudice.md`) — this bank REPLACES the generic `19th_century.md` for
Pride and Prejudice lessons so every worked example comes from Austen's novel (never any other set
text).

**Mark-scheme facts are identical to the shared AQA 19th-century novel grid** (30 marks: AO1 12 /
AO2 12 / AO3 6; six levels of five marks each; verbatim AQA band descriptors; NO AO4/SPaG on the
novel) — reused unchanged from the vetted Pride and Prejudice mark-scheme-assessment bank. Only the
EXAMPLES are scoped to Austen's novel: Elizabeth, Darcy, Wickham, Charlotte Lucas, Mr Collins;
pride and prejudice as twin failures of judgement; "In vain I have struggled", "a countenance ...
of pleasing address"; the entail and the marriage market.

Parsed deterministically (keys + feedback stripped before reaching the client). All questions worth
2 marks each.

### **SECTION A: AQA (8702 — Pride and Prejudice)**

1. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO Weighting\]**
   * **Question:** How is the AQA 19th-century novel question (Paper 1 Section B) marked?
   * **Options:** A) AO1=12, AO2=12, AO3=6 for a 30-mark total, with no AO4, B) AO2 only, C) AO3 carries the most marks because it is a novel of manners, D) A single 40-mark question with AO4 SPaG.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The AQA novel response is marked out of 30 across six levels of five marks each — AO1 and AO2 weighted equally at 12 marks each and AO3 (context) at 6, half their weight. There is no AO4 on the novel; SPaG is assessed only on the Shakespeare and modern texts.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why B:** Analysis of Austen's methods matters greatly, but believing AO2 is marked alone ignores the equal 12 marks for argument (AO1) and the 6 for context (AO3).
   * **Why C:** Context feels central to a novel of manners, yet AO3 is the lightest strand at just 6 of the 30 marks — half the weight of argument or methods.
   * **Why D:** SPaG is assessed on the Shakespeare and modern-text questions, which makes this tempting, but AO4 does not apply to the 19th-century novel.
2. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO1 Level 6\]**
   * **Question:** AQA's Level 6 AO1 descriptor requires a "critical, exploratory, \[BLANK\] response to task and whole text" — reading Pride and Prejudice as a vehicle for an idea, not a courtship to retell.
   * **Answer:** Conceptualised
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The exact Level 6 wording is "critical, exploratory, conceptualised response to task and whole text." Treating the novel as a construct exploring first impressions as a failure of judgement the marriage plot exists to correct is what lifts AO1 into the top band.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Guesses like "detailed" or "thoughtful" describe lower bands — "thoughtful" is the Level 5 keyword, whereas the Level 6 decisive word is conceptualised.
3. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO3 Integration\]**
   * **Question:** Which sentence is a Level 6 "integrated" use of context for AQA?
   * **Options:** A) "Austen published the novel in 1813.", B) "Austen draws on the entail, which cuts Longbourn away from the Bennet daughters, to make marriage an economic necessity — the pressure that drives her satire of a marriage market ordering human worth by rank and fortune.", C) "The novel is set in Regency England.", D) "People in the Regency period cared a lot about marriage."
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Level 1 is a bolt-on fact; Level 6 AO3 is "exploration of ideas/perspectives/contextual factors shown by specific, detailed links between context/text/task." Here the entail makes marriage an economic necessity for the Bennet sisters, which drives the concept (judgement distorted by rank and fortune), which drives Austen's satire of the marriage market.
   * **AO:** AO3
   * **Why A:** A publication date feels like solid context, but a fact bolted onto nothing explains no authorial choice — the classic Level 1 add-on.
   * **Why C:** Naming the setting gestures at period without connecting any contextual idea to Austen's method or concept.
   * **Why D:** A general statement about Regency attitudes describes the era rather than the text — integration means context that drives a specific choice.
4. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests AO1 Quality\]**
   * **Question:** Which features signal a top-band AQA AO1 response on Pride and Prejudice? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) Judicious micro-quotations such as "In vain I have struggled", B) Treating Elizabeth and Darcy as constructs exploring pride and prejudice as twin failures of judgement, C) Retelling the plot from the refused proposal through the letter to the marriage in sequence, D) A conceptualised thesis in the introduction.
   * **Correct:** A, B, D
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, D. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** Judicious micro-quotes, construct-level thinking, and a conceptual thesis all push into Level 6 — "judicious use of precise references to support interpretations." Plot retelling (dislike → proposal → letter → Pemberley → marriage) caps you at the lower bands.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why C:** Retelling the plot feels like proving you know the novel, but it only shows knowledge of events — AQA rewards conceptualised argument, not narrative summary.
5. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO3 Chain\]**
   * **Question:** True or False: For AQA, context about the entail and the marriage market should be integrated into your analysis rather than saved for a standalone history paragraph.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA rewards context as a "driver", not a "bolt-on" — Level 6 AO3 wants "specific, detailed links between context/text/task." Weave why Charlotte accepts Mr Collins and why the entail pressures the Bennet sisters (marriage as economic necessity) into the analysis; never write a separate history paragraph.
   * **AO:** AO3
   * **WhyWrong:** Choosing False usually comes from a KS3 "context paragraph" habit — but a bolt-on caps your AO3 marks because it never links context to Austen's choices.
6. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO2 Effects\]**
   * **Question:** A paragraph reads: "Austen uses irony in the opening sentence. This is a technique. It tells us the novel is about marriage." For AO2, which AQA Level does this reach, and why?
   * **Options:** A) Level 6 — analysis with exploration of the effects, B) Level 5 — examination of how the method works, C) Level 3 — identification of the method with only a general comment on effect, D) Level 4 — clear explanation of the effect.
   * **Correct:** C
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Naming the irony and adding "it tells us the novel is about marriage" is AQA Level 3 AO2 — a general comment on the method. Level 6 wants "analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously" and "exploration of effects", reading the mock-maxim as exposing the economics of the marriage market.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why A:** There is no analysis and no exploration of layered effects here, so the top band is out of reach.
   * **Why B:** Examination requires sustained working-through of how the irony makes meaning, which is absent.
   * **Why D:** Clear explanation needs the effect explained, not merely asserted as "about marriage".
7. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO1 References\]**
   * **Question:** AQA's AO1 descriptor requires "\[BLANK\] use of precise references" — short, well-judged quotations such as "In vain I have struggled" embedded in your sentence.
   * **Answer:** Judicious
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Judicious" means well-judged and precise. The single phrase "In vain I have struggled", chosen to pin an argument that pride corrupts even Darcy's declaration of love, outperforms six general references.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Students often guess "frequent" or "accurate" — but the descriptor's keyword is judicious, because choosing the right micro-quotation matters more than quoting often.
8. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Level 6 Name\]**
   * **Question:** In the AQA mark scheme, what is the name of Level 6 (26–30 marks)?
   * **Options:** A) Clear understanding, B) Convincing, critical analysis and exploration, C) Thoughtful, developed response, D) Simple, explicit comments.
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA names Level 6 "Convincing, critical analysis and exploration" — a critical, exploratory, conceptualised response to task and whole text. "Exploration" is the top-band verb, above the "thoughtful, developed response" of Level 5.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why A:** "Clear understanding" is Level 4 (16–20) — clear and explained, two bands below the critical exploration of the top.
   * **Why C:** "Thoughtful, developed response" is Level 5 (21–25), one band below Level 6.
   * **Why D:** "Simple, explicit comments" is the bottom band, Level 1 (1–5) — narrative and descriptive rather than critical.

11. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO2 Descriptor Ladder\]**
    * **Question:** Moving up the AQA levels for AO2, the demand climbs from "identification of the writer's methods". Which phrasing marks the Level 6 top?
    * **Options:** A) Awareness of the writer making choices, B) Analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously, C) Explained/relevant comments on the writer's methods, D) Clear explanation of the writer's methods.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Level 6 AO2 asks for "analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously" plus "exploration of effects". The verbs climb: awareness, identification, comment, explanation, and finally analysis and exploration.

13. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Conceptualised\]**
    * **Question:** AQA Level 6 AO1 rewards a "conceptualised" response. A conceptualised reading of Pride and Prejudice is one that...
    * **Options:** A) uses difficult, complicated vocabulary to signal sophistication, B) is organised around a concept — pride and prejudice as twin failures of judgement that the marriage plot exists to correct — treating Elizabeth, Darcy and the plot as evidence for that idea, C) packs in as much Regency historical context as it can, D) is very long and dense with detail.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Conceptualised" means built around a concept — reading the novel as an exploration of pride and prejudice as twin failures of judgement, with the characters and events as evidence. It is about organising ideas, not complexity, length or context-stuffing.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Hard vocabulary is not a concept — conceptualised is about the idea, not the wording.
    * **Why C:** Context supports AO3; it does not make a response conceptualised.
    * **Why D:** Length is not conceptualisation — a short response can be conceptualised.
14. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Judicious\]**
    * **Question:** Two students support the idea that Elizabeth misjudges Wickham. One quotes six lines of his account of Darcy; the other selects the single phrase "a countenance ... of pleasing address". Whose reference is "judicious" — and what does "judicious" mean?
    * **Options:** A) The first — judicious means marshalling as much supporting evidence as possible, B) The second — judicious means a well-judged, selective reference chosen to do the most argumentative work, C) Both equally — judicious just means quoting accurately, D) Neither — judicious means choosing the longest quotation available.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Judicious" means well-judged and selective — chosen because it does real work, not because it is long or abundant. The precise phrase "a countenance ... of pleasing address" outperforms six general lines. This is why AQA Level 6 says "judicious use of precise references".
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Judicious is about wise selection, not quantity — more can mean less precise.
    * **Why C:** Accuracy alone is not judiciousness; the CHOICE must serve the argument.
    * **Why D:** Length is irrelevant; aptness to the point is what counts.

16. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Vocabulary Critical\]**
    * **Question:** AQA asks for a "critical" style. In a mark scheme, what does "critical" actually mean?
    * **Options:** A) Pointing out the novel's faults and what Austen did badly, B) Forming an evaluative, argued judgement and taking a position on interpretation, C) Writing in a consistently serious, formal tone, D) Disagreeing with other readers' interpretations.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. In a mark scheme "critical" does NOT mean fault-finding. It means evaluative and argued — you weigh interpretations and commit to a position. AQA pairs it as "critical, exploratory": an argued reading that opens up meaning.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** This is the everyday sense of "critical" (negative), not the assessment sense.
    * **Why C:** Tone is not what "critical" rewards — an argued judgement is.
    * **Why D:** Critical means evaluating the text, not other students.

20. **Type: Ranking \[Tests AO1\]**
    * **Question:** Rank these four openings on Pride and Prejudice from WEAKEST to STRONGEST by AQA AO1 Level (type the letters in order, weakest first).
    * **Options:** A) "Austen constructs pride and prejudice as twin failures of judgement that the marriage plot exists to correct, using Elizabeth's education of feeling to interrogate how a society orders human worth by rank and fortune.", B) "Elizabeth dislikes Mr Darcy, then changes her mind, and in the end they marry.", C) "Pride is a bad thing in the novel, and it causes a lot of misunderstanding and unhappiness.", D) "Austen presents pride as an obstacle through Darcy's first proposal, making the reader judge how far social rank distorts his sense of worth."
    * **Correct:** B, C, D, A
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Each rung is one clear AQA band higher. The plot-summary rung, narrating events only, is Level 1 (simple comment). The bare value-judgement about a bad thing causing unhappiness, with no method and no evidence, is Level 2 (supported comment). The clear rung that names a method — the first proposal — and its effect on the reader is Level 4 (clear understanding). The critical, conceptualised rung, arguing an idea from the first line, is Level 6.
    * **WhyWrong:** Weakest to strongest runs from plot narration, to a value-judgement with no method, to an explained method plus reader effect, to a conceptualised argument. Each rung is exactly one AQA Level higher.

23. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests AO2 Methods\]**
    * **Question:** Which count as valid AO2 writer's methods to analyse in Pride and Prejudice? (Select all that apply)
    * **Options:** A) Specific word choices and their connotations (such as the counterfeit "truth" of the opening maxim), B) Form (free indirect discourse blurring the narrator's voice with Elizabeth's), C) Structure (the placement of the first proposal, the arrival of Darcy's letter), D) The biography of Jane Austen.
    * **Correct:** A, B, C
    * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
    * **Feedback:** Word-level choice, form and structure are all AO2 methods — the deliberate choices Austen made on the page. But a method only counts as AO2 once you pair it with its effect on the reader; naming "free indirect discourse" or "irony" alone is feature-spotting. The author's biography sits outside the text and earns nothing.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why D:** A writer's biography is background about the author, not a method within the text; AO2 analyses language, form and structure.
