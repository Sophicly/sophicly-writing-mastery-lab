# **GCSE English Literature Mark Scheme Mastery Quiz: OCR Love (per-cluster) v1.0**

Per-CLUSTER mark-scheme-quiz bank for the **OCR GCSE (J352) Poetry Across Time — Love** cluster
(from *Towards a World Unknown*). Served by `SWML_Quiz_Bank::pick_session()` via the canonical slug
ladder (`love.md`) — this bank REPLACES the generic `poetry_anthology.md` for Love lessons so every
worked example comes from a Love-cluster poem (never a Power & Conflict / Belonging / other-board
anthology poem).

**Mark-scheme facts follow the OCR (J352) Poetry Across Time scheme** — Part (a) (the comparison of
the two printed poems) is AO2-dominant with AO1 secondary, marked out of 20; Part (b) ("Explore in
detail one other poem from your anthology") weights AO1 and AO2 equally, also out of 20. AO3
(context) is not assessed on either part and AO4 (SPaG) plays no part. The response is marked
holistically across six levels, Level 1 (1–3) up to Level 6 (18–20); Level 6 demands a "sustained
critical style in an informed personal response" with "a sustained, interwoven comparison of texts".
This is the EASIER drill — the harder examiner-level judging lives in the matching
mark-scheme-ASSESSMENT bank. Only the poem EXAMPLES are scoped to the Love-cluster poems:
Bright Star · Now · The Perseverance · Warming Her Pearls · Poem for My Love · Looking at Your
Hands · Love After Love · Dusting the Phone · Flirtation · Lullaby · In Paris With You ·
I Wouldn't Thank You for a Valentine · Morning Song.

Parsed deterministically (keys + feedback stripped before reaching the client). All questions worth 2
marks each.

### **SECTION A: OCR (J352 — Love)**

1. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Part (a) AO Weighting\]**
   * **Question:** For OCR (J352) Part (a) — the comparison of the two printed poems — how are the marks weighted?
   * **Options:** A) AO2 dominant with AO1 secondary, out of a total of 20, B) AO1 only, C) AO3 dominant, with context paragraphs carrying most marks, D) AO4 (SPaG) forms one of the scored criteria.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. OCR Part (a) is AO2-dominant with AO1 secondary, marked out of 20 — most credit rewards methods analysis of both printed poems, underpinned by a conceptual argument.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why B:** The comparison feels like pure argument, but AO1 is only the secondary strand in Part (a) — the dominant weighting rewards methods analysis of both poems.
   * **Why C:** Context feels like a natural part of poetry essays, but AO3 is not assessed in OCR Part (a) at all.
   * **Why D:** SPaG tempts because it appears elsewhere in Literature, but AO4 plays no part in this poetry question.
2. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Part (b) AO Weighting\]**
   * **Question:** For OCR Part (b) — exploring one other single poem from your anthology, such as Walcott's Love After Love — how are the marks weighted?
   * **Options:** A) AO1 and AO2 weighted equally, out of a total of 20, B) AO1 only, C) AO2 only, carried over from Part (a), D) AO3 only.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Part (b) balances AO1 (argument) and AO2 (methods) equally, out of 20. Both must be strong.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why B:** A single-poem exploration feels like pure argument, but Part (b) credits argument and methods equally — analysis of language, form and structure carries half the marks.
   * **Why C:** Methods dominate Part (a), so carrying that weighting across is tempting, but in Part (b) AO2 shares the marks equally with AO1.
   * **Why D:** Context seems a natural addition to any poetry essay, yet AO3 is not assessed in Part (b) either.
3. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO Distinction\]**
   * **Question:** True or False: OCR Part (a) and Part (b) assess the same AO weightings.
   * **Answer:** False
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Part (a) = AO2 dominant + AO1 secondary. Part (b) = AO1 + AO2 equal. They are different tasks with different weightings — plan accordingly.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Answering True assumes one rubric covers the whole question, but the parts differ — the Part (a) comparison is methods-dominant while the Part (b) single-poem exploration balances argument and methods equally.
4. **Type: True/False \[Tests Part (b) Task\]**
   * **Question:** True or False: OCR Part (b) asks you to explore ONE other poem from your anthology — such as Plath's Morning Song — and does not require a two-poem comparison.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Part (b) is a single-poem exploration ("Explore in detail one other poem from your anthology"); only Part (a) requires comparison.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Answering False confuses the parts — comparison lives in Part (a); Part (b) explores one poem in depth.
5. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Part (a) Focus\]**
   * **Question:** In OCR Part (a), where should most of your analytical energy go when comparing the two printed poems?
   * **Options:** A) Plot summary of each poem, B) Context paragraphs on each poet's era, C) Close analysis of language, form and structure — AO2 is the dominant assessment objective, D) Biography of each poet.
   * **Correct:** C
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Part (a) names AO2 the dominant objective — close, word-level methods analysis of both poems beats any other strategy.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why A:** Retelling the poems feels safe, but plot summary demonstrates comprehension only and earns very little on a methods-dominant task.
   * **Why B:** Context paragraphs feel scholarly, yet AO3 is not assessed in Part (a) — time spent there earns no marks.
   * **Why D:** The poet's life seems illuminating, but biography is neither a method nor an assessed objective on this question.
6. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Unassessed Objectives\]**
   * **Question:** Which of the following earns NO marks anywhere on OCR Poetry across time?

   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AO3 (context) is not assessed in Part (a) or Part (b), and AO4 (SPaG) plays no part in this poetry question — analytical time belongs to argument (AO1) and methods (AO2).
   * **AO:** Vocabulary
   * **Why B:** AO2 is the dominant objective in Part (a) and half the marks in Part (b).
   * **Why C:** AO1 is the secondary strand in Part (a) and half the marks in Part (b).
   * **Why D:** Comparing the two printed poems is the very task Part (a) is built on.
7. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Holistic Marking\]**

   * **Correct:** A

11. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests Interwoven — Grade 9 separator\]**

18. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Sustained Comparison\]**
    * **Question:** A Part (a) response analyses Duffy's Warming Her Pearls in full for six sentences, then tacks the other printed poem on in a final sentence. Why does this cap below Level 6?
    * **Options:** A) Level 6 requires "a sustained, interwoven comparison of texts" — both poems woven together throughout, not block treatment, B) The paragraph is too short, C) Warming Her Pearls should always be analysed second, D) Six sentences exceed the limit allowed for one poem.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Block treatment — one poem in full, then the other — caps you lower; the top band achieves a sustained, interwoven comparison, the two poems woven together throughout the response.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why B:** Length is not the fault; the block structure is.
    * **Why C:** No rule fixes the order of the poems; the fault is treating them separately at all.
    * **Why D:** There is no sentence limit per poem — the missing element is the interwoven comparison itself.

22. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Methods and Effects Comparison\]**
    * **Question:** In an interwoven Part (a) paragraph on Warming Her Pearls and In Paris With You, what must the weaving actually carry to reach Level 6?
    * **Options:** A) A comparison of the two poets' METHODS and their DIFFERING effects on the reader — Duffy's dramatic monologue lets us overhear a desire the maid cannot declare, while Fenton's refrain brings back 'I'm in Paris with you' in place of the love his speaker will not name, B) Comparative connectives such as "similarly" and "whereas" in every sentence, C) An equal word count for each poem, D) A context sentence for each poet.
    * **Correct:** A
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The interweaving is only the mechanic — Level 6 needs it to carry a comparison of the two poets' METHODS and their DIFFERING effects on the reader; weaving and connectors alone are not AO2 comparison.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why B:** Connectives signal comparison but do not perform it — without methods and effects they are empty joinery.
    * **Why C:** Balanced coverage analyses nothing by itself; the marks follow methods and effects, not word counts.
    * **Why D:** AO3 (context) is not assessed on this question, so context sentences earn nothing here.
23. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Evaluative Vocabulary\]**
    * **Question:** Which phrase signals OCR Level 6 critical style in a response on Keats's Bright Star?
    * **Options:** A) "The poem is good", B) "Keats's single arcing sentence suspends the sonnet between the star's 'lone splendour' and the beloved's 'ripening breast', so the form itself performs the steadfastness the speaker craves", C) "This is a sonnet", D) "Keats wrote poems".
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Option B evaluates, conceptualises (form as meaning), and sustains sophisticated analysis with quotations precise, pertinent and skilfully interwoven. That's Level 6.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why A:** A verdict like "the poem is good" sounds evaluative, but it offers judgement with no method, evidence or reasoning behind it.
    * **Why C:** Naming the form is accurate terminology, yet "this is a sonnet" is identification — Level 6 needs the form's effect analysed and judged.
    * **Why D:** A bare factual statement about the poet describes nothing in the text — it is the flattest possible description, not critical style.
24. **Type: Ranking \[Tests Comparison Ladder\]**
    * **Question:** Rank these four Part (a) sentences comparing Warming Her Pearls and In Paris With You from WEAKEST to STRONGEST (type the letters in order, weakest first).
    * **Options:** A) "Both poems are about love.", B) "Both speakers hide their true feelings, and both poems use repetition.", C) "Duffy uses a dramatic monologue to voice the maid's hidden desire, whereas Fenton uses the refrain 'I'm in Paris with you' to avoid speaking of love.", D) "Where Duffy's confiding monologue lets the maid's forbidden desire surface between her dutiful words, Fenton's returning refrain answers every refusal to 'talk of love' with Paris itself — two forms engineered to carry what their speakers cannot say, so the reader overhears one love and decodes the other."
    * **Correct:** A, B, C, D
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Each rung climbs OCR's grid. A bare shared subject is "a basic response to both text and task". Naming a shared feature "Makes some explicit, relevant comparisons between texts" but analyses no effect. An explicit comparison of methods with integrated quotation reaches a convincing critical style. The top rung achieves a sustained, interwoven comparison in which both poets' methods and their differing effects on the reader are argued together — the Level 6 standard.
    * **WhyWrong:** Weakest to strongest runs from a bare shared subject, to a relevant but undeveloped comparison, to an explicit methods comparison with integrated evidence, to a sustained, interwoven comparison arguing both poets' methods and their differing effects on the reader.
