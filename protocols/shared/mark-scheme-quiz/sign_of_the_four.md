# **GCSE English Literature Mark Scheme Mastery Quiz: AQA The Sign of the Four (per-text) v1.0**

Per-TEXT mark-scheme-quiz bank for *The Sign of the Four* on the **AQA 8702**
19th-century novel question (Paper 1 Section B). Served by `SWML_Quiz_Bank::pick_session()` via the
canonical slug ladder (`sign_of_the_four.md`) — this bank REPLACES the generic `19th_century.md` for
The Sign of the Four lessons so every worked example comes from Conan Doyle's novel (never any other
set text).

**Mark-scheme facts are identical to the shared AQA 19th-century novel grid** (30 marks: AO1 12 /
AO2 12 / AO3 6; six levels of five marks each; verbatim AQA band descriptors; NO AO4/SPaG on the
novel) — reused unchanged from the vetted Sign of the Four mark-scheme-assessment bank. Only the
EXAMPLES are scoped to Conan Doyle's novel: Holmes, Watson, Mary Morstan, Jonathan Small, Tonga, the
Sholtos; rationalism confronting an unknowable imperial world; "an exact science", "dense drizzly
fog", "whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth"; the fog and the Agra treasure.

Parsed deterministically (keys + feedback stripped before reaching the client). All questions worth
2 marks each.

### **SECTION A: AQA (8702 — The Sign of Four)**

1. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO Weighting\]**
   * **Question:** How is the AQA 19th-century novel question (Paper 1 Section B) marked?
   * **Options:** A) AO1=12, AO2=12, AO3=6 for a 30-mark total, with no AO4, B) AO2 only, C) AO3 carries the most marks because empire is central to the novel, D) A single 40-mark question with AO4 SPaG.
   * **Correct:** A
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The AQA novel response is marked out of 30 across six levels of five marks each — AO1 and AO2 weighted equally at 12 marks each and AO3 (context) at 6, half their weight. There is no AO4 on the novel; SPaG is assessed only on the Shakespeare and modern texts.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why B:** Analysis of Conan Doyle's methods matters greatly, but believing AO2 is marked alone ignores the equal 12 marks for argument (AO1) and the 6 for context (AO3).
   * **Why C:** Context feels central to a novel steeped in empire, yet AO3 is the lightest strand at just 6 of the 30 marks — half the weight of argument or methods.
   * **Why D:** SPaG is assessed on the Shakespeare and modern-text questions, which makes this tempting, but AO4 does not apply to the 19th-century novel.
2. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO1 Level 6\]**
   * **Question:** AQA's Level 6 AO1 descriptor requires a "critical, exploratory, \[BLANK\] response to task and whole text" — reading The Sign of the Four as a vehicle for an idea, not a case to retell.
   * **Answer:** Conceptualised
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The exact Level 6 wording is "critical, exploratory, conceptualised response to task and whole text." Treating the novel as a construct exploring rationalism striving to master a threatening, unknowable world is what lifts AO1 into the top band.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Guesses like "detailed" or "thoughtful" describe lower bands — "thoughtful" belongs to the Level 5 "thoughtful, developed response", whereas the Level 6 decisive word is conceptualised.
3. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO3 Integration\]**
   * **Question:** Which sentence is a Level 6 "integrated" use of context for AQA?
   * **Options:** A) "The Sign of the Four was published in 1890.", B) "Conan Doyle draws on late-Victorian unease about fortunes seized from colonial India to construct the Agra treasure as morally tainted plunder — an anxiety that drives his choice to sink it irrecoverably in the Thames.", C) "The novel is set in Victorian London.", D) "Victorian people had a big empire and thought it was important."
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Level 1 is a bolt-on fact; Level 6 AO3 is "exploration of ideas/perspectives/contextual factors shown by specific, detailed links between context/text/task." Here imperial unease about colonial plunder drives the concept (such wealth can never be cleanly possessed), which drives Conan Doyle's method (the treasure drowned in the Thames).
   * **AO:** AO3
   * **Why A:** A publication date feels like solid context, but a free-standing fact bolted onto nothing explains no authorial choice — the classic Level 1 add-on.
   * **Why C:** Naming the setting gestures at period without connecting any contextual idea to Conan Doyle's method or concept.
   * **Why D:** A general statement about empire describes the era rather than the text — integration means context that drives a specific choice.
4. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests AO1 Quality\]**
   * **Question:** Which features signal a top-band AQA AO1 response on The Sign of the Four? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) Judicious micro-quotations such as "an exact science", B) Treating Holmes and the Agra treasure as constructs exploring reason and empire, C) Retelling the plot from Mary Morstan's arrival to the treasure lost in sequence, D) A conceptualised thesis in the introduction.
   * **Correct:** A, B, D
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, D. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** Judicious micro-quotes, construct-level thinking, and a conceptual thesis all push into Level 6 — "judicious use of precise references to support interpretation(s)." Plot retelling (Mary Morstan → the Sholtos → Small's confession → the treasure lost) caps you at the lower bands.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why C:** Retelling the case feels like proving you know the novel, but it only shows knowledge of events — AQA rewards conceptualised argument, not narrative summary.
5. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO3 Chain\]**
   * **Question:** True or False: For AQA, context about empire and the 1857 Indian Rebellion should be integrated into your analysis rather than saved for a standalone history paragraph.
   * **Answer:** True
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA rewards context as a "driver", not a "bolt-on" — Level 6 AO3 wants "specific, detailed links between context/text/task." Weave why Jonathan Small feels so bitterly wronged (the 1857 Indian Rebellion, colonial plunder) into the analysis; never write a separate history paragraph.
   * **AO:** AO3
   * **WhyWrong:** Choosing False usually comes from a KS3 "context paragraph" habit — but a bolt-on caps your AO3 marks because it never links context to Conan Doyle's choices.
6. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO2 Effects\]**
   * **Question:** A paragraph reads: "Conan Doyle uses pathetic fallacy — the 'dense drizzly fog' over London. This is a technique. It tells us the mood is gloomy." For AO2, which AQA Level does this reach, and why?
   * **Options:** A) Level 6 — analysis with exploration of the effects, B) Level 5 — examination of how the method works, C) Level 3 — identification of the method with only a general comment on effect, D) Level 4 — clear explanation of the effect.
   * **Correct:** C
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Naming the pathetic fallacy and adding "it tells us the mood is gloomy" is AQA Level 3 AO2 — a general comment on the method. Level 6 wants "analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously" and "exploration of effects", reading the fog as a city that cannot perceive its own guilt.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why A:** There is no analysis and no exploration of layered effects here, so the top band is out of reach.
   * **Why B:** Examination requires sustained working-through of how the fog makes meaning, which is absent.
   * **Why D:** Clear explanation needs the effect explained, not merely asserted as "gloomy".
7. **Type: Fill-in-the-Blank \[Tests AO1 References\]**
   * **Question:** AQA's AO1 descriptor requires "\[BLANK\] use of precise references" — short, well-judged quotations such as "an exact science" embedded in your sentence.
   * **Answer:** Judicious
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Judicious" means well-judged and precise. The single phrase "an exact science", chosen to pin an argument about Holmes embodying Victorian faith in reason, outperforms six general references.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **WhyWrong:** Students often guess "frequent" or "accurate" — but the descriptor's keyword is judicious, because choosing the right micro-quotation matters more than quoting often.
8. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Level 5 Descriptor\]**
   * **Question:** In the AQA AO1 ladder, which phrasing describes Level 5, sitting one band below the conceptualised top?
   * **Options:** A) Some explained response, B) Thoughtful, developed response with apt references integrated into interpretation, C) Critical, exploratory, conceptualised response to task and whole text, D) Simple comment.
   * **Correct:** B
   * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. AQA Level 5 AO1 is a "thoughtful, developed response" with "apt references integrated into interpretation" — development plus integrated quotation, sitting just below the "critical, exploratory, conceptualised" demand of Level 6.
   * **AO:** AO1
   * **Why A:** "Some explained response" is the Level 3 wording — explained comment that stays at character/plot level, two bands below.
   * **Why C:** "Critical, exploratory, conceptualised response to task and whole text" is the Level 6 top band, not Level 5.
   * **Why D:** A "simple comment" is the bottom of the ladder — undeveloped and unexplained.
9. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests Level 6 Phrasing\]**
   * **Question:** Which phrases appear verbatim in AQA's Level 6 descriptors? (Select all that apply)
   * **Options:** A) Analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously, B) Judicious use of precise references to support interpretation(s), C) Specific, detailed links between context, text and task, D) Identification of the writer's methods.
   * **Correct:** A, B, C
   * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
   * **Feedback:** Judicious analysis of methods, judicious precise references, and specific detailed contextual links are all Level 6 wording across AO2, AO1 and AO3. "Identification of the writer's methods" is the Level 3 AO2 wording — a rung far below the top band.
   * **AO:** AO2
   * **Why D:** "Identification of the writer's methods" is the Level 3 register of the AO2 ladder, with only a general comment on effect — the opposite end of the scale from the exploratory top band.
10. **Type: True/False \[Tests AO Weighting\]**

11. **Type: MCQ \[Tests AO2 Descriptor Ladder\]**

    * **Options:** A) Identification of the writer's methods with a general comment on effect, B) Analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously, C) Comment on the writer's methods, D) Clear explanation of the effects.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Level 6 AO2 asks for "analysis of the writer's methods with subject terminology used judiciously" plus "exploration of effects". The verbs climb: awareness, identification, comment, explanation, and finally analysis and exploration.

    * **Answer:** Analysis

13. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Conceptualised\]**
    * **Question:** AQA Level 6 AO1 rewards a "conceptualised" response. A conceptualised reading of The Sign of the Four is one that...
    * **Options:** A) uses difficult, complicated vocabulary to signal sophistication, B) is organised around a concept — reason confronting an unknowable, imperial world — treating Holmes, Small and the plot as evidence for that idea, C) packs in as much Victorian historical context as it can, D) is very long and dense with detail.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Conceptualised" means built around a concept — reading the novel as an exploration of reason confronting an unknowable, imperial world, with the characters and events as evidence. It is about organising ideas, not complexity, length or context-stuffing.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Hard vocabulary is not a concept — conceptualised is about the idea, not the wording.
    * **Why C:** Context supports AO3; it does not make a response conceptualised.
    * **Why D:** Length is not conceptualisation — a short response can be conceptualised.
14. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Judicious\]**
    * **Question:** Two students argue that Holmes embodies Victorian faith in reason. One quotes six lines; the other selects the single phrase "an exact science". Whose reference is "judicious" — and what does "judicious" mean?
    * **Options:** A) The first — judicious means marshalling as much supporting evidence as possible, B) The second — judicious means a well-judged, selective reference chosen to do the most argumentative work, C) Both equally — judicious just means quoting accurately, D) Neither — judicious means choosing the longest quotation available.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. "Judicious" means well-judged and selective — chosen because it does real work, not because it is long or abundant. The precise phrase "an exact science" outperforms six general ones. This is why AQA Level 6 says "judicious use of precise references".
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Judicious is about wise selection, not quantity — more can mean less precise.
    * **Why C:** Accuracy alone is not judiciousness; the CHOICE must serve the argument.
    * **Why D:** Length is irrelevant; aptness to the point is what counts.

16. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Vocabulary Critical\]**
    * **Question:** AQA asks for a "critical" style. In a mark scheme, what does "critical" actually mean?
    * **Options:** A) Pointing out the novel's faults and what Conan Doyle did badly, B) Forming an evaluative, argued judgement and taking a position on interpretation, C) Writing in a consistently serious, formal tone, D) Disagreeing with other readers' interpretations.
    * **Correct:** B
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. In a mark scheme "critical" does NOT mean fault-finding. It means evaluative and argued — you weigh interpretations and commit to a position. AQA pairs it as "critical, exploratory": an argued reading that opens up meaning.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** This is the everyday sense of "critical" (negative), not the assessment sense.
    * **Why C:** Tone is not what "critical" rewards — an argued judgement is.
    * **Why D:** Critical means evaluating the text, not other students.

20. **Type: Ranking \[Tests AO1\]**
    * **Question:** Rank these four openings on The Sign of the Four from WEAKEST to STRONGEST by AQA AO1 Level (type the letters in order, weakest first).
    * **Options:** A) "Conan Doyle constructs the Agra treasure as tainted colonial plunder, using the novel to interrogate whether empire can morally hold the wealth it seizes.", B) "Mary Morstan asks Holmes for help, the treasure is found, and it is finally lost in the Thames.", C) "The treasure is a bad thing in the novel, and it causes a lot of trouble and death.", D) "Conan Doyle presents the treasure as destructive through the deaths it causes, making the reader question the cost of empire."
    * **Correct:** B, C, D, A
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. Each rung is one clear AQA band higher. The plot-summary rung, narrating events only, is Level 1 (simple comment). The bare value-judgement about a bad thing causing trouble, with no method and no evidence, is Level 2 (supported comment). The clear rung that names a method — the deaths — and its effect on the reader is Level 4 (clear explained response). The critical, conceptualised rung, arguing an idea from the first line, is Level 6.
    * **WhyWrong:** Weakest to strongest runs from plot narration, to a value-judgement with no method, to an explained method plus reader effect, to a conceptualised argument. Each rung is exactly one AQA Level higher.
21. **Type: MCQ \[Tests Extract Springboard\]**
    * **Question:** For the AQA 19th-century novel question, what is the "springboard" way to use the printed extract?
    * **Options:** A) Spend the whole answer on the extract and mention the rest of the novel in the conclusion, B) Ignore the extract and write everything from memory, C) Use the extract as your AO2 evidence bank, then move out to the whole novel for AO1 argument and arc, D) Copy long stretches of the extract to prove you have read it.
    * **Correct:** C
    * **Feedback:** ✓ Correct. The extract is your evidence bank for close language analysis (AO2). To reach the top bands you must move beyond it to argue the whole novel's development — Mary Morstan's arrival, the Sholtos, Small's confession, the treasure lost. Whole-text argument is the AO1 separator.
    * **AO:** AO1
    * **Why A:** Staying in the extract feels safe, but never moving beyond it means you cannot prove whole-text understanding.
    * **Why B:** Ignoring the extract discards your richest source of detailed language evidence.
    * **Why D:** Copying stretches proves transcription, not thinking; the extract is for analysing choices, not reproducing.
22. **Type: Select All That Apply \[Tests AO2 Methods\]**
    * **Question:** Which count as valid AO2 writer's methods to analyse in The Sign of the Four? (Select all that apply)
    * **Options:** A) Specific word choices and their connotations (such as "dense drizzly fog"), B) Form (the first-person narration through Watson, the confession-within-the-story), C) Structure (shifts of pace across the narrative, the placement of Small's confession), D) The biography of Arthur Conan Doyle.
    * **Correct:** A, B, C
    * **Scoring:** 2 marks for A, B, C. 1 mark if mostly correct.
    * **Feedback:** Word-level choice, form and structure are all AO2 methods — the deliberate choices Conan Doyle made on the page. But a method only counts as AO2 once you pair it with its effect on the reader; naming "narration" or "pathetic fallacy" alone is feature-spotting. The author's biography sits outside the text and earns nothing.
    * **AO:** AO2
    * **Why D:** A writer's biography is background about the author, not a method within the text; AO2 analyses language, form and structure.
