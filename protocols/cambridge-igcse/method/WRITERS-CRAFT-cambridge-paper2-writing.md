# PART ONE: KNOW THE GAME YOU ARE PLAYING

Two hours. Two pieces of writing. 80 marks. But the two sections reward almost opposite skills:

| | Section A: Directed Writing | Section B: Composition |
|---|---|---|
| marks | 40 = **15 READING + 25 writing** | 40 = 16 content & structure + **24 style & accuracy** |
| length | 250–350 words | 350–450 words |
| source | TWO texts you must evaluate | your imagination — choice of 4 tasks |
| the trap | writing beautifully but ignoring the texts | content that ignores the chosen task |

⚠️ **The 700-word persuasive essay from other boards misfires here — not because length is
penalised (nothing on this paper deducts marks), but because it earns nothing on the Reading
table.** A text-free argument scores Reading Level 1 (*"very limited response with minimal
relation to the text"*, 1–2 of 15): absolute ceiling ~27/40, realistic 19–25, plus double the
time spent. And the Writing table has its own trap for the opposite student: **Level 3 is
"relies on the sequence of the original text"** — a text-by-text walkthrough caps writing at
~10–13/25 however good the prose. The winning shape reorganises BOTH texts' ideas around YOUR
argument. Everything in Part Two follows from those two table facts.

**The clock:** ~55 minutes on Section A (including 10–12 reading the two texts), ~55 on
Section B, 10 checking. Word guidance is advisory (*"offered to help them organise their
time"*) — but it describes the efficient shape of the marks.

# PART TWO: SECTION A — THE EVALUATOR'S FRAME

Every Section A task in six years has the same skeleton: a form (**letter 17 · speech 12 ·
article 11 — nothing else ever**), an audience, and two bullets — the first is ALWAYS
*"evaluate the ideas, attitudes and opinions given in both texts."*

## The tables you are marked on (verbatim)

**Table B, Reading — out of 15:**

| Level | Marks | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **6** | 13–15 | • Successfully evaluates ideas and opinions, **both explicit and implicit**. • **Assimilates** ideas from the text to give a developed, sophisticated response. |
| **5** | 10–12 | • Some successful evaluation of ideas and opinions, both explicit and implicit. • A thorough response, supported by a detailed selection of relevant ideas from the text. |

| **2** | 3–4 | • Identifies explicit ideas and opinions. • Makes a limited response with little evidence from the text. |
| **1** | 1–2 | • Very limited response with minimal relation to the text. |

**Table A, Writing — out of 25:**

| Level | Marks | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **6** | 22–25 | • Highly effective style capable of conveying subtle meaning. • Carefully structured for benefit of the reader. • Wide range of sophisticated vocabulary, precisely used. • Highly effective register for audience and purpose. • Spelling, punctuation and grammar almost always accurate. |
| **5** | 18–21 | • Effective style. • Secure overall structure, organised to help the reader. • Wide range of vocabulary, used with some precision. • Effective register for audience and purpose. • SPaG mostly accurate, occasional minor errors. |

| **2** | 6–9 | • Limited style. • Not well sequenced. • Limited vocabulary or words/phrases copied from the original. • Limited register awareness. • Persistent errors. |
| **1** | 1–5 | • Expression unclear. • Poor sequencing. • Very limited vocabulary or copying. • Very limited register awareness. • Errors impede communication. |

**Read the ladder and the whole method falls out.** The reading climb is: *identify* (L2) →
*comment* (L3) → *begin to evaluate* (L4) → *evaluate implicit too* (L5) → **"assimilate"**
(L6). Assimilate means the texts' ideas are digested INTO your argument — you can no longer see
the join. That is also exactly what rescues the writing mark from Level 3's "relies on the
sequence of the original."

## The Five Moves

### Move 1: The Two-Voices Map
Before writing, reduce each text to a POSITION, not a topic. Two sentences. If you cannot write
them, read again.
- **(work pack):** Text A: "the limits, not the job, decide the outcome." Text B: "it works —
  but only on your terms."
- **(habits pack):** Text A: "design beats willpower, but a habit is a tool, not the goal."
  Text B: "the apps work until you start serving them; a habit only a phone witnesses isn't
  yours."
- **(fashion pack):** Text A: "there is no virtuous purchase, only a less wasteful wardrobe."
  Text B: "the problem is disposable clothes, not cheap ones."
- **Why it works:** evaluation needs two things to WEIGH. A topic cannot be weighed; a claim can.

### Move 2: The Verdict Verbs
Evaluation lives in verbs that judge: *overstates · concedes · assumes · proves from experience
what the other only reports · converges on · the weakness in this claim is…* Three or more,
spread through the piece. The tell you're stuck at Level 3: every text-verb is "says" or
"states" — that is *commenting*, and the table prices it at 5–6 of 15.
- **The same idea at three levels:**
  - L2 (identifies): "Text A says part-time jobs teach time management."
  - L3 (comments): "Text A says jobs teach time management, which is a good point because
    students are busy."
  - L5–6 (evaluates/assimilates): "The article's strongest card is time management — and
    Amara's blog unknowingly proves it, since her grades rose only after the job forced her to
    plan. What she adds, and the article misses, is the condition: it worked because she capped
    her hours."

### Move 3: The Convergence Find
The two texts always disagree somewhere and agree somewhere deeper. Finding the deeper agreement
is the highest-value single move — it hands you an *implicit* idea (the L5/L6 gate) and gives
bullet two its content.
- **(work):** the balanced article and the bakery blogger both land on: guard the hours, judge
  the employer. That IS the advice your letter gives.
- **(machines):** Carmen's switch-back-what-you-need month is Text A's closing advice, lived:
  stay the master; check you still can.
- **(fashion):** researcher and teenager arrive at the identical rule — lifespan beats price —
  from opposite lives. An article that notices this writes itself.

### Move 4: The Second Bullet Is the Point
Bullet two ("advise your friend…", "suggest how…") is where evaluation becomes PURPOSE. Your
advice must be *built from* the texts' strongest surviving ideas — the ones your evaluation
left standing. Advice from nowhere scores like a text-free essay.

### Move 5: Wear the Form Lightly but Visibly
The form earns register marks in its first and last five lines: a letter opens and signs off
like a letter; a speech addresses its audience and lands its close; an article is headlined and
hooks. In between, all three are the same thing: a structured argument in your register.

| form | opening | register | close |
|---|---|---|---|
| **letter to a friend** | "Dear —", warm personal hook | direct, contractions fine | wish + name |
| **letter to authority** | formal salutation, purpose stated | measured, precise | formal sign-off |
| **speech** | direct address, hook in line one | rhetorical: questions, threes, we/you | a line built to be remembered |
| **article** | headline + first-line hook | engaging, controlled | circle back to the hook |

## A FULL MODEL — Section A at the top bands
*(our Practice Paper 1 task: letter to a friend, advice on taking a part-time job while
studying; ~300 words. Margin labels show the moves firing.)*

> Dear Sam,
>
> You asked what I think about the bakery job — so I did some reading, and I'm going to give it
> to you straight. **[form: warm, direct]**
>
> Everything I found says the same two things at once: a job could be the best thing you do this
> year, and it could quietly sink you. The difference isn't the job — it's the limits you put
> round it. **[assimilation: both texts digested into one thesis]** The research is honest about
> the danger: a few hours a week does no harm and often helps, but past about ten, grades slip
> and sleep goes. One teacher describes marking homework "visibly written at midnight after a
> six-hour shift" — and I know you, Sam, you'd be that hero. **[evaluate + develop + audience]**
>
> But here's the voice I trust more than the research, because she's actually lived it: a
> student who works Saturdays in a bakery. She missed lie-ins and two birthdays — she doesn't
> pretend otherwise — yet her grades went UP, because the job forced her to plan her week.
> **[weighing sources: experience vs report]** Notice the condition, though. One day, never two.
> Exam weeks sacred. And a boss who respects that — her test, and now mine for you: if they
> won't, leave, because "a job should make the rest of your life work better, not swallow it."
> **[convergence → advice]**
>
> So: yes — take it, on those terms. Cap your hours before you start, keep exam weeks
> untouchable, and treat the first month as a trial you're allowed to fail. **[bullet 2,
> concrete]**
>
> Tell me what you decide.
> Anya

**Why it scores:** both texts evaluated and *woven* (never walked through in sequence) ·
implicit ideas surfaced (the condition behind the blogger's success) · advice built from the
surviving ideas · letter register held start to finish · ~300 words.

# PART THREE: SECTION B — COMPOSITION

One task from four: two descriptive, two narrative — every paper, all 40. **24 of the 40 marks
are style and accuracy.** Choose in 60 seconds using your prepared bank (the choice itself is a
drilled skill).

## The tables you are marked on (verbatim)

**Three facts from the tables that decide strategy:**
1. **Table B contains no reference to content, plot, relevance, task or title** — 24 marks of
   pure prose quality, identical for all four questions. This is why a polished, rehearsed
   piece is so strong here — and why the prepared-story strategy works.
2. **Relevance wording exists only at Table A Levels 1–4, never at 5–6** — and Cambridge states
   descriptors *"should not be interpreted as hurdle statements."* Loose task-fit costs ~5–6
   Table A marks; there is no cap. Tight fit is worth drilling, not fearing.
3. **The L6 vs L5 difference is bought by consistency of QUALITY** — "precise" vs "mostly
   precise", "consistent" vs "mostly consistent". Sustained crafting, the whole way through.

## THE NARRATIVE CRAFT

**Sophicly's method: the prepared story** — up to three stories with distinct emotional cores,
mastered, adapted on the day. Evidence, bank design and adaptation drills:
`PREPARED-STORY-STRATEGY-cambridge-sectionB.md`. The craft rules that make a story worth
preparing, each pointed at a table line:

### Move 1: The Narrow Window *(Table A: "well balanced … for deliberate effect")*
350–450 words is a SCENE, not a saga. One protagonist, one place (one shift at most), under an
hour of story time. The whole-life epic is the most reliable Level 3 shape there is ("plot is
straightforward, limited use of the features of narrative writing" — because there's no room
left to use them).

### Move 2: The Turn *(Table A: "effective climax")*
A story is a change: something believed at the start is not believed at the end. Name the turn
in one sentence before you write; if you can't, you have an anecdote.
- **Worked example:** "She thinks the licence exam is the obstacle; she discovers the fear was."
  One line, and the climax has an address.

### Move 3: Show the Consequence *(Table A: "convincing details"; Table B: "chosen for effect")*
"I was terrified" is a claim. "My knuckles whitened on the rim of the basket" is evidence.
Emotion enters through the body and the object, not the label.

### Move 4: The Loaded Object *(the adaptation engine)*
Give the story one object that carries it — a letter, a watch, a door. Objects hold feeling,
absorb titles ('The watch', 'The message', 'The portrait'), and give endings something concrete
to close on. This is also what makes a prepared story bend to a set title in one paragraph.

### Move 5: Land the Ending *(Table A L6: "carefully managed for deliberate effect")*
The last paragraph answers the first — an echo, a reversal, the object again. Endings that
merely stop are the second most reliable Level 3 shape.

## THE DESCRIPTIVE CRAFT

Descriptive is the underrated choice: no plot to manage, pure Table B — but it has its own
table column, and the column tells you exactly how it fails.

### Move 1: The Camera, Not the Plot *(Table A descriptive column, L4→L2)*
Read the column downwards: at L4 "a tendency to write in a narrative style" is *tolerated*; at
L3 the details "may be more typical of a narrative"; at L2 the description has collapsed into
"the recording of some relevant events". Drift is a downward slope, not a cliff — and at L5/L6
the issue simply disappears because the piece is fully descriptive. People may act; nothing
*happens*. The moment a character wants something, you are writing a story.

### Move 2: The Structured Sweep — with a moving lens *(Table A L6: "varieties of focus")*
Pick an organising path (near → far · outside → in · before → during → after · one hour
compressed) so the reader always knows where they are — then VARY THE FOCUS along it: wide shot
to close-up, the room to the single cup, the crowd to one face. The top band's own words are
*"varieties of focus"* — one path, many depths of field.

### Move 3: The Five-and-One *(Table B: "precise, well-chosen")*
All five senses across the piece — ONE sense per moment, precisely. "The kitchen was loud and
hot and busy" is three senses wasted; "steam climbed the window and turned the street outside
to ghosts" is one sense earning.

### Move 4: The Human Detail *(Table A: "convincing overall picture")*
Empty places need one human trace (the half-drunk cup, the propped-open door); crowded places
need one individual pulled briefly into focus. It is what stops description being a list.

### Move 5: The Turning Hour *(structure without plot)*
Choose the moment of change — closing time, first light, just before opening. Time pressure
gives description the momentum plot would otherwise provide (real titles: 'Just before opening
time', 'The last bus of the night').

## A MODEL DESCRIPTIVE OPENING — with the lens moving
*(task: 'Just before opening time', our Practice Paper 2 slot)*

> The café holds its breath at seven fifty. Chairs stand upside down on tables like acrobats
> frozen mid-trick, and the espresso machine — polished last night to a surgeon's shine —
> ticks softly as it warms. **[wide shot → single object]** Behind the counter, order: cups in
> ranks, spoons nested, the day's pastries still boxed and breathing butter through the
> cardboard. **[one sense per moment]** On the till sits a mug of tea going cold, the only
> untidy thing in the room, its owner's apron slung over the office door. **[the human detail]**
> Outside, the street is already rehearsing — a bus sighs past, a dog tows its runner toward
> the park — but in here the light lies in long, unbroken slabs across a floor nobody has
> crossed since it dried. **[focus: out, then in]** Eight minutes. The clock is the loudest
> thing in the building.

# PART FOUR: THE TECHNIQUE TOOLKIT — SUSTAINED CRAFTING, THE TABLE'S OWN STANDARD

The full technique compendium lives in *The Complete Writer's Craft: Creative Non-Fiction*
(Model Answer Resources). Everything there transfers — because Cambridge's tables never count
devices and never punish abundance. The top band's actual words: vocabulary and structures
*"chosen for effect"*, *"precise"*, register *"consistent"*; the Q1 writing summit is a *"highly
effective style capable of conveying subtle meaning."*

**So the rule is not "use fewer techniques." The rule is: use every technique you can CONTROL —
and make each one earn its place.** The same principle as AQA's "sustained crafting of
linguistic devices": density of *crafted* technique wins; what loses is the mechanical version —
devices dropped in to be counted. A plain, technique-free piece cannot reach "highly effective
style" any more than a formulaic one can.

The test for every device, from the table's own vocabulary:
1. **Chosen for effect** — you can say what this device DOES to this reader at this moment.
2. **Precise** — the right word inside it; a nearly-right word breaks W3 at the top band.
3. **Register-safe** — it belongs to the form you're in (a firework flourish inside a formal
   letter breaks the register W4 demands you sustain).
4. **Sustained** — the crafting runs the whole piece; a dazzling first paragraph followed by
   plain filler reads as "mostly" — and "mostly" is the Level 5 word, not the Level 6 one.

| technique | crafted deployment at 250–450 words |
|---|---|
| **rule of three** | the persuasive workhorse — in the ask, the build, the close; vary the shapes so each is chosen, not stamped |
| **rhetorical question** | early to open the gap, later to close it — the answer belongs to your argument |
| **direct address** | the spine of speech + friend-letter; keeps register warm and consistent |
| **metaphor** | extended and returning beats scattered — a metaphor that comes back reads as design ("structured for deliberate effect") |
| **contrast** | the evaluator's natural device: text vs text, before vs after |
| **sensory image** | the composition's engine — precise, one sense earning per moment |
| **short sentence** | the emphasis tool both sections share. Use after a long one. Like this. |
| **personification** | descriptive writing's quiet weapon (the balloon "sighing down") |

## The thematic response bank — evaluation angles ready per theme
*(each theme: the tension the two texts will stage, the convergence to hunt for, and a model
opening line. Full practice papers exist for the first four — `PACK-P2-paper01–04`.)*

**1. Work & career** — the tension: opportunity vs exploitation/overload. The convergence:
conditions decide the outcome (hours, employer, purpose). Model opening (letter): *"You asked
what I think about the job — so I did some reading, and I'm going to give it to you straight."*

**2. Self, habits & mind** — the tension: systems help vs systems take over. The convergence:
tools for starting, not for depending. Model opening (speech): *"Raise your hand if you've ever
downloaded an app to fix your life. Keep it up if it's still on your phone."*

**3. Consumption & waste** — the tension: cheap-and-democratic vs disposable-and-destructive.
The convergence: lifespan beats price. Model opening (article): *"THE FOUR-POUND JACKET — In a
mountain of clothes in a Chilean desert lies the answer to a question nobody asked a teenager."*

**4. Technology & AI** — the tension: liberation of attention vs loss of capability and choice.
The convergence: master, not servant; check you still can. Model opening (speech): *"Somewhere
in your house, a machine has already made three decisions for you today. Name one."*

**5. Culture & the arts** — the tension: tradition's value vs relevance/access. The likely
convergence: participation over preservation. **6. Education & school** — the tension: structure
vs freedom (all-year schooling, outdoor learning, volunteer schemes). The convergence: what
actually changes the learner.

**The drills:**
1. **Two-Voices sprints:** any pack insert → both positions in two sentences, five minutes.
2. **Verdict-verb rewrites:** take a "Text A says…" draft and re-verb every claim.
3. **The 350-word discipline:** one timed Section A per week; count AFTER, never during.
4. **Prepared-story adaptation drills:** per the strategy doc — titles first.
5. **Descriptive focus check:** swap drafts, hunt for smuggled plot — then check the lens moved
   (wide → close) at least three times.
