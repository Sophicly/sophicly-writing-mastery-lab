# FORM MASTERY — PAPER 1 QUESTION 3
## All six forms, with a full model answer for each — and a deep section on the REPORT
### Cambridge IGCSE 0500 / 0990. Every model is written from ONE shared text so you can see exactly what changes between forms and what does not. Companion volumes: *The Complete Reader's Craft* · *The Past-Questions Compendium* · *The Traps*.

**Why one text, six forms.** The commonest Q3 failure is not weak writing — it is writing the
*wrong form*, or writing every form the same way (`CAMBRIDGE-TRAPS-what-loses-marks.md` T2, T18).
The cure is to see the SAME material become six different pieces. Every model below is built from
Text C of Sophicly Practice Paper 1 (`PACK-P1-paper01-travel-INSERT.md` — *Chasing the dawn*,
Marta's first balloon flight with her instructor Karl). Read that text first; then read the six
models and notice what stays (the facts) and what changes (voice, structure, purpose).

**What every model does, whatever the form** — these are the reading marks, and they are 15 of the
25:
- Uses **detail from the text**, developed rather than copied
- Covers **all three bullets** with equal attention
- Sustains a **convincing voice** (scored in the READING table — see the craft volume)
- Reads as the named form to its named audience, in ~250–350 words

# PART ONE — THE REPORT (the form Sophicly has not taught before)

**Frequency: 2 of 40 papers** (`s24/11` — Majd's own diagnostic; `w23/11`). Rare — but it is the
one Majd has sat, and the only form Cambridge has ever labelled *"formal"*.

## What a report IS, in exam terms

A report is written **because someone in authority asked for it**, and it exists to let that person
**make a decision**. That single fact generates every convention:

| feature | why |
|---|---|
| **Headed** (*Report to: … Subject: … Date: …*) | the reader files it; they need to know what it is at a glance |
| **Formal, neutral register** — no contractions, no slang, no jokes | you are speaking as a professional, not a friend |
| **Impersonal where possible** — *the safety procedures were followed*, not *I was brilliant* | credibility comes from evidence, not enthusiasm |
| **Sectioned**, often with sub-headings | the reader must find the part they care about |
| **Ends on recommendations or reassurance** | a report that only describes has not done its job |
| **Feelings appear as professional judgement** — *this caused understandable concern* | emotion is reported, not performed |

**The two real tasks show the pattern exactly.** In `s24/11` Mike must *"reassure the company that
its various concerns … are unfounded"*; in `w23/11` Edgar must *"evaluate potential problems"* and
*"suggest how the experiences … could be improved"*. Both end forward-looking. **Teach the shape:
purpose → findings → recommendation.**

## The three traps specific to reports
1. **Writing a letter with headings.** *Dear Sir, I hope you are well…* — wrong register from line
   one. A report has no salutation.
2. **Defensiveness.** When the task asks you to reassure, the persuasive move is *acknowledge the
   concern, then answer it with evidence*. Denial reads as weak; Cambridge's own top-band word for
   Q3 reading is **evaluate**.
3. **Stopping at description.** Bullet 3 in both real tasks is forward-looking. No recommendations
   = marks left on the table.

## MODEL — REPORT
*Task (written in the real Cambridge style, on our ballooning text): You are Karl, the instructor.
The flying school's safety committee has read Marta's published account of her first flight and
has questions about it. Write a formal report. In your report you should: • explain what happened
during the flight and landing, and the safety measures in place • explain how the trainee responded
to the demands of the flight • recommend what the school should do about first-flight training.*

> **Report to:** The Safety Committee, Dawn Flight School
> **Subject:** Trainee first flight, 14 May — response to the published account
> **Prepared by:** K. Andersen, Chief Instructor
>
> **1. Purpose**
> This report addresses the committee's questions arising from Ms Osei's published account of her
> first supervised flight, and recommends changes to first-flight training.
>
> **2. The flight**
> The launch took place shortly after five o'clock, in the calm conditions we require for trainee
> flights. The envelope was cold-inflated by ground crew before the burner was lit, and the balloon
> was held on its tether until I was satisfied the trainee was correctly positioned in the basket.
> The trainee operated the burner for the ascent under my direct supervision. We climbed to
> approximately three hundred metres and located an easterly current, at which point I began
> instrument instruction. The account's description of the ground "simply let go" is a fair
> description of a normal ascent and does not indicate any irregularity.
>
> The landing was firmer than average. The wind freshened during the descent, and the basket
> touched, lifted and dragged before coming to rest against a hedge. This is within normal
> parameters. The trainee had been briefed in the landing position — knees bent, both hands on the
> rope, no exit until instructed — and complied fully. Neither occupant was injured.
>
> **3. The trainee's response**
> Ms Osei was visibly apprehensive on boarding and during the ascent. This is usual and is not a
> concern. What matters is that she followed every instruction while apprehensive, held a level
> altitude unaided for three minutes, and remained composed through a firm landing. In my
> assessment she is progressing appropriately toward solo flight.
>
> **4. Recommendations**
> I recommend that trainees be briefed that a firm landing is normal rather than exceptional, and
> that first-flight accounts intended for publication be reviewed by the instructor beforehand, so
> that a routine flight is not read as a hazardous one.

**Why it scores:** headed and sectioned · formal throughout (no contractions) · every claim
anchored in the text's own events (the fans, the tether, the three level minutes, the hedge) ·
concerns *acknowledged then answered with evidence* rather than denied · ends on recommendations ·
the voice is a professional's, consistently.

# PART TWO — THE OTHER FIVE FORMS

## JOURNAL (7 of 40) ⭐ blind spot

**Reader: yourself.** Past tense, first person, dated. Write what the character would not say
aloud. Structure: what happened → what I actually felt → what I now think or fear. Cambridge's real
journal tasks nearly all ask for FEELINGS or REFLECTION in bullet 2 or 3 — a neutral account of
events misses the form.

> *Task: You are Karl. That evening you write your journal.*
>
> **Tuesday, late.**
>
> Marta flew today, and I have been turning it over ever since. I told her she did well. What I
> did not tell her is that for the first two minutes I was not certain she would squeeze the
> burner at all. She stood there with both hands on the rim, white to the knuckles, and I have
> learned to say nothing in that moment — a nervous trainee who is told to hurry never comes back.
>
> She looked down when I asked her to. That is the whole thing, really. Not courage exactly;
> obedience under fear, which is more useful. The ones who cannot look down are the ones I worry
> about.
>
> Three level minutes at three hundred metres. She thought I was being kind when I said "smooth".
> I was not being kind. Most of them chase the burner for weeks — burn, over-climb, panic, sink —
> and she found the rhythm on her first morning.
>
> Then the landing, which the wind decided rather than either of us. Touched, leapt, dragged,
> tipped us against the hedge, and I saw her face go from fright to laughter in about a second. I
> shouted the usual — knees, rope, stay in — and she did all three without arguing. I have flown
> with licensed pilots who argue.
>
> I have been doing this nineteen years and I still cannot explain to anyone why. Today is the
> nearest I get: someone climbs in who has only ever read about it, and by the time they climb
> out it has become the thing they will organise the rest of their life around. I watched it
> happen this morning at about ten past five.
>
> She flies solo next month. She will be fine. I will not sleep.

**Why it scores:** private thoughts he would never say to her · feelings that CHANGE across the
entry · every detail traceable to the text (the knuckles, "look down", the three minutes,
"smooth", the hedge) · reflection in the final paragraphs, as the form demands.

## INTERVIEW (7 of 40) ⭐ blind spot

**The three bullets ARE the three questions** — Cambridge often says *"the interviewer asks the
following three questions only."* Write question, answer, three times. Spoken register:
contractions, asides, a self-correction — but controlled. Structure is handed to you free; spend
the saved effort on voice and detail.

> *Task: You are Karl. You are interviewed for a podcast about unusual jobs. The interviewer asks
> three questions only: • What actually happens on a trainee's first flight? • What makes someone
> a good balloon pilot? • Why do people take it up?*
>
> **— What actually happens on a first flight?**
>
> Earlier than anyone expects, is the first thing. We're in the field by five, because we need the
> air still. The crew unrolls the envelope on the wet grass and we cold-inflate it with fans —
> that's before any flame at all — and then the burner goes in and this enormous red and gold
> thing simply stands up out of the ground. That's usually when the trainee goes quiet. Then they
> climb in, they squeeze the handle themselves, and the field drops away. I let them do the ascent.
> They should feel that it's theirs from the first second.
>
> **— What makes someone a good pilot?**
>
> Not fearlessness — I'd be suspicious of that. Patience, mostly. A balloon answers a moment after
> you ask it to, like a big animal deciding whether to obey, and people who can't wait for that
> answer end up chasing it up and down all morning. Marta, the trainee I flew with in May, held
> three level minutes on her first attempt. Three minutes doesn't sound like anything. It's
> everything. And you need to be able to take a firm landing without deciding you've nearly died —
> we bounced, dragged and finished against a hedge that day, which is a perfectly ordinary landing
> and looks like a disaster from outside.
>
> **— Why do people take it up?**
>
> The silence, I think, though nobody says that at the open day. Between burns there's no engine,
> no wind in your ears, because you're travelling with the wind. You hear a farm dog half a
> kilometre below. People come for the view and they stay for that.

**Why it scores:** spoken register with contractions and asides · each answer develops text detail
rather than repeating it · a distinct personality (dry, unhurried, quietly proud) · all three
questions answered fully.

## SPEECH / TALK (11 of 40 — the commonest form)

Address the audience in the room. Hook → the bullets as three movements → a close built to be
remembered. If the task carries a purpose verb (*persuade*, *encourage*), it governs the tone.

> *Task: You are Karl. You give a talk to a group of teenagers at an open day, encouraging them to
> try ballooning.*
>
> Good morning. I want to start by telling you the least glamorous fact about my job: my alarm
> goes off at ten past four.
>
> That's the price of it, and I'm telling you first so nobody feels tricked. Balloons fly at dawn
> because dawn is when the air holds still. By five we're standing in a wet field watching two
> fans push cold air into a piece of fabric the size of a house. Then the burner lights, and that
> flat, sagging thing stands up in front of you — and I've watched a hundred people forget they
> were cold at exactly that moment.
>
> Here's what I'd like you to understand about the flying itself: you will do it. Not watch it.
> Every trainee I take up squeezes the burner for their own ascent, and the ground lets go while
> their hand is on the handle. Three hundred metres up, we find a current and the balloon starts
> to answer you — slowly, a moment late, like a big animal deciding whether it agrees. Learning to
> wait for that answer is the whole skill. One of our trainees held a level altitude for three
> minutes on her first morning. She said afterwards it felt like being handed a medal, and she was
> right.
>
> And then, if you're lucky, the quiet arrives. No engine. No wind in your ears, because you're
> moving with it. Just a heron below you on a grey river and somebody's chimney smoke going up
> dead straight.
>
> You do not need to be fit, brave, or tall enough to see over the basket — well, that one, a
> little. You need to be awake at four. Come and stand in the field once. That's all I'm asking.

**Why it scores:** direct address throughout · a hook that disarms · patterns of three · text
detail developed into argument · ends on a concrete, memorable ask.

## LETTER (8 of 40)

Everything is decided by the recipient. To family or a friend: warm, confiding, contractions,
personal sign-off. To an authority: formal salutation, purpose in line one, measured register.

> *Task: You are Karl. You write to your sister, who has never understood why you fly.*
>
> Dear Ines,
>
> You asked me again at Christmas why I still do this at fifty-one, and I gave you a rubbish
> answer, so here is a better one. I took a trainee up in May and I think she has explained it for
> me.
>
> Her name is Marta. Six months of theory, and then the actual morning: four o'clock alarm, a
> field silver with dew, the crew hauling the envelope out flat while she stood there in her
> boots looking like someone who had made a serious mistake. You would have liked her. She got
> into the basket one leg at a time, very carefully, as if it might object.
>
> I let her do the ascent — I always do — and when she squeezed the burner the ground simply let
> go of us, and her knuckles went white on the rim. She didn't say anything for about a minute.
> Then I asked her to look down, and she did, and I watched the whole thing arrive: the field
> going small, the fields stitched together with hedges, the silence in between the burns. That's
> the part I've never been able to describe to you at a dinner table. There's no engine. You move
> with the wind, so there's no wind either. You hear a dog barking half a kilometre underneath you.
>
> She held the balloon level for three minutes on her own and grinned at the instruments like an
> idiot. I gave her one word of praise and she has probably repeated it to everyone she knows.
>
> That's why, Ines. Nineteen years of it, and I still get to be in the basket when it happens to
> somebody.
>
> Love,
> Karl

**Why it scores:** genuine warmth and a real relationship in the address · confiding register with
contractions · the same text detail as every other model, re-voiced entirely · a personal sign-off.

## ARTICLE (5 of 40)

Usually you are a JOURNALIST — an outsider reporting on the text's events. Third person about the
subject, headline first, journalistic distance: interested and informed, never gushing.

> *Task: You are a journalist. You write a magazine article about learning to fly a balloon.*
>
> **THE FOUR O'CLOCK CLUB**
> *Why the world's slowest aircraft still recruits, one wet field at a time*
>
> There is no comfortable way to describe what balloon training asks of you first: the alarm goes
> at ten past four, and there is no version of the sport that avoids it. Balloons fly at dawn
> because dawn is when the air stops moving, and the people who take it up learn to organise their
> lives around a single still hour.
>
> What they get in exchange is difficult to sell and easy to underestimate. Marta Osei, who flew
> for the first time this spring after six months of theory lessons, describes a field "silvered
> with dew" and two roaring fans forcing an envelope full of cold air until it billowed into a
> trembling hill of fabric — and then the burner, and the hill standing up. Her instructor let her
> operate the burner for the ascent herself, which is standard practice: trainees are expected to
> fly from the first second, not watch.
>
> The skill, by all accounts, is patience. A balloon responds a slow moment after the burner fires,
> and novices spend weeks over-correcting: climbing, panicking, sinking. Osei held a level altitude
> unaided for three minutes on her first morning, an achievement her instructor acknowledged with a
> single word.
>
> Landings are the part outsiders misread. Hers was firm — the basket touched, lifted, dragged and
> stopped against a hedge — and is entirely routine. What is not routine, and what every pilot
> mentions eventually, is the silence: no engine, no wind in the ears, and a heron visible on the
> river below.
>
> Osei flies solo next month. Her alarm, she reports, is already set.

**Why it scores:** headline + standfirst · journalist's third person and distance · the subject
quoted and characterised · the same events reframed as reporting rather than experience · a close
that lands.

# PART THREE — THE DRILL THIS DOCUMENT EXISTS FOR

**The form transplant.** Take any Text C — ours, or any real insert. Keep the facts. Write the
same three bullets as two different forms, back to back. Twenty minutes each.

What must stay identical: the events, the details, the people. What must change completely: who
you are talking to, how much you admit, what you want from them, and how the piece is shaped.

**Priority order for a student who has already sat a report** (Majd): journal → interview → speech
→ letter → article. That is untested-and-common first, per the 40-paper counts.

**Do not confuse this with Paper 2.** Q3 is a READING question in role: 15 of its 25 marks are for
using Text C. A beautifully written piece that floats free of the passage forfeits them
(`CAMBRIDGE-TRAPS-what-loses-marks.md` T19).

⚠️ **On these models:** they are written from Sophicly's own Practice Paper 1 text, which we
authored — so every detail above is checkable against `PACK-P1-paper01-travel-INSERT.md`. In the
exam, every detail must come from the paper's own Text C. Use these to learn the FORMS, never as
content to reuse.
