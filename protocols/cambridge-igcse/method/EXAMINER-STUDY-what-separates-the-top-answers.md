# WHAT SEPARATES THE TOP ANSWERS — Cambridge 0500/0990, from the examiners' own words

**What this is.** The mark scheme tells you what a Level 6 answer *is*. It does not tell you what a
Level 6 answer *does*. The examiner reports do — in passing, buried in per-question commentary, and
never collected. This document collects them.

**Provenance.** **Every Principal Examiner Report that exists for this syllabus, 2020–2025** —
sixteen sessions: `m21 m22 m23 m24 m25 · s21 s22 s23 s24 s25 · w20 w21 w22 w23 w24 w25`, in
`Examiner Reports (mirror)/` and `June 2024 (real past papers)/`. **646,000 words.** Text extracted
with `pdftotext -layout` into `_scan/er-text/`; `_scan/extract-er-discriminators.js` pulls
**1,645 unique top-band attribution sentences** into `_scan/ER-DISCRIMINATORS.txt`. Every quotation
below is verbatim from that corpus and carries its session and variant.
**Nothing here is inferred from general GCSE knowledge.**

⭐ **There is no June 2020 report, and there never was.** The series was cancelled; no scripts were
marked. Confirmed rather than assumed — no `0500_s20_er` exists on either mirror, and neither does
`0580_s20_er` (Mathematics, an unrelated syllabus), while November 2020 exists for both. **So this
corpus is complete, not merely large:** there is no report we are missing.

⭐ **0500 and 0990 reports are the same document.** Measured, not assumed: the June 2024 pair diff
at **99.7% word overlap**, and the only differences are the page-footer title lines. A 0500 finding
applies to Majd's 0990 without qualification.

⭐⭐ **EVERY MAJOR FINDING CARRIES A RECURRENCE COUNT — `n/16`, the number of SESSIONS that state it.**
Counted mechanically by `_scan/count-recurrence.js`, re-runnable. This matters more than it sounds:
a discriminator named in one report is an examiner's impression of one cohort; one named in fifteen
is how the paper is marked. **Where a count is low, it is printed low** — see §7.2, which is the
weakest finding in this document and says so.

⚠️ **Phrase count ≠ concept count, and the difference is not cosmetic.** The examiners re-use
sentence formulas, so an exact-phrase grep undercounts an idea expressed in other words. Both are
reported. The register finding (§0.6) is the case in point: the specific Q1(f) sentence appears in
**3/16**, the principle behind it in **15/16** — and reading only the first number would have
buried the single most transferable skill on the syllabus.

# 0 · THE SIX THINGS THAT ARE TRUE ON EVERY QUESTION OF BOTH PAPERS

These recur across both papers, and each one is a *behaviour*, not a quality — which is why they
are teachable and why the band tables cannot express them. Counts are sessions out of sixteen.

| # | the behaviour | sessions |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | Evaluate, don't reproduce | **16/16** |
| 0.2 | Plan the long answers before writing | **16/16** |
| 0.3 | Extra material *dilutes* the evidence | **16/16** |
| 0.4 | The guidance numbers are the shape of the answer | 12/16 |
| 0.5 | Paper 1 carries 15 writing marks | — *(structural, stated once)* |
| 0.6 | ⭐ Adapt the source's register to the target form | **15/16** |

### 0.1 ⭐⭐ EVALUATE, DON'T REPRODUCE. This is THE line between the middle and the top.

It is the same fault under six different names. Paper 1 Q2(d): *"generalised assertions rather than
considering specific words more closely"* (s23/12). Paper 1 Q3: *"tended to be too narrative as they
relied heavily on the sequencing of the original text"* (s21/12). Paper 2 Section A: *"Less effective
responses tended to repeat the ideas in the texts, rather than selecting points and commenting on
them"* — that exact sentence appears in **all sixteen sessions**. Q1(f): *"relied on trying to
offer a summary of the whole text in the order it was presented"* (s21/11).

**In every case the winning move is the same: select, then do something to what you selected.**

### 0.2 ⭐ A PLAN IS NAMED AS A PROPERTY OF THE STRONGEST ANSWERS, not as advice

> *"The strongest responses to the selective summary task often showed evidence of candidates having
> planned the route through the content of their answer before writing their response."* (s21/11,
> repeated near-verbatim in s22/11, s23/11, s24/13)

> *"…preceded by a bullet-pointed plan in which ideas from the text were noted briefly before being
> included in a fluent own-words response."* (s22/12)

Note **"route through"** — not a list of content, an ORDER. Examiners can see a plan in the finished
script, and they say so.

### 0.3 ⭐ MORE IS NOT SAFER — extra material actively costs marks

> *"Less successful responses attempted to include extra guesses in their answers to Questions
> 1(a) - (e) taking up valuable examination time by doing so and often **diluting evidence of
> understanding**."* (s24/11)

*Diluting* is the examiners' own word and they use it repeatedly. A hedged list of three possible
answers is worse than one clean answer. Length works both ways: *"Length was often an indicator of
the level of the response with some less successful responses being too short… and others very long
and wordy"* (s23/13).

### 0.4 THE GUIDANCE NUMBERS ARE THE SHAPE OF THE WINNING ANSWER

> *"the word guidance offered in Question 2(d) and Question 3 is **not a requirement of the task in
> itself** — the guidance is offered to help them organise their time efficiently and offer
> sufficient evidence of their skills and understanding to target higher levels."* (s24/11)

So "about 250–350 words" is not a rule — but the successful cohort is described, every session, as
the one that followed it. The 120-word summary cap is different: it IS a maximum.

### 0.5 ⭐ PAPER 1 IS NOT A PURE READING PAPER

> *"Whilst Paper 1 is primarily a test of Reading, **15 of the 80 marks available are for Writing** –
> divided between Question 1(f) and Question 3."* (s21/11)

Students who treat 1(f) and Q3 as comprehension answers leave those 15 marks on the table.

### 0.6 ⭐⭐ ADAPT THE SOURCE'S REGISTER TO THE TARGET FORM — **15/16 sessions**, and it is ONE skill worth 80 of the 160 marks

**This is the biggest finding in the study, and it only became visible once the corpus was complete.**
In the first seven reports it looked like a quirk of Q1(f). Across all sixteen it is a principle the
examiners apply to **every extended-response task on both papers** — Q1(f), Q3 and Section A — which
between them carry **15 + 25 + 40 = 80 of the syllabus's 160 marks.**

The source material always has a register. The task always demands a different one. Marks are given
for **noticing the gap and closing it**, and lost for carrying the source's voice across unchanged.

**On Q1(f)** — spoken/informal source, formal written summary (3/16 verbatim: s22, w22, w23):
> *"Many had recognised the opportunity to demonstrate their writing skills by **adapting the relaxed,
> informal register** of Dee's responses to the reporter's questions **to a more formal style suitable
> for a written summary**, whereas less assured responses had often missed this chance – for example
> echoing the text with reference to 'kids' or the 'Redmond Guy'."* (s22/11)
> *"adapting the more informal and expansive style of the original text to a more formal, efficient
> style suitable for a written summary"* (w23)

**On Q3** — the register is set by role + audience + form, and the examiners describe it in the same
words every session:
> *"there was often a clear adaptation of style and register to appeal to an audience of young
> people"* (s21, s23, w20 …)
> *"at the highest level, the potential sensitivities of the recipient informed the style and tone of
> the letter"* (m24)

**On Section A** — and here failing to adapt is named as the thing that caps an otherwise accurate
script:
> *"there were many responses which were **accurate in the main but showed limited adaptation of style
> from the original texts** to suit the style, context and register of a letter to a head teacher,
> **reducing the effectiveness of the response**."* (m22)

⭐ **The top-band version of it, worth teaching directly** — register as a source of authority rather
than mere politeness:
> *"This **tone of authority acquired through values shared with a young audience** was a subtle and
> successful adaptation of style and register for many high-level responses."* (w20)

⭐ **And a hard Level 5 threshold on Section A that pairs with it** (w21, w22):
> *"A sustained and appropriate register for this audience, **avoiding summary** and staying fully
> focused on the fact that the visitors would be students **led to marks in Level 5 and above**."*

⇒ **Teach it once, bank it three times.** A student who learns to ask **what register the source is
in, and what register the task wants** is answering Q1(f), Q3 and Section A with the same move.

# PAPER 1

## 1 · Q1(a)–(e) — short retrieval [15 marks total]

**What the top answers do:**

1. **Give DISTINCT ideas.** *"successful answers were careful to identify two separate and distinct
   reasons from the four available"* (s21/11); *"able to derive three distinct reasons of the four
   available in the specified paragraph"* (s21/11). Where four are available and two are asked for,
   two *different* ones score; the same idea twice scores once.
2. **Answer only the question's focus.** *"Successful answers in part (ii) were careful to identify
   only those ideas related to camping **that would slow down progress**, rather than explaining the
   benefits and attractions of camping"* (s21/11).
3. **Use the marks and the space as the length signal.** *"paying attention in Questions 1(a)–(e) to
   the marks and space available as a helpful indicator of the length and detail they needed"*
   (s21/11, s24/11).
4. **Recast, don't echo.** *"A failure to recast information from the passage to address the question
   sometimes diluted evidence of focus and/or understanding"* (s24/11).

**What kills it — the circular answer.** Named explicitly and it scores nothing:
> *"repeating the language of the question where own words were specified… such responses provided
> **no evidence of understanding** as a consequence – for example, in 1(b)(i) suggesting that 'this
> means their limits were challenged'."* (s22/11)
> *"'endless means they do not end'"* (s22/11)

⭐ **And it catches good candidates:** *"Several otherwise stronger candidates offered circular
answers in one or more of their responses"* (s24/11).

**Own-words precision, worked:** *"'quizzed' suggested a more searching drilling down and testing
approach than simply 'questioned' or 'asked' alone"* (s23/11); *"'saw' … does not get the idea of
them meeting"* for *encountered* (s21/12). ⇒ The test is: **would your synonym replace the word in
the sentence without changing what it means?** (s23/12: *"offered viable answers which would
accurately replace the words in the text without altering the meaning"*.)

## 2 · Q1(f) — SUMMARY [15 = 10 reading + 5 writing]

The 5 writing marks are the most-missed marks on Paper 1, because candidates do not notice they are
being marked as writers.

**What the top answers do:**

1. ⭐ **Plan the route first** (see §0.2 — this question is where it is named most often).
2. ⭐⭐ **REORDER the material.** *"focusing sharply on the task by referring to a wide range of the
   challenges she faced, **reordering the material where necessary to aid fluency and achieve logical
   progression**"* (s21/12, s23/12). And its opposite: *"relied on trying to offer an own words
   version of the whole text in the order it was presented – sometimes making only minimal changes
   such as moving from first to third person"* (s23/11).
3. ⭐ **Break free of the source's structure AND its language.** *"More effective responses were **not
   dependant on the structure or language of Text B** to communicate their ideas and were often able
   to offer more concise explanations as a result"* (s21/11).
4. ⭐⭐ **CONVERT THE REGISTER — this is the hidden writing mark.** Text B is usually spoken, informal,
   first-person. The summary is formal continuous prose.
   > *"Many had recognised the opportunity to demonstrate their writing skills by **adapting the
   > relaxed, informal register** of Dee's responses to the reporter's questions **to a more formal
   > style suitable for a written summary**, whereas less assured responses had often missed this
   > chance – for example echoing the text with reference to 'kids' or the 'Redmond Guy'."* (s22/11)

   Same finding in w22/12 (*'hanging around'*, *'this "we" business'*) and in s24/12, where lifting is
   blamed on *"not adapting the style of the original text"*. **Nothing in the band table says this.**
5. **Show overview by drawing from across the text.** *"relevant ideas had been carefully selected
   from **different parts of the text** and then re-organised more efficiently for their reader"*
   (s22/11).
6. **Be concise, and let concision be the length control.** *"The strongest responses tended to adhere
   to the advised length through adopting a concise and focused approach"* (s21/12).

**What kills it:** repeating the same idea (named every session); extraneous material (*"broken bikes
and bandits"*, *"her supportive family"*, *"painting teddy bears"*); lifting — *"lifting sections of
text is unlikely to evidence understanding of either the ideas in the passage or requirements of the
task"* (s21/11); and at worst *"no creditable content as they were entirely copied from the text"*
(s24/13). Also: **importing from the other texts** (s24/13).

## 3 · Q2(a)–(c) — short language items [10 marks]

- **2(a)** — *"focused on the underlined word or phrase, located the correct version in the text and
  gave it as the answer"* (s21/12). Marks are lost by giving too much: *"including too much of the
  text and therefore moving beyond explaining just the underlined phrase"*. **A word or phrase, not a
  sentence** (w21/12 key messages).
- **2(b)** — meaning **in context**, checked against the sentence (see §1 above).
- **2(c)** — ⭐ **it is 3 marks, so make three distinct points about ONE example** · **15/16 sessions**.
  > *"A number of successful responses had noted carefully the number of marks available and focused
  > their response to make **three distinct points in relation to their one chosen example**."*
  > (s21/11, s22/11, s23/11 — three sessions, same sentence)

  Choose the example you can mine three ways, and start with what it means in context before saying
  what it suggests: *"began with an explanation of the meaning of their example in context, ahead of
  going on to explain what the meaning suggested about Anna's attitude"* (s23/11).
  ⛔ *"Less successful responses often attempted to discuss more than one example – **time that might
  have been more profitably spent in Question 2(d)** where there were up to 15 marks available"*
  (s21/11).

## 4 · ⭐⭐ Q2(d) — LANGUAGE ANALYSIS [15 marks, content only]

The question Cambridge writes about at greatest length, and the one where the band table is least
informative. Six discriminators, in order of how often the reports name them.

### 4.1 Six choices — three from EACH named paragraph, and only from those paragraphs
> *"offered clear analysis of **six relevant selections – three from each paragraph**"* (s22/11)

⛔ *"a few candidates offered choices from paragraphs other than 8 and 11 in the language Question
2(d) and so **included explanations that could not be credited**"* (s24/11). Answers must also be
*"balanced across both parts of the question"* (s23/11).

### 4.2 ⭐ Choose what you can EXPLAIN — not the first three, and not the obvious devices · **14/16**
> *"**Rather than selecting the first three choices in each half they came across, or the most
> 'obvious' literary devices**, successful responses often set out to identify those relevant
> selections that they felt best able to explain."* (s22/11; s23/11 adds *"and discuss in some detail"*)

### 4.3 Meaning first, then effect — always in that order
> *"often beginning by explaining meaning and then moving on to consider effect"* (s21/11)
> *"consider the meanings of carefully chosen phrases in the context of the text and then consider
> the effect in terms of connotations and the atmosphere created"* (s21/12)

**This is the middle-band ceiling, stated outright:** *"Middle range responses were usually effective
when explaining meanings but **struggled to explore the effects fully**, and the least effective
responses tended to offer quotations… but struggled to find anything relevant to say about them"*
(s23/12).

### 4.4 ⭐⭐ GO INSIDE THE PHRASE — the single clearest top-band marker · **12/16 sessions**
> *"The strongest responses considered **words within their choices individually**, as well as
> suggesting how they worked within the longer phrase and/or in the context of the description as a
> whole."* (s21/11)
> *"selected phrases but also considered the individual words within them suggesting how they worked
> within the context of the whole language choice"* (s21/12, s23/12)
> *"Where candidates considered **all of the key words** in slightly longer choices they were able to
> avoid those more generalised comments of less effective responses."* (s21/11, s23/11)

Worked examples the examiners themselves give: *"taking opportunity to consider how **'sprawled'**
added to the sense of the imposing mountains stretched out across the horizon"*; *"the suggested
**latent threat in 'sleeping'**"* (both s23/11, on *'giant sleeping tarantulas'*).

### 4.5 ⭐ "TALK YOUR READER THROUGH IT" — explore possible meanings before landing on one
> *"The most successful answers were often able to **'talk their reader through' their understanding
> of words** within relevant choices, considering different possibilities of meaning, associations
> and connotations, **ahead of arriving at** an understanding of how and why these particular words
> might have been used by the writer in this context."* (s22/11, near-identical in s21/11, s21/13)

And the register of that exploration is named: *"Responses at level 5 frequently showed **imagination
and precision** when discussing choices"* (s21/11, s22/11, s23/11).
A useful entry route: *"Stronger answers were often able to **visualise the images**, using
explanation of the meaning/what you could 'see/hear happening' in context as the starting point"*
(s21/11).

### 4.6 Build to an overview — and reach the writer's purpose
> *"explored how their judiciously selected choices worked **both individually and together** to
> influence the reader's impression, **building to an overview**"* (s21/11, s23/11)
> *"consider meanings in context, as well as the effects of the powerful language identified,
> **demonstrating understanding of the writer's purpose in a clear overview**"* (s21/12, s22/12,
> s22/13, w22/11)

### 4.7 ⛔ What kills it — quoted, because these are the exact answers students write
- **Naming devices.** *"labelling of devices without explanation of how these were working in this
  instance meant opportunities to target higher levels were missed"* — **in 15 of the 16 sessions**,
  which makes it the most-repeated single criticism on Paper 1. And plainly: *"it is the **quality of the analysis** when considering how language is
  being used which attracts marks"* (s23/11).
- **Empty generics.** *"'The writer uses images that make us imagine what it was like to be there.' –
  such empty, generic comments are unlikely to be a useful starting point"* (s22/11); *"'the writer
  uses lots of adjectives and images in this paragraph'"* (s23/11).
- **Long quotation, thin comment.** *"very long quotations with general explanations, rather than
  engaging closely with specific words"* (s21/13, s23/12).
- **Recycling the text's own words in your explanation.** *"'confusing', complex' and 'giant' were
  often repeated"* (s23/11).
- **Note form** (s21/12, s23/12), and **answering it last**: *"appeared to have been answered last and
  were very brief, generalised and/or incomplete"* (s22/11).

## 5 · ⭐⭐ Q3 — EXTENDED RESPONSE IN ROLE [25 = 15 reading + 10 writing]

The biggest question on Paper 1, and 15 of its 25 marks are for **reading**.

### 5.1 ⭐⭐ It is a reading test wearing a writing costume · **15/16 sessions**
> *"The best responses **remembered this was a test of reading skills as well as writing**, responding
> carefully to details of Anna's experience as suggested by the passage, and **did not stray outside
> the bounds of the text** to offer unrelated generic training advice such as eating a carbohydrate
> high (or low) diet…"* (s23/11)

Same warning as *"inventing untethered material"* (key messages, four sessions) and *"sometimes
inventing material that moved too far away from the text itself"* (s23/12, s24/12).

### 5.2 ⭐⭐ Turn the narrator's REPORT into the character's JUDGEMENT
This is the top-band move, and it is what "evaluate" means on this question:
> *"The most convincing responses… indicated that candidates had **revisited the passage** to examine
> carefully the details of the narrator's account of events and **make judgements based on the
> evidence in the text about how Vivian might have presented them from her point of view**."* (s21/11)
> *"weaving in and extending some of his **excuses (as presented by the writer) to become
> explanations/justifications** (developments of his implied perspective)"* (s22/11)

### 5.3 ⭐ Write from hindsight — the character is looking back, and knows more now
> *"stronger answers provided **an evaluation of each challenge as seen through the perspective of a
> more experienced Vivian looking back post-tour**, having successfully completed her journey."*
> (s21/11)
> *"the best focused on interpreting the evidence in the text **with the benefit of hindsight**"*
> (s23/11)
> *"The most convincing answers had recognised the **evidently more positive attitude of the narrator
> by the end** of the piece and had clearly revisited the passage to examine carefully the details of
> the narrator's account of events leading up to that change."* (s22/11)

### 5.4 ⭐ Mine the implicit, and support every point with a specific detail
> *"higher level responses often included these and **other implicit ideas** such as the boat needed
> some work, and that Andy was perhaps **putting on a brave face** or trying to play down his fears"*
> (s23/13)
> *"The best responses **looked for details to support each** job, for example linking the role of
> mechanic to the mention of a **generator, sewage treatment plant, or ski repair workshop**."*
> (s21/12)

Contrast the named failure: *"used the ideas in the text **thinly**, often simply listing the jobs
available… without offering any further details"* (s21/12) — the same sentence recurs in s22/12,
s22/13, s23/12.

### 5.5 All three bullets, developed equally — the third is where answers collapse
> *"offering **a vague response to the third bullet** without offering any further details"* (s22/12)
> *"Some less effective responses only addressed one or two of the bullets."* (s23/12)
> *"Creating a plan prior to answering this question would help to ensure adequate coverage of each of
> the bullet points."* (s22/13)

And bullet 1 rewards looking *outside* its own paragraph: *"Stronger answers to bullet one offered
ideas beyond Chris' report… **noting details later in the text**"* (s21/11).

### 5.6 Voice = role + audience + form, and it must hold to the last line
> *"the best responses developing a convincing voice and tone for **her answers to the interview
> questions**"* (w21/11); *"an appropriately **lively and enthusiastic** tone and register"* for an article for young
> readers (s23/12); *"enthusiastic and encouraging **but also realistic**"* for a commander's talk
> (s21/12).
> ⛔ *"Lapses into narrative… indicated an **inconsistency of style** in less assured responses and
> missed opportunities to aim for higher levels."* (s21/13)

⭐ **Form-specific, for interviews:** *"The strongest answers often **took little time to get into the
answers** to the questions and allowed Vivian opportunity to answer each in some detail and at some
length"*, while *"Some less successful answers however were **drawn away from the text by their enthusiasm
to imitate a TV host**"* (s21/11). Any words you give the interviewer must still carry text detail
(s22/11).

### 5.7 ⛔ Also fatal: writing as the wrong person
*"a number **misread the instructions and wrote from Andy or Torries' perspective**"* (s23/13);
*"not all remembered that Veda was writing to **both** of her parents"* (w22/12).

# PAPER 2

## 6 · ⭐⭐ SECTION A — DIRECTED WRITING [40 = 15 reading + 25 writing]

### 6.1 ⭐⭐ THE LEVEL 6 MOVE: INTERROGATE THE TEXTS' ASSUMPTIONS · **15/16 sessions**

Not *agree or disagree with their conclusions* — **attack the reasoning underneath them.** This is
the clearest top-band discriminator anywhere on either paper, and the band table does not hint at it.
Every quotation below is an examiner describing a Level 5/6 reading mark:

> *"thoughtful **interrogation of the limits** of individual or collective actions as opposed to those
> actions available to governments and large companies"* (s21/21)
> *"**questioned its unvarying approbation** of online relationships as being **overly simplistic**"*
> (s21/22)
> *"challenged in some higher-level responses was that text's implication of **a simple binary
> division**"* (s21/22)
> *"thoughtful interrogation of Text A's claims of the **ease and economy** of implementing outdoor
> learning and its **blithe dismissal of concerns** about safety and practicality"* (s22/22)
> *"**challenged the underlying assertion** in Text A that the very desirable 'soft skills'… could
> only be found there"* (s22/22)
> *"thoughtful consideration of **the assumption** that delaying tasks and decisions was automatically
> harmful or that there was **some inherent virtue** in meeting arbitrarily set deadlines"* (w21/21)
> *"The **assumptions and generalisations underpinning** such views were sometimes interrogated
> thoughtfully"* (w21/22)

### 6.2 ⭐ Probe the text you AGREE with — that is where the marks are
> *"Although across the mark range more responses derived their evaluative points from Text B,
> **higher level evaluation was more often seen when probing Text A**, especially when laying bare its
> **idealised, romanticised** views of the natural world and its benefits."* (s22/22)

Most candidates instinctively attack the text they dislike. The examiners say the opposite pays.

### 6.3 The stance, named
> *"the most effective responses adopted a **consistently evaluative, critical stance** and **read
> effectively between the lines** of the texts, drawing inferences and making judgements"* (s21/23,
> s23/22)
> *"did not simply agree or disagree… but engaged with the passage by evaluating the material,
> inferring and expanding upon their ideas"* (s23/23)

⭐ **And synonyms are not evaluation:** *"the phrase… needed some probing and interpretation **beyond
paraphrasing or the use of synonyms** to show clear evaluation for Level 5 and 6"* (s21/23).

### 6.4 The floor is low — so no paragraph should be evaluation-free
> *"Where **even a single evaluative point was firmly made**, marks at the bottom of Level 5 could be
> awarded if there was otherwise reasonable coverage of the reading material."* (s23/22)

But the ceiling needs range and cohesion: *"Many responses missed the opportunity to be awarded a
mark in Level 6… because their relatively brief responses **did not develop a number of points
throughout to create an evaluative, cohesive argument**"* (w21/21).

### 6.5 The winning STRUCTURE — and it vindicates a compressed IUMVCC · **13/16 sessions**
> *"The opening and concluding sections of the most effective responses tended to **introduce and sum
> up the main points, with the intervening sections arguing a coherent case**."* (s21/23, s23/21,
> s23/22 — three sessions, same sentence)
> *"tended to reflect a range of points made in each text and were **reordered** in a response which
> was sensibly structured and paragraphed"* (Level 5 writing, s21/21, s21/22, s23/23)
> ⛔ *"Less effective responses sometimes struggled to provide a coherent argument and were **more
> tied to the sequencing of the texts**."* (s21/21, s23/22)

### 6.6 ⭐ NEVER LET THE AUDIENCE SEE "TEXT A" AND "TEXT B"
> *"less effective responses tended to **refer to the texts as Text A and B** with limited grasp of
> what the intended audience knew or understood"* (s21/21)
> *"a significant number of responses awarded Level 5 or even Level 6 for Reading made **inappropriate
> reference to the texts** in this way, **affecting their marks for Writing**"* (s21/22)

⭐ **Note the mechanism: it costs WRITING marks even when the reading is top-band.** Also fatal:
*"comparative analyses of the texts themselves rather than an evaluation of the ideas in them"*
(s22/22).

### 6.7 Register must survive to the last line, and precision beats ambition
> *"Letters often began appropriately but **lost the register and form of a letter before the end**."*
> (s22/21)
> *"Sometimes the friendly and conversational style of the opening was followed in the body… with an
> impersonal, discursive style **close to the language of the texts** and lacking convincing, direct
> address."* (s23/22)
> *"A few responses used sophisticated vocabulary, usually precisely, but **in an overly formal,
> discursive style which was less appropriate for the given audience**."* (s21/22)

**Level 6 writing, defined:** *"Accomplished writing which was accurate and controlled as well as
appropriate in tone and register"* … *"often **authoritative in style and convincing in their
arguments** but also **fluent and virtually free of error**"* (s21/21, s22/21, s23/23).
**Level 5:** *"purposeful and clear, though not as ambitious and wide ranging in vocabulary or as
precise in register or style"* (s21/21).

## 7 · SECTION B — COMPOSITION [40 = 16 content & structure + 24 style & accuracy]

Cambridge's own statement of the bar (s21/21, s23/21 key messages):
> *"In order to achieve high marks, candidates were required to: • use an appropriate form, style and
> register in both questions • **structure ideas and organise their writing effectively** to persuade and engage the reader
> • produce **detailed and evocative descriptions and engaging, credible narratives** • understand how
> different audiences, purposes and genres should influence the style adopted • **construct varied
> sentences accurately**, with a clear attempt to influence and interest the reader • use **precise and
> wide-ranging vocabulary**"*

### 7.1 DESCRIPTIVE — narrow the frame, then go deep

⭐ **The commonest cause of a mediocre content mark is too WIDE a subject, not too little vocabulary.**
> *"Many awarded marks in Level 4 did not achieve the 'convincing picture' required for Level 5
> because they attempted **far too wide an inclusion** of natural or geographical features for a
> limited scenario: sometimes a short walk along a path encountered **mountains, waterfalls, forests
> and many animals in uneasy juxtaposition**."* (w22/21)

And its mirror, at the top:
> *"Some very effective responses… **focused description narrowly on a small area of their room**,
> evoking the almost surreal transformation of the familiar in darkness and exhaustion"* (s21/22)
> *"The most effective descriptions **avoided these more general, stereotypical ideas and focused more
> closely on details and specific moments**."* (w21/22, w22/22)

⭐ **Describe the thing the question actually names.** *"a significant proportion of responses
described **not a group of people but a beach or a holiday resort**, or a holiday journey in
painstaking detail, so that Examiners were rarely able to award very high marks for Content and
Structure"* (s22/22).

**What earns Level 6:**
> *"Level 6 responses were characterised by **this cohesive structure** as well as **carefully chosen
> detail and striking images**."* (s21/22, s22/23, s23/21) — and the report names where the cohesion
> comes from: *"often provided by **the narrator's reactions or a specific atmosphere**"* (s23/21).
> *"unusual, closely observed details created convincing, evocative scenes in the best responses"*
> (s21/22, s23/22)
> *"an ability to use **both simple and complex** language and sentence structures to create subtle,
> complex atmospheres"* (s21/21)

**⛔ Named failure modes:** the **inventory** (*"rather like inventories, with items pictured but not
developed as 'well-chosen images'"*, s21/22; *"inventory-like, prosaic"*, s23/22); the **narrative
preamble** (*"lengthy narrative preambles to set the scene unbalanced the main focus"*, s21/21);
**value judgements instead of detail** (*"only the value judgements, 'amazing', 'fabulous' etc, were
employed rather than the detailed description"*, s23/22); and the **sensory checklist** —
> *"responses were not infrequently structured by categories of sensory images: 'I could hear…',
> 'I could smell...' **This mechanical approach**… generally did not produce a cohesive and engaging
> descriptive piece."* (w22/21)

### 7.2 NARRATIVE — a simple story, well told, driven by character

⭐⭐ **The most counter-intuitive finding in the whole corpus — but also the WEAKEST-EVIDENCED thing
in this document, and it should be taught as a strong tendency rather than a law: 4/16 sessions**
(s21, s22, s25, w25), against 15/16 for the findings above. It is stated flatly where it appears:
> *"As ever, **the best narratives were subtle and not full of action** – some candidates tried to
> **pack too much drama** into a short piece of writing, not always successfully."* (s21/23)
> *"the best narratives were often **a simple story well told**, such candidates did not try to pack
> too much drama into a short piece of writing, which often leads to an unsuccessful narrative."*
> (s22/23)

Proof by example — a Level 6 story with no drama at all: *"One of the Level 6 responses… **eschewed
drama entirely** and was a profoundly meditative account of a boy's growing relationship with his
grandfather, developed during their fishing trips to the local lake"* (s22/22).

⭐ **Character drives event — not the other way round:**
> *"While the events in a story were important in creating such credibility, **Level 6 responses paid
> attention to characterisation and how events were driven by character traits and choices**."*
> (s23/21)
> *"**Effective characterisation of the protagonist or narrator was often a factor in Examiners
> selecting a mark in Level 5 rather than Level 4**."* (s22/21; m22/22 repeats it, lower-casing
> *examiners*)

**Shaping and pace:**
> *"stories needed to be **well-managed with some conscious shaping of the narrative beyond a simple
> retelling of events**"* (s21/21, s22/21)
> *"moments of drama and tension were **carefully paced with more descriptive sections** and stories
> were **resolved in interesting ways**"* (s21/21)
> *"created tension and pace, supporting the narrative detail with the **deliberate manipulation of
> paragraph and sentence length** for effect"* (s23/22)

**Handling a given title or phrase:** *"well organised and often **original interpretations** of the
title"* (w22/22); *"the plot often **led up to the prompt, fully incorporating this** into the
narrative, leading to a suitable climax and conclusion"* (s23/23) — as opposed to *"the prompt being
included in what was **a simple a timeline of events**"* — the second article is the source's own slip.

⭐ **Restraint beats spectacle.** A top-band school-shooting story worked because the narrator was
hidden and could only hear: *"In this approach **the absence of horrific sights of violence was
convincing and effective**"* (s23/22).

### 7.3 ⭐⭐ THE 24 STYLE & ACCURACY MARKS — one error keeps more students out of Level 5 than any other

> *"A frequent reason for keeping an otherwise clearly written story out of Level 5 was **weak
> demarcation of sentences, most commonly the use of commas where full stops were needed**."*

That sentence appears, essentially verbatim, in **15 of the 16 sessions** — the examiners vary only
the adjective (*weak* · *insecure* · *ineffective* demarcation), and s25 says it twice in one report.
**It is the most-repeated statement in the entire corpus**, and the highest-value single correction
available on Paper 2.

The rest of the named list, in the examiners' order of frequency:
- *"The **correct punctuation of speech** was rare below Level 5."* (s22/21)
- *"The **omission of definite and indefinite articles**, the incorrect use of participles or errors in
  grammatical agreement contributed to a lack of fluency and accuracy which kept many responses out
  of Level 5."* (s21/22)
- *"**imprecise, sometimes over-ambitious vocabulary which obscured meaning**"* (s21/21) — and, for
  Section A, *"vocabulary was sometimes wide-ranging and complex but **used with less precision**"*
  (s21/21, w21/22). **Reach is only rewarded when it is accurate.**
- *"fluctuating tense use"* (s21/22)

**Level 6 style, defined:** *"a **fluent and varied use of language which was subtle enough to create a
range of effects** and helped to engage the reader"* (w22/22); *"A sophisticated and precise range of
vocabulary allowed Examiners to consider the highest marks for Style and Accuracy"* (s22/21).

# 8 · THE TEACHING IMPLICATIONS — what this changes about our protocols

Recorded here so a later port does not have to re-derive them. Each one traces to a section above.

1. ⭐⭐ **REGISTER ADAPTATION should be taught ONCE, as a shared move across Q1(f), Q3 and Section A**
   (§0.6) — 15/16 sessions, 80 of the 160 marks. This is the highest-leverage change on the list and
   it is not currently a step anywhere: no protocol asks the student to name the source's register,
   name the target's, and close the gap. Cheaper to build than three separate steps, and it teaches
   transfer rather than a per-question trick.
2. **Q2(d) needs a "go inside the phrase" step** (§4.4) and a "possible meanings before settled
   meaning" step (§4.5). Our marking must also refuse to credit device-labelling (§4.7).
3. **Q3's core move is REPORT → JUDGEMENT, written from hindsight** (§5.2–5.3). That is not TTECEA
   and it is not a generic "write in role" scaffold. It belongs in the planning ladder.
4. **Section A's Level 6 move is interrogating an ASSUMPTION** (§6.1). TOGA's *Weigh* is the right
   home for it, and the target of the weighing should be the premise, not the conclusion. §6.2 says
   to aim it at the text the student agrees with.
5. **The compressed IUMVCC ruling of 18 Aug is confirmed by the examiners' own words** (§6.5) —
   intro and conclusion frame, middle argues a case, material reordered.
6. **Section B teaching should push AGAINST the instinct to add drama** (§7.2) and against wide
   subjects (§7.1). Both are the opposite of what students assume.
7. **A comma-splice drill is worth more marks than any Section B content lesson** (§7.3).
8. **"Don't name Text A to your audience" is a hard rule, not a style note** (§6.6) — it costs
   writing marks at every level.
