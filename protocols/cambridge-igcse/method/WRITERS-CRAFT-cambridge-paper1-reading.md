# PART ONE: THE PAPER THAT NEVER CHANGES

Most students walk into Paper 1 hoping the texts will be kind. You will walk in knowing the
machine — because in six years and forty papers, the machine has not changed once.

**Three texts. One subject. Three ways of seeing it.**

| | what it is | what it's for |
|---|---|---|
| **Text A** | factual, informative | gives you the subject — facts, definitions |
| **Text B** | opinion, experience, or the career angle | gives you attitudes — **the summary lives here** |
| **Text C** | a narrative — a story or personal account | **half the paper's marks live here** — Q2 AND Q3 |

**Eighteen questions, same marks, every time:** Q1(a)–(e) short answers on Text A [15] · Q1(f)
the summary of Text B [15] · Q2(a)–(c) short answers on Text C [10] · Q2(d) the language question
[15] · Q3 the extended response [25]. Total 80. No exceptions in 40 papers.

**The reading strategy this dictates:** you get 15 minutes of reading in a 2-hour paper. Spend
them unequally — skim A for facts, read B asking "what does this writer *think*?", and read
**Text C slowly, twice**. Text C carries 50 of the 80 marks. The examiners add one more reason
to work in order: *"having worked through Question 2 and already familiarised themselves with
Text C, candidates following the order of tasks as set were best placed"* for Q3.

**The clock** *(Sophicly's derivation from the mark weights — the paper sets no timings; at 1.5
minutes per mark this paper is more generous than AQA, so there is time to do this properly):*

| task | marks | minutes |
|---|---|---|
| reading the insert | — | 15 |
| Q1(a)–(e) | 15 | 12 |
| Q1(f) summary | 15 | 18 |
| Q2(a)–(c) | 10 | 8 |
| Q2(d) language | 15 | 22 |
| Q3 extended response | 25 | **35** |
| final check | — | 10 |

The June 2024 examiner report found candidates writing their **Question 2(d)** answers *"longer
and more carefully crafted"* than their **25-mark Q3**. Effort spent inversely to reward. Never
you.

**Two marking facts that change behaviour everywhere on this paper:**
1. **Marks are never deducted.** Generic Marking Principle 3: *"marks are not deducted for
   errors … marks are not deducted for omissions."* Errors are ignored, not punished. Never
   waste time erasing — add the better answer.
2. **Unexpected valid answers MUST be credited.** *"All examiners are instructed that
   alternative correct answers and unexpected approaches … must be given marks that fairly
   reflect the relevant knowledge and skills demonstrated."* The only condition: *"the content
   must be clearly related to and derived from the texts."*

# PART TWO: CRAFT ONE — THE SUMMARY (Q1f)

**What it tests:** R1, R2, R5 — understanding explicit and implicit meaning, and *selecting for
a purpose*. 10 marks Reading + 5 Writing. It is a **selective** summary: the question names a
focus ("advantages and appeal", "reasons why", "difficulties faced") and only material serving
that focus exists for you.

## The tables you are marked on (verbatim)

**Table A, Reading — out of 10:**

| Level | Marks | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **5** | 9–10 | • A very effective response that demonstrates a thorough understanding of the requirements of the task. • Demonstrates understanding of a wide range of relevant ideas and is consistently well-focused. • **Points are skilfully selected to demonstrate an overview.** |
| **4** | 7–8 | • An effective response that demonstrates a competent understanding of the requirements of the task. • Demonstrates understanding of a good range of relevant ideas and is mostly focused. • Points are carefully selected and there is some evidence of an overview. |
| **3** | 5–6 | • A partially effective response that demonstrates a reasonable understanding of the requirements of the task. • Demonstrates understanding of ideas with occasional loss of focus. • Some evidence of selection of relevant ideas but may include excess material. |
| **2** | 3–4 | • A basic response that demonstrates some understanding of the requirements of the task. • Demonstrates general understanding of some relevant ideas and is sometimes focused. • There may be some indiscriminate selection of ideas. |
| **1** | 1–2 | • A response that demonstrates a limited understanding of the task. • The response may be a simple list of unconnected ideas or show limited focus. • There is limited evidence of selection. |

**Table B, Writing — out of 5** *(note: only THREE levels exist here — there is no "Level 5"
for summary writing):*

| Level | Marks | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **3** | 4–5 | • A relevant response that is expressed clearly, fluently and mostly with concision. • The response is well organised. • The response is in the candidate's own words (where appropriate), using a range of well-chosen vocabulary which clarifies meaning. |
| **2** | 2–3 | • A relevant response that is generally expressed clearly, with some evidence of concision. • There may be some lapses in organisation. • The response is mainly expressed in the candidate's own words but there may be reliance on the words of the text. |
| **1** | 1 | • A relevant response that lacks clarity and concision. • There may be excessively long explanations, or the response may be very brief. • The response may include lifted sections. |

**Read those tables like a student, and the whole method falls out:** the Reading marks are
LEVELLED, never tallied per point. A student can list twelve correct points and sit at Level 3
("some evidence of selection … excess material"). The Level 5 words are **range · consistent
focus · skilful selection · overview**. The Writing words are **concision · organisation · own
words**. Every move below serves one of those seven words.

## The Five Moves

### Move 1: The Question Filter
Underline ONLY what answers the exact question asked. "Advantages and appeal" means benefits —
a fascinating disadvantage is not merely unhelpful, it is a **focus** failure ("consistently
well-focused" is the Level 5 line it breaks).
- **Worked example (Hyperloop, real June 2024):** Text B praises speed and directness but also
  admits it "remains untested at scale". The doubt is TRUE and INTERESTING — and worth nothing,
  because the question asks what *supporters* consider its *advantages and appeal*.
- **Worked example (our ballooning pack):** "flights are cancelled whenever the wind is
  unsuitable" sits in the middle of the appeal paragraphs. It is a distractor. The mark scheme's
  own note for such tasks: points that "argued against the advantages or appeal" were the mark
  of *"less focused responses"*.
- **Application:** rewrite the question as a yes/no test before reading — *"does this sentence
  give me an advantage? "* — and run every paragraph through it.

### Move 2: The Over-Harvest, then the Selection
Find 12–15 candidate points. Then SELECT the set that covers the WHOLE text — early ideas, middle
ideas, late ideas — because grouped, whole-text selection is what the table calls an *overview*.
- **Why it works:** *"skilfully selected"* implies rejected alternatives. The examiners praised
  answers that *"grouped ideas together"* under umbrella points and noted the best candidates
  *"had produced and followed a bullet point plan"*.
- **Application:** plan as bullets, draw a neat line through the plan after writing (Cambridge's
  own advice), and check the plan reaches the final paragraph of the text — late points are the
  most commonly missed.

### Move 3: The Simultaneous Translation
Convert each point into your own words AS you harvest, not afterwards. The Writing table's
Level 2 line is *"reliance on the words of the text"*; Level 1 is *"lifted sections"*.
- **Nuance from the examiners:** you are *"not expected to change all key words"* — technical
  terms (Hyperloop, propane, dog-sled) stay; it is the surrounding prose that must be yours.

### Move 4: The Compression Engine
Combine grouped points into single sentences. *"It is faster, cheaper and produces less
pollution than air travel"* = three points, eleven words. Concision is a named criterion of the
Writing table ("mostly with concision") — and compression is also what makes RANGE fit in 120
words, which feeds the Reading mark.

### Move 5: The Cold Open
One flowing paragraph. No "In this text, the writer discusses…", no bullets, no conclusion —
examiners record that excess *"often arose from … an unnecessary introduction or conclusion"*
and from *"attempts to comment on the writer's viewpoint"*. A summary contains no opinion and no
frame. **Start with the first point.**

**What actually happens past 120 words (the mechanism, not a myth):** nobody stops reading and
nothing is deducted. The cost is built into the tables: a wordy answer is not *"consistently
well-focused"* (Reading L5), fails *"concision"* (Writing), and the report names over-length
answers as *"missed opportunities to target higher marks"* — plus every extra minute is stolen
from the 25-mark Q3. Enforcement is real; it works through the descriptors, not scissors. Do
not count words in the exam (examiners call it wasted time) — rehearse until you know what 120
words of your handwriting looks like.

## A MODEL AT FULL MARKS' SHAPE
*(real June 2024 task: "According to Text B, what might supporters of the Hyperloop consider to
be its advantages and appeal?" — 99 words, covering all 13 of the mark scheme's indicative
points, grouped into umbrella sentences)*

> Supporters believe the Hyperloop would transform travel between cities: it is extremely fast,
> takes the most direct route – climbing over or passing beneath obstacles – and so saves
> considerable journey time. It is also greener than existing transport, being magnet-powered
> and less polluting, yet cheap to run. They argue the science is sound and the technology
> already proven, so the system would operate reliably and efficiently. Passengers would travel
> in comfort, while its futuristic, elegant design adds to its appeal. Beyond carrying people,
> it could transport cargo, and its potential to boost trade between countries makes it a
> promising investment.

**Why it is Level 5 + Level 3:** cold open · every sentence an umbrella holding 2–3 grouped
points · whole-text range (the opening claims through to the cargo/investment ideas at the end)
· own words with technical terms kept · zero comment, zero frame. That grouped, whole-text
selection IS the "overview".

**And the same task at Level 3, so you can hear the difference:**

> *In this text the writer talks about the Hyperloop and its advantages. The Hyperloop is a new
> form of transport which is very fast. It is faster than other transport. It is also good for
> the environment because it uses magnets. Some people think it will not work but supporters
> disagree with this. It would be comfortable for the passengers to travel in…*

The tells: a wasted opening frame · repetition ("very fast… faster") · a distractor dragged in
("some people think it will not work") · one-point-per-sentence, no grouping · the text's own
sequence followed start to finish. Same knowledge, five marks fewer.

# PART THREE: CRAFT TWO — THE LANGUAGE QUESTION (Q2d)

**What it tests:** *"the ability to select powerful or unusual words and … an understanding of
ways in which the language is effective."* 15 marks, content only. Cambridge names two
paragraphs of Text C, tells you what each is about, and demands **three choices from each,
including imagery**, in about 200–300 words.

## The table you are marked on (verbatim) — out of 15

| Level | Marks | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **5** | 13–15 | • Wide-ranging discussion of judiciously selected language with some high-quality comments that add meaning and associations to words/phrases **in both parts of the text** and demonstrate the writer's reasons for using them. • Tackles imagery with some precision and imagination. • There is clear evidence that the candidate understands how language works. |
| **4** | 10–12 | • Explanations are given of carefully selected words and phrases. • Explanations of meanings within the context of the text are secure and effects are identified **in both parts of the text**. • Images are recognised as such and the response goes some way to explaining them. • There is some evidence that the candidate understands how language works. |
| **3** | 7–9 | • A satisfactory attempt is made to select appropriate words and phrases. • The response mostly gives meanings of words and any attempt to suggest and explain effects is basic or very general. • **One half of the text may be better addressed than the other.** |
| **2** | 4–6 | • The response provides a mixture of appropriate choices and words that communicate less well. • The response may correctly identify linguistic devices but not explain why they are used. • Explanations may be few, general, slight or only partially effective. • They may repeat the language of the original or do not refer to specific words. |
| **1** | 1–3 | • The choice of words is sparse or rarely relevant. • Any comments are inappropriate, and the response is very thin. |

**And the mark scheme's operational rule, verbatim:** *"Mark holistically for the overall
quality of the response, **not for the number of words chosen**, bearing in mind that for the
higher bands there should be a range of choices … and that this should include the ability to
explain images."*

## The shape: six micro-analyses, no essay

This is not an essay. It is **six micro-analyses** (~40–50 words each), no introduction, no
conclusion, no essay furniture. If you learned a three-paragraph analytical structure for
another board, leave it at the door — three developed paragraphs run to ~450 words of
scaffolding this task never rewards. The 200–300 guidance is advisory, but it describes the
winning shape exactly: **6 × (short exact quotation → precise meaning in context → effect)**.

## The Four Moves

### Move 1: Three-of-Six, Both Halves, Always
Three choices from EACH paragraph, coverage even. The Level 3 line is written for the student
who forgets: *"one half of the text may be better addressed than the other."* An unbalanced
answer ceilings at Level 3 however brilliant its best half. And choices from the WRONG
paragraphs *"could not be credited"* at all (June 2024, it happened). → Bullet-check paragraph
numbers before writing; 3+3, tick as you go.

### Move 2: Meaning → Connotation → Effect — the ladder, in order
Level 4 requires *"explanations of meanings within the context of the text are secure"* — so
START from precise meaning, then associations, then effect.
- **Worked example (our pack, Text C paragraph 8):** *"a silence so deep it felt like a held
  breath"* — 'held breath' literally means air kept in, unreleased (meaning); it carries
  suspense, deliberateness, the moment before something happens (connotation); so the sky's
  quiet becomes expectant rather than empty — the reader waits with her (effect).
- **The Level 2 tell, from the table itself:** naming the device without the why — *"may
  correctly identify linguistic devices but not explain why they are used."* 'This is a simile'
  earns the least valuable sentence in the answer.

### Move 3: The Image X-Ray
For each image, name what is compared to what, then say what the comparison *smuggles in*.
Imagery is compulsory ("your choices should include the use of imagery") and the Level 5 verb is
*"tackles imagery with some precision and imagination"*.
- **Worked example (paragraph 10):** *"the envelope sighing down … like something falling
  asleep"* — the deflating balloon is compared to a creature ending its day: 'sighing' gives it
  breath and tiredness, 'falling asleep' makes the ending peaceful and trusted. After the
  violence of the dragged landing, the machine is forgiven — the writer loves it again.

### Move 4: Reach — your writing is not being marked here
Cambridge, directly: candidates should *"work at the very edges of their vocabulary range …
rather than limiting their answer to those words they are sure they can spell correctly."*
Writing skills are NOT assessed in 2(d). Spelling errors in this answer cost nothing; a timid
word choice costs precision.

## A MODEL PAIR — the same quotation at Level 2 and Level 5

> **Level 2:** "The writer uses a simile, 'straight as a pencil line'. This is effective because
> it helps the reader imagine the smoke."
>
> **Level 5:** "'Straight as a pencil line' turns the farmhouse smoke into something drawn
> rather than drifting: a pencil line is deliberate, thin and perfectly controlled, so the
> comparison tells us the morning air is utterly windless. The world below seems sketched and
> still unfinished — which makes the balloon's silent movement through it feel almost like
> trespass."

Same quotation. The first names a device and asserts effectiveness; the second climbs the whole
ladder — meaning ('deliberate, thin, controlled'), the physical implication (windless), and the
reader-effect (stillness, trespass) — and shows *"the writer's reasons for using them"*.

# PART FOUR: CRAFT THREE — THE EXTENDED RESPONSE (Q3)

**What it tests:** R1, R2, **R3** — *"analyse, evaluate and develop facts, ideas and opinions,
using appropriate support from the text."* 15 marks Reading + 10 Writing. You write 250–350
words **in role, in a named form**, answering three bullets.

## The tables you are marked on (verbatim)

**Reading — out of 15** *(read the last line of every level):*

| Level | Marks | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **5** | 13–15 | • The response reveals a thorough evaluation and analysis of the text. • Developed ideas are sustained and well related to the text. • A wide range of ideas is applied. • There is supporting detail throughout, well integrated, contributing to a strong sense of purpose and approach. • All three bullets are well covered. • **A consistent and convincing voice is used.** |
| **4** | 10–12 | • A competent reading of the text with some evidence of basic evaluation or analysis. • A good range of ideas is evident. • Some ideas are developed but may not be consistently sustained. • Frequent, helpful supporting detail, contributing to a clear sense of purpose. • All three bullets are covered. • **An appropriate voice is used.** |
| **3** | 7–9 | • The text has been read reasonably well. • A range of straightforward ideas is offered. • Opportunities for development are rarely taken. • Supporting detail is present but there may be some mechanical use of the text. • There is uneven focus on the bullets. • **The voice is plain.** |
| **2** | 4–6 | • Some evidence of general understanding of the main ideas; the response may be thin or lack focus. • Brief, straightforward reference to the text. • There may be reliance on lifting. • One of the bullets may not be addressed. • **The voice might be inappropriate.** |
| **1** | 1–3 | • Very general, with little reference to the text, or a reproduction of sections of the original. • Content insubstantial or unselective. • Little realisation of the need to modify material. |

**Writing — out of 10:**

| Level | Marks | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **5** | 9–10 | • Effective register for audience and purpose. • The language sounds convincing and consistently appropriate. • Ideas firmly expressed in a wide range of effective and/or interesting language. • Structure and sequence sound throughout. |
| **4** | 7–8 | • Some awareness of appropriate register. • Language mostly fluent, clear. • Sufficient vocabulary range for subtlety and precision. • Mainly well structured and sequenced. |
| **3** | 5–6 | • Language clear but comparatively plain and/or factual, expressing little opinion. • Ideas rarely extended; explanations adequate. • Some sections well sequenced; flaws in structure. |
| **2** | 3–4 | • Some awkwardness of expression, inconsistency of style. • Language too limited for shades of meaning. • Structural weakness; some copying. |
| **1** | 1–2 | • Expression and structure lack clarity. • Language weak, undeveloped. • Very little attempt to explain ideas. • Frequent copying. |

**The two facts most students never learn:**
1. **VOICE IS SCORED IN THE READING TABLE.** Every reading level ends on voice — "consistent
   and convincing" → "appropriate" → "plain" → "might be inappropriate". Sounding like the
   character IS evidence of reading, because a convincing voice can only be built from what the
   text implies about that person. Optimise voice and you are optimising 15 marks, not 10.
2. **There are no form-specific descriptors anywhere.** No table mentions salutations,
   headlines, datelines or sign-offs. Form conventions matter only as the *means* to
   *"effective register for audience and purpose"* — wear the form, don't perform it.

## The Four Moves

### Move 1: The Bullet Contract
All three bullets compulsory, **equal attention** — reorder or interleave freely (examiners
allow it; coverage is what they check — "uneven focus on the bullets" is the Level 3 line, a
missed bullet is Level 2). Before writing, find 3–4 details in Text C for EACH bullet. Details
first, prose second.
- **Worked planning example (Majd's real paper — Mike's report, three bullets):** bullet 1
  (what happened + safety measures) ← the runaway start, the brake, the commands, the evening
  routines; bullet 2 (what tourists enjoy) ← the silence, the husky bond, the landscapes, Lyn's
  delight; bullet 3 (dogs are well treated + why sledding matters) ← the dogs' eagerness, their
  care, the low-impact life of the hosts. Eleven details banked before the first sentence.

### Move 2: Development and Evaluation, Not Copying
The reading marks reward details **developed** — extended, explained, consequence added — and at
the top, **evaluated** (R3): the role-holder weighing and judging. Level 1 is *"a reproduction
of sections of the original"*; Level 3's warning is *"mechanical use of the text"*. The
character knows things the text implies; write from inside that knowledge.

### Move 3: The Voice Decision
Before the first sentence, decide: WHO am I (role) · WHO am I addressing (audience) · WHAT do I
sound like (register). **The register is set by the TASK, never by a house style.** Measured
across all 40 papers: "formal" is specified in exactly ONE task (June 2024's report — the only
one in six years); "persuade" in five. Everything else leaves register to be inferred — and the
examiners' praise-phrase is *"an appropriate register for their imagined audience"*.

### Move 4: Answer the Task Set, Not a Nearby One
June 2024's casualties, verbatim: candidates *"writing letters back home from Lyn, presenting
the words of speeches to those thinking of taking a holiday in Alaska, or scripting TV
interviews with Mike"* — instead of Mike's report. Wrong form, wrong role or wrong perspective
closes *"the widest range of opportunities"*. Read the task twice; name role + audience + form
in the margin.

## THE SIX FORMS — frequency measured across 40 papers, each with its register and a model opening

| form | n | register + conventions | model opening (in role, from real task contexts) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **speech/talk** | 11 | direct address to the named audience, rhetorical build, a close that lands | *"You're here because you're thinking about the trek. Good. Let me tell you what the brochure can't."* |
| **letter** | 8 | salutation matched to the recipient — warm for family/friends, measured for authority; sign-off in register | *"Dear Mum, You'll never guess where I'm writing this from — a camp bed, halfway up a mountain, happier than I've been in years."* |
| **journal** | 7 | first person, past tense, dated, PRIVATE — honesty the writer would show no one; feelings that change across the entry | *"Tuesday, late. I keep telling everyone the flight went 'fine'. This journal gets the truth: for the first minute I was terrified."* |
| **interview** | 7 | Q&A format or continuous with embedded questions; SPOKEN register — contractions, hesitations used deliberately; answers that develop the text | *"— People call your job dangerous. Are they wrong? — No, they're half right. But the danger isn't where they think it is."* |
| **article** | 5 | headline + hook; journalist's engaging register unless the role says otherwise | *"THE QUIET REVOLUTION — Nobody noticed when the world's slowest form of transport became its most wanted ticket."* |
| **report** | 2 | formal, headed, neutral-to-reassuring register, addressed to whoever commissioned it, recommendations at the end | *"Report to: The Ultimate Experience Travel Company. Subject: the evening expedition of 12 March and our safety procedures."* |

**Journal and interview are the classic blind spots** — commoner than students assume, almost
never rehearsed. (The practice programme routes real papers by these forms —
`PRACTICE-PROGRAMME-real-papers-by-theme.md`; our Practice Paper 1's Q3 is a journal for
exactly this reason.)

# PART FIVE: THE SHORT-ANSWER DISCIPLINE (Q1a–e, Q2a–c)

Rules from the mark scheme and the examiners' own casualty lists:
1. **Answers come from the text — never from your own knowledge, opinion or experience.**
   And obey line/paragraph references absolutely: a brilliant answer from the wrong paragraph
   *"cannot be credited"*.
2. **Marks are never deducted — but hedging earns nothing.** An answer that *"tries to cover
   all possibilities"* or self-contradicts kills its own mark. One committed answer.
3. **No circular answers:** *"repeating some or all the language of the question where own words
   were specified"* = zero evidence. Synonym the key terms.
4. **Explain the WHOLE phrase.** June 2024: explaining 'unusual' but repeating 'types' = 1 of 2.
   Both strands, every phrase.
5. **Q2(a): the exact word or phrase ONLY — no sentence.** Cambridge: *"it is not necessary to
   write answers to Question 2(a) in full sentences"*; copying the question out *"added
   unnecessary time pressure"*. The trap on the other side: one extra word beyond the underlined
   meaning ('wafted', 'consciously') loses the mark.
6. **Q2(c): ONE example, three distinct points about it.** Discussing a second example is
   *"time that might have been more profitably spent in Question 2(d)"*.
7. **Mark tags size the answer:** [1] = one precise phrase · [2] = one item, both strands ·
   [3] = three distinct ideas, or two developed.
8. **SPaG is not assessed on Paper 1** outside the two writing tables (summary /5, Q3 /10) —
   never burn checking time on spelling in short answers.

# PART SIX: PRACTICE, MODELS AND THE TRAPS

- **The complete trap list** — 26 documented mark-losers with Cambridge's verbatim words:
  `CAMBRIDGE-TRAPS-what-loses-marks.md`. Read it as the anti-method.
- **A full practice paper in exact exam structure** with a mark scheme keyed to these methods:
  `PACK-P1-paper01-travel-*`. Majd's own June 2024 paper (0990/11) is the reference sitting.
- **Real papers routed by theme and Q3 form** (journals and interviews starred):
  `PRACTICE-PROGRAMME-real-papers-by-theme.md`.

**The drills:**
1. **Summary sprints:** Text B of any real insert → harvest 12–15 points in 10 minutes → SELECT
   the whole-text set → 120 words in 8. The selection step is the levelled skill.
2. **Effect ladders:** one image → precise meaning in context → connotations → effect. Three
   rungs, in order, every time.
3. **The 3+3 discipline:** every 2(d) practice marked first for balance (three per paragraph,
   even depth) before quality.
4. **Form transplants:** same Text C, same bullets — write the journal, then the interview.
   The details stay; the voice changes. That isolation is the skill.
5. **The clock rehearsal:** one full real paper to the minute-table, weekly.
