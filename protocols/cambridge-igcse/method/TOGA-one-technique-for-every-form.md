# TOGA — ONE TECHNIQUE FOR EVERY FORM
## Writing in role, Cambridge IGCSE First Language English (0500 / 0990)
### Paper 1 Question 3 [25] and Paper 2 Section A [40]. Every move below is derived from the published mark schemes, and the descriptor each one serves is quoted beside it. Companion volumes: *Form Mastery — Paper 1 Q3* (six model answers) · *Form Mastery — Paper 2 Section A* (three model answers) · *The Complete Reader's Craft* · *The Complete Writer's Craft*.

**⚠️ For Abdullah, before this is taught:** the four moves are derived from the tables and are not
open to preference. **The NAME is.** "TOGA" is a proposal — it is memorable for teenagers and it
carries the one idea that matters most here (the form is a costume you put on over the same
writing), but it is yours to rule on. Everything else in this document stands whatever it is called.

# PART ONE — WHY ONE TECHNIQUE IS POSSIBLE AT ALL

Students meet six different forms on Paper 1 Question 3 — **report · journal · interview · speech ·
letter · article** — and three more on Paper 2 Section A. It looks like six things to learn. It is
not, and the mark scheme is unusually blunt about why.

### Finding 1: there is no form-specific descriptor anywhere

Question 3's writing table asks for *"effective register for audience and purpose"* and says
**nothing** about salutations, headlines, datelines, sign-offs or sub-headings. No level mentions
any form by name. The same is true of Paper 2 Section A's writing table.

⇒ **The form is worth about five lines** — the opening, the closing, and the register held in
between. It is a costume. Underneath it, all nine tasks are the same job: *use this text, in this
voice, for this reader.*

### Finding 2: the bullets are the structure, and coverage is marked

Question 3's **reading** table — 15 of its 25 marks — ladders on bullet coverage:

| Level | Marks | What it says about the bullets |
|---|---|---|
| **5** | 13–15 | *"All three bullets are well covered."* |
| **4** | 10–12 | *"All three bullets are covered."* |
| **3** | 7–9 | *"There is uneven focus on the bullets."* |
| **2** | 4–6 | *"One of the bullets may not be addressed."* |

Examiners annotate the script per bullet area. **So the student never has to invent a structure —
Cambridge hands them one, every time, and pays for using it.** Three bullets, three sections. Paper 2
Section A does the same with two bullets.

⚠️ **This is why an imported six-part essay scaffold hurts here.** Laying six sections over three
given bullets is precisely how a script earns *"uneven focus on the bullets"* — Level 3. The
structure is already on the question paper; the skill is what goes *inside* each bullet.

### Finding 3: voice is scored in the READING table, not the writing one

Every single reading level ends on voice: *"A consistent and convincing voice"* (L5) → *"An
appropriate voice"* (L4) → *"The voice is plain"* (L3) → *"The voice might be inappropriate"* (L2).

⇒ A student who assumes voice is a writing mark is **optimising the wrong ten marks**. Voice is
worth more than the entire writing table's structure bullet.

### Finding 4: the failure that costs most is not developing

Level 3's defining line is *"Opportunities for development are rarely taken."* Level 1's is *"a
reproduction of sections of the original"* and *"little realisation of the need to modify material
from the text."* Between those two sits almost every mark a student loses on this question.

# PART TWO — THE FOUR MOVES

**Run them once per bullet.** Three bullets on Paper 1 Q3; two on Paper 2 Section A.

| move | what the student does | the descriptor it serves |
|---|---|---|
| **T — Take it** | Lift a **specific detail** out of the text: an object, a number, a moment, a phrase. | *"There is supporting detail throughout, which is well integrated"* (L5) |
| **O — Own it** | Say it again **in the role's own words.** Never copy the text's sentence. | L1: *"a reproduction of sections of the original"*; L2: *"some reliance on lifting from the text"* |
| **G — Grow it** | Add what this person **would know, think or feel** that the text only implies. | L5: *"Developed ideas are sustained"*; L3: *"Opportunities for development are rarely taken"* |
| **A — Aim it** | Point it at **this reader, for this purpose** — which is where voice and register live. | L5: *"a strong sense of purpose"*, *"a consistent and convincing voice"* |

**Why the order matters.** Take before Own stops invention (a beautiful answer that floats free of
the text scores Level 1 on reading). Own before Grow stops copying. Grow before Aim stops the flat,
dutiful summary that covers all three bullets and still lands on Level 3.

**The budget.** Q3 is 250–350 words across three bullets — about **100 words per bullet**, which is
roughly four sentences. One move each. It fits; a six-part essay structure does not.

## The engine in one paragraph, from a real model

From the **journal** model, where Karl writes privately about Marta's first flight:

> She stood there with both hands on the rim, white to the knuckles, and I have learned to say
> nothing in that moment — a nervous trainee who is told to hurry never comes back.

- **Take** — the text gives *"my knuckles whitened on the rim of the basket."*
- **Own** — it comes back as *"white to the knuckles"*: the same fact, his words, not hers.
- **Grow** — *"I have learned to say nothing… never comes back"* is nowhere in the text. It is what
  nineteen years of instructing would teach a man, and it is the sentence that earns the development mark.
- **Aim** — a journal is written to nobody, so he admits what he would not say aloud.

Four moves, one sentence. That is the whole technique.

# PART THREE — THE ONE VARIABLE BETWEEN THE TWO PAPERS

The four moves are identical on both papers. **The third one swaps its verb**, because the two
reading tables ladder on different things:

| | Paper 1 · Question 3 | Paper 2 · Section A |
|---|---|---|
| the ladder is built on | **developing** the text's ideas in role | **evaluating** two texts against each other |
| L5/L6 wording | *"Developed ideas are sustained and well related to the text"* | *"Successfully evaluates ideas and opinions, both explicit and implicit"* · *"Assimilates ideas"* |
| what L3 looks like | *"Opportunities for development are rarely taken"* | *"Selects and comments on explicit ideas"* |
| so **G** becomes | **Grow it** — add what the role would know | **Weigh it** — judge the claim, and say which text is more convincing and why |

**On Paper 2 Section A, Grow becomes Weigh.** Same beat, same position, one verb. From the Paper 2
letter model:

> The teacher who describes marking homework "visibly written at midnight after a six-hour shift"
> is describing a rota problem, not a work problem.

**Take** the teacher's phrase · **Own** it by framing rather than repeating · **Weigh** it — that
final clause is a verdict on what the evidence actually shows · **Aim** it at a headteacher deciding
policy.

⭐ **IUMVCC AND TOGA COMPOSE — they are not alternatives (Neil, 2026-08-18).** IUMVCC is the
**whole-piece skeleton** the student already knows for non-fiction (Introduction · Urgency ·
Main arguments · Vivid examples · Counter-argument · Conclusion). TOGA is the **sentence engine that
runs inside each of those sections**. Use both on Section A: IUMVCC decides what each part of the
piece is FOR; TOGA decides what happens in the sentences.

It is also the better skeleton for this paper than it first looks, because Section A's worst trap is
Writing Level 3 — *"Relies on the sequence of the original text"* — and IUMVCC is argument-led by
construction, so it immunises against exactly that.

**Two conditions, both from the tables:** compress it (250–350 words over six sections is one or two
sentences each, not paragraphs), and **source the evidence** — on this paper *Vivid examples* must
come from BOTH given texts and be weighed, because 15 of the 40 marks ride on that. The
*Counter-argument* slot is the natural home for the weaker text's claim.

# PART FOUR — THE SAME ENGINE, WEARING SIX COSTUMES

All six Paper 1 models are written from the **same passage** — Marta's first balloon flight. What
changes is only the costume and where the *Grow* comes from. Read across the table: the facts never
move.

| form | the costume (≈5 lines) | what *Grow* adds in this form |
|---|---|---|
| **Report** *(2 of 40)* | Headed *Report to / Subject / Prepared by*; sectioned; no salutation; formal throughout | Professional judgement — *"This is within normal parameters"* |
| **Journal** *(7 of 40)* | Dated, past tense, written to nobody | The admission he would never make aloud — *"I will not sleep"* |
| **Interview** *(7 of 40)* | The three bullets **are** the three questions; spoken register, contractions, asides | Personality and unhurried opinion — *"Not fearlessness — I'd be suspicious of that"* |
| **Speech** *(11 of 40)* | Direct address from line one; a close built to be remembered | The argument the audience should leave holding — *"Come and stand in the field once"* |
| **Letter** *(8 of 40)* | Salutation and sign-off; register set entirely by the recipient | The relationship — what this reader in particular needs to hear |
| **Article** *(5 of 40)* | Headline, standfirst, journalist's third person | Context and distance — *"Landings are the part outsiders misread"* |

**The drill this makes possible — the form transplant.** Do the reading work **once**: pick the
detail for each bullet, and decide the Grow. Then write the task's form. Then re-cast the *same four
moves* as a second form in twenty minutes, with no new reading. What survives the transplant is
everything that carries marks; what changes is five lines.

# PART FIVE — HOW TO TEACH IT

**Session 1 — prove the costume claim.** Give the student the report model and the journal model
side by side and ask what actually changed. They will say *everything*. Then show them that the
facts are identical — the fans, the tether, the three level minutes, the hedge — and only the voice
moved. This is the lesson that makes the other five forms stop being frightening.

**Session 2 — one bullet, four moves.** Take a single bullet from a real Q3 task. Write only that
bullet, four sentences, labelling T-O-G-A in the margin. Do it three times with three different
details before ever writing a whole answer.

**Session 3 — hunt the missing G.** Give the student a competent Level-3 answer (covers all three
bullets, lifts detail accurately, adds nothing) and ask them to find where development should have
gone. This is the most valuable twenty minutes on this question, because Level 3 → Level 5 is almost
entirely this one move.

**Session 4 — the transplant.** As above: same reading, two forms, twenty minutes each.

**Marking shorthand for the tutor.** In the margin, mark **T · O · G · A** where each move fires, and
circle the bullet numbers. A script that shows T and O but no G is sitting at Level 3, and now the
student can see exactly why.
