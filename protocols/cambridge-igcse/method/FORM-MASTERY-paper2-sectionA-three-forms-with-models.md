# FORM MASTERY — PAPER 2 SECTION A
## The three forms — letter, speech, article — with a full model answer for each
### Cambridge IGCSE 0500 / 0990. Every model is written from ONE shared pair of texts so you can see exactly what changes between forms and what does not. Companion volumes: *The Complete Writer's Craft — Paper 2* (the method) · *The Past-Questions Compendium — Paper 2* (every real task) · *The Traps*.

**Why one pair of texts, three forms.** Section A has used exactly three forms in six years —
**letter 17 · speech 12 · article 11 — nothing else ever**. The marks, though, barely notice the
form: the Reading table (15 marks) and the Writing table (25 marks) are identical whichever form
the task names. What the form controls is **register** (a whole bullet of every Writing level) and
the **first and last five lines**. So the cure for form-anxiety is the same as Paper 1's: watch the
SAME material — the same two texts, the same evaluation — become three different pieces, and notice
how little actually changes. Every model below is built from the two texts of Sophicly Practice
Paper 1 (`PACK-P2-paper01-work-INSERT.md` — a balanced article on students taking part-time jobs,
and *Saturday girl*, the blog of Amara, 17, who works in a bakery). Read those texts first.

**What every model does, whatever the form** — these are the marks, and they do not move:

- **Weaves both texts around its OWN argument — never walks through them.** The Writing table's
  Level 3 says, in Cambridge's own words: *"Relies on the sequence of the original text."* A
  *Text A says… Text B says…* tour caps writing at 10–13 of 25 however elegant the sentences.
- **Surfaces IMPLICIT ideas.** The Reading table's top two levels both demand evaluation of ideas
  *"both explicit and implicit"*; Level 4 is *"mainly explicit"*. The idea a text does not quite
  say out loud is the boundary between the top two levels and everything below.
- **Evaluates with verdict verbs** — *overstates, concedes, proves from experience, converges on* —
  and **assimilates**: the Level 6 word. The texts' ideas are digested into the argument until you
  cannot see the join.
- **Covers both bullets, and builds bullet 2 from the surviving ideas.** The advice/view the task
  asks for must come from what your evaluation left standing, not from nowhere.
- **~250–350 words, in the register the form demands, sustained to the last line.**
- ⚠️ **No language analysis.** Paper 2 does not test how writers achieve effects (that is Paper 1's
  R4, and it is not assessed here). A sentence like *the writer uses a rule of three to engage the
  reader* earns nothing in Section A — evaluate what the texts *claim*, never how they are written.

# PART ONE — WHAT CHANGES AND WHAT MUST NOT

| | **letter** | **speech** | **article** |
|---|---|---|---|
| you are talking to | one named person | a room, directly | readers who can stop reading |
| opening | salutation + purpose (formal) or warm hook (friend) | a hook in line one, addressed to "you" | headline, then a first line that earns line two |
| register | measured and precise (authority) / warm, contractions fine (friend) | rhetorical: questions, threes, we/you | engaging but controlled; journalist's poise |
| the verdict sits | stated early, argued through | built to, landed in the close | threaded through, sealed in the last line |
| close | formal sign-off / wish + name | a line built to be remembered | circle back to the hook |

**What must not change:** the Two-Voices map, the convergence you found, the verdict you reached,
and the evidence you weighed. Work those out ONCE, before you know the form. The form is the
costume; the evaluation is the body.

**The letter has two registers — check who you are writing to.** Of the 17 real letter tasks,
**9 address a friend or relative** (warm, confiding, contractions) and **8 address an authority** —
a headteacher (five times), city authorities, a boss, another family's parents (measured, precise,
no contractions). The friend-letter model lives in *The Complete Writer's Craft — Paper 2*, Part
Two — this volume deliberately models the OTHER register, because the formal letter is the one
students practise least and Cambridge sets almost as often.

# PART TWO — THE FORMAL LETTER (to an authority)

**8 of 17 letter tasks.** The recipient decides everything: state your purpose in the first
sentence, argue in measured prose, propose rather than demand, and sign off formally. Warmth is
replaced by precision — but the verdict verbs and the woven evidence are exactly the same as in
the friend letter.

## MODEL — LETTER TO A HEADTEACHER
*Task (written in the real Cambridge style, on our two texts): The headteacher of your school is
concerned that part-time jobs may be damaging students' studies, and has asked for students'
views before deciding what the school should do. Write a letter to your headteacher, giving your
views. In your letter you should: • evaluate the ideas, attitudes and opinions given in both
texts • give your views on what the school should do about students who work part-time.*

> Dear Mrs Okafor,
>
> You asked for students' views on part-time working, and I am writing to offer mine: the school
> should guide working students rather than restrict them. **[form: purpose and position in the
> first sentence — argument-led from line one]**
>
> The case against jobs is real, but narrower than it first appears. The research does not show
> that work harms study; it shows that too many hours do. Below roughly ten hours a week, working
> students match or better their classmates; beyond that, grades slip and sleep shrinks. The
> teacher who describes marking homework "visibly written at midnight after a six-hour shift" is
> describing a rota problem, not a work problem. **[evaluation: the evidence reframed — an
> implicit idea surfaced, not a summary]**
>
> What persuades me most is that the sternest warning and the warmest recommendation agree. The
> article concludes that the difference is not the job but "the limits set around it"; the student
> who blogs about her bakery Saturdays has reached the same rule by experience — one day, never
> two, exam weeks untouchable — and her grades rose after she started, precisely because the job
> forced her to plan. When report and lived experience converge, the conclusion deserves weight.
> **[the convergence find, named and used; verdict verbs; assimilation]**
>
> One danger, however, students cannot manage alone: employers. At sixteen, few of us know what a
> fair contract looks like, and fewer still complain when shifts quietly grow. This — not working
> itself — is where students are genuinely exposed, and where the school could help. **[implicit
> idea: the power imbalance beneath Text A's examples; sets up bullet 2]**
>
> I would therefore suggest three measures: teach students what a fair rota and contract look
> like; advise a ceiling of one working day a week in term time; and treat exam weeks as
> protected. A ban would simply drive jobs out of sight. Limits, openly taught, would do what the
> evidence actually asks. **[bullet 2: advice built from the surviving ideas, concrete and
> proposable]**
>
> Yours sincerely,
> Daniel Mensah

**Why it scores:** position stated, then argued — never a tour of the texts · both texts woven
into every paragraph, with implicit ideas surfaced (the dose, not the job; the contract
imbalance) · the convergence carries the verdict · advice a headteacher could actually act on ·
formal register sustained to the sign-off, no contractions anywhere · ~300 words.

# PART THREE — THE SPEECH (12 of 40 — the second-commonest form)

Address the room from the first line and never let go of it. Rhetorical questions, patterns of
three, we/you throughout — and a close built to be remembered, because a speech is judged by what
its audience walks out repeating. If the task carries a purpose verb (*persuade*, *suggest*,
*explain*), it governs the tone.

## MODEL — SPEECH
*Task (real Cambridge style, same two texts): Your school is holding an assembly about balancing
school and work. You have been asked to give a speech to your year group about taking a part-time
job while studying. Write the words of your speech. In your speech you should: • evaluate the
ideas, attitudes and opinions given in both texts • give your view on whether students in your
year should take a part-time job, and how to make it work.*

> Have you ever earned a pound? Not been given one — earned one. **[hook: direct address,
> line one]**
>
> Because that, according to everything I have been reading, is where this argument actually
> starts. The case for a Saturday job sounds like a careers leaflet: employers like it, you learn
> to handle money, you learn to manage time. All true, apparently. But the voice I trust is not
> the leaflet — it is the girl who ices buns at seven on a Saturday morning. She has missed
> birthdays. She has stood at a till in mocks week while her chemistry notes sat at home. And she
> would not give it up for anything — because the money is hers, the confidence is hers, and,
> listen to this, her grades went *up*. Up. Because a job forced her to plan her week for the
> first time in her life. **[evaluation by weighing: report vs experience; spoken register;
> her evidence assimilated into the argument]**
>
> So should we all rush out and grab a rota? Not quite — because the research has a number in it,
> and the number is the whole point. A few hours a week: fine, often better than fine. Past about
> ten: grades slip, sleep shrinks, and teachers start marking homework "visibly written at
> midnight after a six-hour shift". The job is not the danger. The dose is. **[implicit idea made
> memorable; short sentences doing the emphasis]**
>
> And here is the part nobody puts on a poster: some employers count on us not knowing better.
> Rotas that arrive late. Shifts that quietly grow. We are cheap — and at sixteen, almost nobody
> complains. **[the second implicit idea: exploitation; pattern of three]**
>
> So here is my advice, borrowed from the baker and backed by the research. Take the job — on
> your terms. One day, never two. Exam weeks sacred, no exceptions. And judge any boss by one
> test: if they will not respect your limits, leave — because a job should make the rest of your
> life work better, not swallow it. **[bullet 2 as the climax; the close built to be repeated]**

**Why it scores:** the room addressed from line one and never dropped · both texts evaluated and
woven around one argument (never sequenced) · both implicit ideas surfaced and made quotable ·
rhetorical patterning that serves the argument rather than decorating it · the advice lands as
the ending · ~300 words.

# PART FOUR — THE ARTICLE (11 of 40)

You are writing for readers who owe you nothing: the headline and first line buy the second.
Several real tasks GIVE you the title — use it exactly; otherwise write your own. Keep a
journalist's poise: engaged, informed, never gushing — and seal the piece by circling back to
where it began.

## MODEL — ARTICLE
*Task (real Cambridge style, same two texts): Write an article for your school or college
newspaper about students taking part-time jobs. In your article you should: • evaluate the ideas,
attitudes and opinions given in both texts • advise students what they should consider before
taking a part-time job. Begin your article with a suitable headline.*

> **THE TEN-HOUR QUESTION**
> *Should you get a job? Wrong question, says the evidence — ask how much, and for whom.*
> **[headline + standfirst: the article's verdict compressed]**
>
> Fifty years ago nobody would have asked. Paper rounds and Saturday shifts were simply what
> teenagers did. Now, with exam pressure rising, the corridor splits into two camps: those who
> call a weekend job character-building, and those who call it academic sabotage. The odd thing,
> on the evidence, is that both camps are right — about different jobs. **[hook; both texts'
> frame assimilated into one journalist's line]**
>
> The case for working is stronger than it sounds. Employers repeatedly say that students with
> work experience interview better; money you have earned is money you understand. One
> seventeen-year-old who spends her Saturdays behind a bakery counter credits her manager with
> teaching her more about dealing with people "than any lesson I have ever sat in" — and reports
> the result nobody predicts: her grades rose, because the job forced her to plan. **[evaluation:
> the strongest claims selected and developed, not listed]**
>
> But the research draws a line, and it sits at about ten hours a week. Below it, working
> students match or beat their classmates. Above it, grades slip, sleep shrinks and absences
> creep upwards. And there is a quieter problem: young workers are cheap, and some employers lean
> on that — rotas that arrive late, shifts that grow, and sixteen-year-olds who do not know what
> a fair contract looks like and rarely complain. **[the number; the implicit idea beneath
> Text A's examples]**
>
> So the advice writes itself — and, remarkably, the enthusiast and the sceptics agree on it.
> Cap your hours before you start. Protect exam weeks absolutely. And apply the baker's test to
> any employer: if they will not respect your limits, leave. A job, she writes, should make the
> rest of your life work better, "not the thing that swallows it". The difference, as the
> research concludes, is not the job — it is the limits set around it. **[bullet 2 from the
> convergence; the close answers the headline]**

**Why it scores:** headline and standfirst that state the angle · journalist's distance with the
blogger quoted and characterised · both texts woven around the article's own question — never
walked through · implicit ideas surfaced (the dose; the contract imbalance) · advice concrete,
sourced from the texts, and sealed by circling back · ~300 words.

# PART FIVE — THE DRILL THIS DOCUMENT EXISTS FOR

**The form transplant, Paper 2 edition.** Take any Section A pack — ours, or any real paper. Do
the reading work ONCE: the Two-Voices map, the convergence, your verdict. Then write the task's
form — and immediately re-cast the same argument as a second form, twenty minutes, no new
reading.

What must stay identical: the positions, the convergence, the verdict, the evidence you weighed.
What must change completely: who you are talking to, how the piece opens and closes, how much of
yourself you put in, and every register choice in between.

Notice, when you compare your two pieces, how much survived the transplant. That is the real
lesson of this volume: **the form is worth about five lines of specific technique; the
evaluation is worth everything else.** A student who panics about forms is usually a student who
has not yet noticed how little the form actually demands.

⚠️ **On these models:** all three are written from Sophicly's own Practice Paper 1 texts, which we
authored — so every detail above is checkable against `PACK-P2-paper01-work-INSERT.md`. In the
exam, every detail must come from that paper's own two texts. Use these to learn the FORMS, never
as content to reuse. And note what none of the three models ever does: quote at length. A short
attributed phrase, twice or three times a piece, is the ceiling — the Writing table's bottom
levels are built from "words/phrases copied from the original text".
