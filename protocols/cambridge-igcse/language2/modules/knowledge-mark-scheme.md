## 1.2 Section A, Question 1 — the AO statement (mark scheme, page 4)

> **Section A: Directed Writing**
>
> **Question 1**
>
> **This question tests the following writing assessment objectives (25 marks)**
>
> W1 articulate experience and express what is thought, felt and imagined
> W2 organise and structure ideas and opinions for deliberate effect
> W3 use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context
> W4 use register appropriate to context
> W5 make accurate use of spelling, punctuation and grammar
>
> **and reading assessment objectives (15 marks)**
>
> R1 demonstrate understanding of explicit meanings
> R2 demonstrate understanding of implicit meanings and attitudes
> R3 analyse, evaluate and develop facts, ideas and opinions, using appropriate support from the text
> R5 select and use information for specific purposes.

Immediately above it, on the same page:

> **Note:** All Examiners are instructed that alternative correct answers and unexpected approaches in candidates' scripts must be given marks that fairly reflect the relevant knowledge and skills demonstrated. **Nonetheless, the content must be clearly related to and derived from the passage.**

The syllabus (pp. 9–10) gives the parent AOs and the component weighting:

> AO1 Reading — R1…R5 (R4 = `demonstrate understanding of how writers achieve effects and influence readers`; **R4 is not tested in Paper 2 Q1**)
> AO2 Writing — W1…W5
>
> Weighting in components %: **Paper 2 — AO1 Reading 20, AO2 Writing 80.**

## 1.6 Section B — Table A, Composition: Content and structure (out of 16) — VERBATIM

**The descriptive/narrative split: the descriptors ARE split, but only partially.** Every level has a shared **General** block (two bullets, tagged W1 and W2) that applies to both task types, followed by a two-column **Specific – descriptive | Specific – narrative** block. This structure is identical at all six levels. Recorded exactly:

> **Marking criteria for Section B**
>
> **Table A, Composition: Content and structure**

| Level | Marks | General (both types) | Specific – descriptive | Specific – narrative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **6** | **14–16** | • Content is **complex**, engaging and effective. (W1)<br>• Structure is secure, well balanced and **carefully managed for deliberate effect**. (W2) | • **Many** well-defined and developed ideas and images create a **convincing** overall picture with **varieties of focus**. | • The plot is well-defined and **strongly** developed with features of fiction writing such as description, characterisation and **effective climax**, and **convincing** details. |
| **5** | **11–13** | • Content is **developed**, engaging and effective. (W1)<br>• Structure is well managed, with **some** choices made for deliberate effect. (W2) | • **Frequent**, well-chosen images and details give a **mostly** convincing picture. | • The plot is defined and developed with features of fiction writing such as description, characterisation, climax and details. |
| **4** | **8–10** | • Content is **relevant** with some development. (W1)<br>• Structure is competently managed. (W2) | • A selection of **relevant** ideas, images and details, **even where there is a tendency to write in a narrative style**. | • The plot is **relevant** and cohesive, with some features such as characterisation and setting of scene. |
| **3** | **5–7** | • Content is straightforward and briefly developed. (W1)<br>• Structure is mostly organised but may not always be effective. (W2) | • **The task is addressed** with a series of **relevant** but straightforward details, **which may be more typical of a narrative**. | • The plot is straightforward, with limited use of the features of narrative writing. |
| **2** | **3–4** | • Content is simple, and ideas and events may be limited. (W1)<br>• Structure is partially organised but limited in its effect. (W2) | • The recording of some **relevant** events with limited detail. | • The plot is a simple narrative that may consist of events that are only partially linked and/or which are presented with partial clarity. |
| **1** | **1–2** | • Content is **occasionally relevant** or clear. (W1)<br>• Structure is limited and ineffective. (W2) | • The description is unclear and lacks detail. | • The plot and/or narrative lacks coherence. |
| **0** | **0** | No creditable content. | | |

*(Bold added for the discriminating words analysed in §5; the mark scheme itself is unbolded.)*

**Recorded exactly as asked — how the split behaves:**
- The **General** bullets are *never* differentiated by task type. Content quality and structure are judged by the same words for a description and for a story.
- The **Specific** columns are differentiated at **every** level, 1 to 6.
- The descriptive column is the only place where **narrative drift** is mentioned, and it appears at exactly two levels: **L4** (`even where there is a tendency to write in a narrative style`) and **L3** (`which may be more typical of a narrative`). It is **absent from L5 and L6.**
- At **L2** the descriptive column stops describing description at all — `The recording of some relevant events` — i.e. at the bottom of the table a description that has collapsed into events is what Level 2 *is*.

## 1.8 The generic marking principles that govern everything above

These are on pages 2–3 and are frequently the answer to "does X get penalised?".

> **GENERIC MARKING PRINCIPLE 2:** Marks awarded are always whole marks (not half marks, or other fractions).
>
> **GENERIC MARKING PRINCIPLE 3:** Marks must be awarded positively:
> • marks are awarded for correct/valid answers … credit is given for valid answers which go beyond the scope of the syllabus and mark scheme
> • marks are awarded when candidates clearly demonstrate what they know and can do
> • **marks are not deducted for errors**
> • **marks are not deducted for omissions**
> • answers should only be judged on the quality of spelling, punctuation and grammar when these features are specifically assessed by the question…
>
> **GENERIC MARKING PRINCIPLE 4:** Rules must be applied consistently, e.g. **in situations where candidates have not followed instructions** or in the application of generic level descriptors.
>
> **GENERIC MARKING PRINCIPLE 5:** Marks should be awarded using the full range of marks defined in the mark scheme…
>
> **GENERIC MARKING PRINCIPLE 6:** Marks awarded are based **solely** on the requirements as defined in the mark scheme. Marks should not be awarded with grade thresholds or grade descriptors in mind.

And, decisive for the prepared-story question:

> **English & Media subject specific general marking principles**
> Components using level descriptors:
> • We use level descriptors as a guide to broad understanding of the qualities **normally expected of, or typical of**, work in a level.
> • **Level descriptors are a means of general guidance and should not be interpreted as hurdle statements.**
> • … we must always be prepared to **meet candidates on their chosen ground, provided it is relevant ground** (e.g. clearly related to and derived from a relevant passage/text and meeting the mark scheme requirements for the question).

## THE THREE FACTS A MARKER MUST NOT FORGET

1. **15 of Section A's 40 marks are READING marks.** A fluent, well-written answer that does not
   evaluate BOTH texts cannot pass Reading Level 1–2, whatever its prose.
2. **Writing Level 3 is *"Relies on the sequence of the original text."*** A text-by-text
   walkthrough caps Section A writing at 10–13 of 25 however good the sentences are.
3. **Table B of Section B contains NO content, plot, task, title or relevance language.** Those 24
   marks are pure style and accuracy, identical for all four composition questions.
